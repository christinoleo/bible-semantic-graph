The Arabic-language philosophical tradition (c. 9th–12th centuries) that received, translated, and transformed Greek thought — chiefly Aristotle read through late-antique Neoplatonic commentators — into a self-standing system covering logic, metaphysics, natural philosophy, ethics, and political theory. Its arc runs from al-Kindī's early synthesis through al-Fārābī's political Platonism, Ibn Sīnā's (Avicenna's) emanationist metaphysics and essence/existence distinction, the Andalusi line of Ibn Bājja and Ibn Ṭufayl, and the Aristotelian purism of [[ibn-rushd]] (Averroes).

It stands apart from [[classical-kalam]] in method and authority: where kalam argues from revelation outward using dialectical theology, falsafa pursues demonstrative science from first principles and treats prophecy as a philosophically explicable phenomenon. The most famous internal Islamic confrontation between the two is [[al-ghazali]]'s *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*, charging the philosophers with unbelief on eternity of the world, divine knowledge of particulars, and bodily resurrection — answered a century later by [[ibn-rushd]]'s *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*. The Latin reception of Avicenna and Averroes reshaped European [[scholastic]] thought.
