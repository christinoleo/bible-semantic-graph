The earliest systematic school of [[classical-kalam]], founded by Wāṣil ibn ʿAṭāʾ in 8th-century Basra and developed by Abū al-Hudhayl, al-Naẓẓām, al-Jāḥiẓ, and the Jubbāʾīs. Organized around its five principles — divine unity ([[tawhid]]), divine justice (*ʿadl*), the promise and the threat, the intermediate position of the grave sinner, and commanding right and forbidding wrong — it pressed reason as a source of moral knowledge prior to revelation.

Its most controversial theses are the [[uncreated-quran|createdness of the Quran]], libertarian [[free-will-and-predestination-in-islam|human free will]], denial of the beatific vision, and a strongly metaphorical reading of the [[divine-attributes-debate|divine attributes]] to protect divine simplicity. Imposed as state doctrine during the *Miḥna* under the caliph al-Maʾmūn, the school lost institutional ground after the reaction led by [[ahmad-ibn-hanbal]] and was eclipsed in the Sunni world by the rising [[ashariyya]] and [[maturidiyya]], though its categories survived in Twelver Shīʿī and Zaydī theology.
