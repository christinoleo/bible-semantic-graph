The Syriac-speaking Christian tradition centered historically in the Sasanid Persian Empire (Seleucia-Ctesiphon, later Baghdad), institutionally separated from the Roman/Byzantine churches by the late 5th century and confessing a [[nestorianism|two-qnome Christology]] that polemical Western and Byzantine sources have called "Nestorian" — though the tradition itself reveres [[nestorius]] as a martyr-witness and reads its Christology through [[theodore-of-mopsuestia|Theodore of Mopsuestia]] rather than through Nestorius directly. The Synod of Mar Aqaq (486) and the formative work of Mar Babai the Great (d. 628) fixed the position: in the one *parsōpā* (prosōpon) of Christ there are two *kyānê* (natures) and two *qnōmē* (hypostases) — a formulation that maps awkwardly onto the post-[[chalcedonian-christology|Chalcedonian]] Greek vocabulary and looks like Nestorianism from Constantinople but functions internally to preserve the same anti-Eutychian, anti-confusion concern Chalcedon does.

The 1994 *Common Christological Declaration* between Pope John Paul II and Patriarch Mar Dinkha IV recognized the Assyrian Church of the East's Christology as substantively orthodox while leaving the historical condemnations untouched — paralleling the Catholic-Oriental Orthodox rapprochement of the same decade. The Church survived Islamic conquest, Mongol patronage and Timur's massacres, and the 20th-century Assyrian genocide; today its dioceses span Iraq, Iran, India (the Chaldean Syrian Church), Australia, and the diaspora.
