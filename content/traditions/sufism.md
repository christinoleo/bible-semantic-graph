The Islamic mystical tradition, oriented toward direct experiential knowledge (*maʿrifa*) of God rather than the dialectical defense of doctrine pursued by [[classical-kalam]] or the demonstrative science of [[falsafa]]. It emerges from the early ascetic and devotional currents of Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and the love-mysticism of Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya, is systematized in Baghdad by al-Junayd and pushed to ecstatic extremes by al-Ḥallāj, and is brought decisively inside Sunni orthodoxy by [[al-ghazali]]'s *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*.

Its mature speculative form is reached in Ibn ʿArabī's *waḥdat al-wujūd* (the unity of being) and the Persian poetic mysticism of Rumi and Ḥāfiẓ, while its social form is the institutional ṭarīqa (order) — Qādirī, Naqshbandī, Shādhilī, Mevlevi, Chishtī — through which Islam spread across Africa, Anatolia, and South and Southeast Asia. Its characteristic vocabulary of stations (*maqāmāt*), states (*aḥwāl*), annihilation (*fanāʾ*), and subsistence (*baqāʾ*) maps an interior itinerary that complements, and sometimes tensely coexists with, the legal and theological sciences.
