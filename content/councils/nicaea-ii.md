The Second Council of Nicaea (787 CE) was convoked by Empress Irene of Athens and her son Constantine VI to end the first phase of **Byzantine iconoclasm**, the imperial policy (launched by Leo III in 726-730 and entrenched under Constantine V) of destroying religious images and persecuting their defenders. The earlier iconoclast Council of Hieria (754) had condemned the production and veneration of icons as idolatry; Nicaea II was the orthodox counter-council.

About 300 bishops attended, with papal legates representing Pope Hadrian I. The principal theological defender of icons, [[john-of-damascus]] (d. 749), had died decades earlier under safer Muslim rule outside the Byzantine empire; his three *Apologies Against Those Who Decry the Holy Images* furnished the council's main theological apparatus. The Studite monk [[theodore-the-studite]] would later defend the council's teaching during the second iconoclast period (815-843).

The council drew a careful distinction (developed by John of Damascus) between **latria** (λατρεία, the worship due to God alone) and **dulia** (δουλεία, the veneration appropriate to saints, the cross, the gospels, and holy images). The decree (*horos*) holds that "the honor paid to the icon passes to the prototype" — citing [[basil-the-great]] — so that venerating an image of [[christ]] or a saint is not idolatry but honor referred through the image to its archetype. The Christological argument is decisive: because the Word truly became flesh, he can be circumscribed in matter; rejecting the icon implies a docetic refusal of the incarnation.

The Latin reception of Nicaea II was botched. A poor Latin translation reaching the Frankish court read the *latria* due to God as if it were applied to icons. [[charlemagne]]'s theologians (the *Libri Carolini*, drafted by Theodulf of Orléans ca. 790) consequently rejected the council, and the Frankish synods of Frankfurt (794) and Paris (825) condemned both iconoclasm and what they took to be Nicaea II's "image worship." Rome eventually accepted Nicaea II, but Carolingian theology resisted full integration for decades.

The second iconoclast crisis (815-843) ended with the "Triumph of Orthodoxy" under Empress Theodora, celebrated annually on the first Sunday of Lent in Orthodox practice. Nicaea II is accepted as the **seventh and last universally received ecumenical council** by Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions. Protestant Reformers — especially in the Reformed/Calvinist tradition — restaged the iconoclast critique in the 16th century, rejecting Nicaea II's image theology.
