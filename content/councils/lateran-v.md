The Fifth Lateran Council (1512-1517 CE) was convoked by Pope Julius II to counter the conciliarist Council of Pisa (1511) — convened by King Louis XII of France and dissident cardinals against Julius II — and continued under Leo X. About 100 prelates attended (predominantly Italian); the council is the **weakest of the late-medieval ecumenical councils** and infamous for its proximity to the Reformation it failed to forestall.

The council annulled the Pisan acts, reaffirmed papal supremacy over councils (against residual conciliarism), and rolled back the **Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges (1438)** — the French royal legislation that had restricted papal interference in the Gallican church and given the French king extensive control of benefices. The Concordat of Bologna (1516), negotiated by Leo X and Francis I, replaced the Pragmatic with a formal arrangement that gave the French crown control over major French ecclesiastical appointments in exchange for renouncing conciliarism — a deal that arguably postponed serious Reformation in France for a generation.

The most doctrinally substantive act was the bull *Apostolici regiminis* (1513), addressing the question of the **immortality of the individual soul**. Aristotelian philosophy at the universities (especially the Averroist tradition at Padua, exemplified by Pietro Pomponazzi) had revived the doctrine of a single, shared intellect for the human race — a position incompatible with personal immortality and individual judgment. The bull condemned this Averroist position and defined that "the soul is truly and per se the form of the human body" (echoing the [[council-of-vienne]]'s adoption of the Thomistic formula), individually immortal, and that philosophers teaching at universities must publicly refute objections to this doctrine.

Other decrees regulated preaching (canon against unauthorized millenarian preaching, in part reacting to the Savonarolan disturbance at Florence), regulated printing (canon requiring episcopal approval of printed books — an early book-censorship measure), suppressed financial abuses in the curia, and called for a crusade against the Turks. Reform of the clergy was discussed but the resulting decrees were vague, weakly enforced, and broadly ineffective.

The council **closed on 16 March 1517**. On **31 October 1517**, [[martin-luther]] posted (or published; the historicity of the posting is debated) his *Ninety-five Theses* at Wittenberg. Lateran V is thus the immediate institutional backdrop to the Reformation — and Catholic and Protestant historians alike read its failure to substantively reform the curia, the benefice system, and the indulgence economy as the proximate institutional cause of why Luther's protest, unlike a hundred earlier ones, ignited a continent-wide rupture.
