The First Lateran Council (1123 CE) was the **first ecumenical council held in the Latin West** and the first convoked by a pope without imperial co-convocation. Pope Callixtus II summoned it to the Lateran basilica in Rome to ratify the [[concordat-of-worms]] (1122), the settlement that ended the **Investiture Controversy** between papacy and empire.

The Investiture Controversy had erupted in the late 11th century with the Gregorian reform under [[gregory-vii]], who condemned lay investiture (the practice of secular rulers conferring ring and crozier on newly appointed bishops) and excommunicated Emperor Henry IV. The dramatic confrontation at Canossa (1077) became the iconic image of papal-imperial conflict. The struggle continued under Gregory's successors against Henry IV and Henry V.

The Concordat of Worms reached between Callixtus II and Henry V distinguished spiritual from temporal investiture: bishops would be canonically elected by the church and invested with their spiritual office (ring and crozier) by ecclesiastical authority, while the emperor would invest them with the temporal regalia (lands and rights) by the touch of the scepter, after election in his presence. Lateran I ratified this compromise as the conciliar judgment of the universal church.

The council promulgated 22 disciplinary canons: prohibiting simony, enforcing clerical celibacy (already required by previous reform councils), regulating the marriage laws within consanguinity, condemning forged ordinations and lay control of churches, and protecting the rights of crusaders. Many provisions consolidated the reform program of the 11th-century papacy.

Lateran I is **not recognized as ecumenical by the Eastern Orthodox Church**, which restricts ecumenical status to the seven universally received pre-schism councils. The Catholic numbering from Lateran I onward reflects post-schism Western consciousness of the papacy's universal jurisdictional claim. The eight Western ecumenical councils between Lateran I and Trent are the institutional history of the medieval Western church negotiating its own internal disputes (investiture, schisms, crusades, conciliarism, heresies) under the developing claim of papal-monarchical authority.
