The First Council of Nicaea (325 CE) convened at Nicaea in Bithynia under the convocation of [[constantine-i]], the first Christian emperor, who summoned bishops from across the empire to resolve the Arian crisis fracturing the Eastern church. Tradition numbers the attendees at 318 (echoing Abraham's 318 servants in Gen 14:14); the actual figure was likely around 250-300, overwhelmingly Eastern bishops. The Western church was thinly represented; [[silvester-i]] of Rome sent two legates.

The central business was the teaching of [[arius]], a presbyter of Alexandria, who held that the [[son-of-god]] was a creature — the highest creature, brought into being before time, but not eternal and not of the same substance as the Father ("there was when he was not"). [[athanasius]], then a deacon attending with [[alexander-of-alexandria]], became the lifelong champion of the Nicene position against Arian recurrences.

The council promulgated the [[nicene-creed]] (the original 325 form, shorter than the later Nicene-Constantinopolitan version), confessing the Son as "begotten not made, of one substance (ὁμοούσιον / [[homoousios]]) with the Father." The term *homoousios* was controversial — non-biblical, previously associated with Sabellian and gnostic uses — but Constantine's intervention secured its inclusion. The creed concluded with anathemas against the specific Arian propositions.

Beyond Christology, Nicaea promulgated 20 disciplinary canons: setting the date of [[easter]] (independent of the Jewish lunar calendar), regulating the rights of metropolitan sees (Rome, Alexandria, Antioch; canon 6), prohibiting self-castration of clergy (canon 1, aimed at Origenist excesses), forbidding usury, and ordering the Meletian schism in Egypt.

Nicaea is accepted as the **first ecumenical council** by all major Christian traditions — Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Assyrian Church of the East, Anglican, Lutheran, Reformed. It is the foundational dogmatic council and the reference point for all subsequent conciliar Christology. The Arian controversy nonetheless continued for decades after; the homoousion was rejected, modified ("homoiousios"), or evaded by powerful factions until vindicated at [[constantinople-i]] (381).
