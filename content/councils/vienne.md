The Council of Vienne (1311-1312 CE) was convoked by Pope Clement V — the first [[avignon-papacy|Avignon pope]] — at Vienne in the Dauphiné under crushing political pressure from King Philip IV ("the Fair") of France. About 170 bishops attended. The council met in three sessions over six months.

The dominant business was the **suppression of the [[knights-templar]]**. Philip IV had launched a coordinated arrest of all Templars in France on Friday, 13 October 1307, on charges of heresy, sodomy, idolatry (worship of the head called Baphomet), blasphemy, and obscene initiation rites. The charges were extracted under torture; the inquisitorial process targeted the order's vast wealth, which Philip wanted to seize. Clement V — installed at Avignon largely through French influence — resisted initially but capitulated under sustained pressure.

The bull *Vox in excelso* (3 April 1312) **suppressed the order** "by way of provision and apostolic ordinance" — not by formal canonical condemnation, since the council itself was not persuaded the heresy charges were proven. The bull *Ad providam* (2 May 1312) transferred Templar property to the [[knights-hospitaller]] (in most cases; in Iberia to new local orders). Templar leaders including Grand Master Jacques de Molay were tried separately and burned at Paris in 1314, after retracting their confessions.

The council also condemned the **Beghards and Beguines** (lay devotional communities, especially in the Low Countries and Rhineland), whose mystical tendencies (associated with the *Free Spirit* heresy) were judged heterodox in the decree *Ad nostrum*. The decree *Cum de quibusdam* discouraged but did not entirely suppress the broader beguinage movement. The trial of [[marguerite-porete]] (burned 1310) had set the precedent. These condemnations contributed to the suppression of vernacular mystical theology and the constraint of female religious life outside formal canonical orders.

Vienne also condemned the views of the radical Franciscan theologian Peter John Olivi (condemned implicitly, posthumously) on the spiritual interpretation of poverty and on the wound of Christ. The council mandated the establishment of chairs of Hebrew, Greek, Arabic, and Chaldean (Syriac) at the major universities — a missionary-philological provision rarely implemented in practice but symbolically marking medieval interest in Eastern languages.

The council illustrates the **subordination of papal-conciliar judgment to secular monarchical power** that characterized the early Avignon period (1309-1378) and helped fuel the conciliarist reaction at [[constance]].
