The Second Council of Constantinople (553 CE) was convoked by Emperor Justinian I and presided over by Patriarch Eutychius of Constantinople. The council attempted a politically motivated reconciliation with the non-Chalcedonian miaphysites by retroactively condemning three Antiochene-leaning theologians ("the Three Chapters") whose writings were resented in Egypt and Syria as crypto-Nestorian.

The **Three Chapters** were: (1) the person and writings of [[theodore-of-mopsuestia]] (d. 428), [[nestorius]]'s teacher and the great Antiochene exegete; (2) the anti-Cyrillian writings of Theodoret of Cyrus (against the Twelve Anathemas), though Theodoret himself, who had been received at [[chalcedon]] after anathematizing Nestorius, was not condemned; (3) the letter of Ibas of Edessa to Mari the Persian, criticizing Cyrillian Christology. All three had been accepted (with reservations) at Chalcedon, making the condemnation appear to retroactively modify the previous ecumenical council.

The council promulgated 14 anathemas defining a "neo-Chalcedonian" or Cyrillian-Chalcedonian Christology — reading Chalcedon through Cyrillian lenses, using the formula "one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh" (theopaschism). Christ's hypostasis is identical with that of the divine Word; the human nature has no independent hypostasis (the doctrine later called *enhypostasia*, developed by [[leontius-of-byzantium]] and clarified in [[john-of-damascus]]).

The council also condemned **Origenism** — a set of teachings attributed to [[origen]] (preexistence of souls, apokatastasis / universal restoration, subordinationist trinitarianism). The historical question of whether these condemnations target Origen himself or later "Isochrist" Origenist monks of 6th-century Palestine remains contested.

Pope Vigilius initially refused to attend and was effectively coerced into ratifying the council under Justinian's pressure. Western reception was bitter: parts of the African and Italian (Aquileia) church rejected the council for decades, viewing the condemnation of the Three Chapters as betraying Chalcedon. The schism in northern Italy (the Schism of the Three Chapters) lasted until 698. Constantinople II is accepted as the **fifth ecumenical council** by Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and most Protestant traditions, but its standing is more contested than the first four.
