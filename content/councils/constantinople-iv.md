The Fourth Council of Constantinople (869-870 CE) is the last council numbered "ecumenical" by Roman Catholic reckoning that the Eastern Orthodox Church does **not** recognize — and a clear symptom of the divergence that will harden into the [[east-west-schism]] of 1054. The Eastern Orthodox instead recognize a different council held in Constantinople in 879-880 (sometimes called Constantinople IV in Orthodox numbering) which rehabilitated Photius and is treated as quasi-ecumenical.

The council was convoked by Emperor Basil I (the Macedonian) to resolve the **Photian Schism**. [[photius-i]] of Constantinople, an immensely learned lay scholar, had been elevated to the patriarchate in 858 after the deposition of Ignatius, with whom he competed for the see. Pope Nicholas I refused to recognize Photius's elevation. The conflict was inflamed by jurisdictional disputes over the missionary church in Bulgaria, by Photius's encyclical of 867 condemning Latin practices (including the [[filioque]] addition to the creed and Latin enforcement of clerical celibacy), and by a synod he held in 867 deposing Nicholas.

When Basil I murdered his predecessor Michael III in 867, he reversed the religious policy: deposed Photius, restored Ignatius, and convoked the council. Some 102 bishops attended; papal legates from Pope Hadrian II presided. The council formally deposed Photius, anathematized him, and ratified the seven previous ecumenical councils. Canon 3 forbade lay violence against bishops; canon 22 restricted lay influence over episcopal elections.

After Ignatius died in 877, Photius was reinstated as patriarch with Roman approval. The 879-880 Council of Constantinople — attended by papal legates of John VIII — reversed the 869 condemnations, restored Photius, and forbade additions to the Nicene-Constantinopolitan creed (read by the East as condemning the Western [[filioque]]). It is this council, not 869, that Eastern Orthodox tradition treats as the legitimate Constantinople IV.

The asymmetric reception — Rome counting 869 as ecumenical, Constantinople counting 879 — is a textbook example of how the schism developed not in one event but as a progressive divergence of conciliar memory. Modern Catholic theologians (notably Yves Congar) have argued that the Catholic numbering of post-Nicaea II councils as "ecumenical" reflects Western convention rather than genuine universality, and several have proposed reducing the count to seven for ecumenical-dialogue purposes.
