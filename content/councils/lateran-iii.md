The Third Lateran Council (1179 CE) was convoked by Pope Alexander III after his protracted struggle with Emperor Frederick I Barbarossa and the imperial antipopes (Victor IV, Paschal III, Callixtus III) ended with the Peace of Venice (1177). About 300 bishops attended, with delegations from across Western Christendom including [[ireland]] and [[scotland]].

The council's most procedurally consequential canon was **canon 1, requiring a two-thirds majority of cardinals for papal election**. This rule, intended to prevent disputed elections of the kind that had produced the Frederick-backed antipopes, has governed papal conclaves ever since (with periodic refinements; John Paul II in 1996 admitted a fallback to simple majority after extended deadlock; Benedict XVI reversed this in 2007). The procedural canon may be Lateran III's most durable legacy.

Doctrinally, canon 27 formally condemned the **[[cathars]]** (also called Albigensians) of Languedoc and the **[[waldensians]]** (followers of Peter Waldo of Lyon, who advocated lay preaching and voluntary poverty). The Cathars held a dualistic theology with Manichaean roots; the Waldensians were closer to a proto-reformist movement initially compatible with orthodoxy but driven into heresy by hierarchical hostility to lay preaching. The canon authorized armed action against them — a step toward the [[albigensian-crusade]] (1209-1229) launched under [[innocent-iii]] after [[lateran-iv]].

Other canons regulated the qualifications and conduct of clergy: prohibiting plurality of benefices, requiring cathedral schools (canon 18, a milestone in medieval education), forbidding ordination of those under canonical age, banning clerical participation in tournaments, restricting Jews from holding Christian servants, and limiting the maintenance of bishops and their retinues during episcopal visitations.

The council reflects the transitional moment in the 12th century when the post-Gregorian papacy began to engage popular heretical movements not only juridically but militarily, while simultaneously building the institutional and educational apparatus (the cathedral schools that would soon become universities) that produced scholastic theology.
