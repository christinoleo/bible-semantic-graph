The First Council of Constantinople (381 CE) was convoked by Emperor Theodosius I as a synod of Eastern bishops (around 150 in attendance) to settle the long aftermath of the Arian controversy and the new disputes over the [[holy-spirit]] and over [[christ]]'s humanity. The Western church and Rome were not invited; the council was originally regional but received ecumenical status retroactively, particularly after its endorsement at [[chalcedon]] (451).

The council vindicated the work of the [[cappadocian-fathers]] — [[basil-the-great]] (who had died in 379, two years before the council), [[gregory-of-nazianzus]], and [[gregory-of-nyssa]]. Gregory of Nazianzus briefly presided after the death of the first president, Meletius of Antioch, but resigned amid disputes over his transfer from the see of Sasima. He delivered the *Five Theological Orations* on the Trinity that defined the Cappadocian theological vocabulary (*one ousia, three hypostaseis*).

Doctrinally, the council condemned three heresies. **Macedonianism** (the Pneumatomachi or "Spirit-fighters") denied the divinity of the [[holy-spirit]], treating the Spirit as a creature. **Apollinarianism** taught that [[christ]] had no human rational soul (the Logos replaced the human νοῦς), denying the fullness of Christ's humanity. **Eunomianism** (radical neo-Arianism) held the Son to be unlike (ἀνόμοιος) the Father.

The council expanded the Nicene Creed into what is now called the [[nicene-constantinopolitan-creed]] — the form recited in liturgy across most of historic Christendom. The expansion added a substantial pneumatological clause: the Holy Spirit is "the Lord, the giver of life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified." The Eastern formula deliberately omitted "and the Son" (the [[filioque]]); its later Western interpolation became the principal dogmatic flashpoint of the [[east-west-schism]].

Canon 3 of Constantinople I assigned the see of Constantinople honorary primacy second after Rome ("because Constantinople is New Rome"), inaugurating the long jurisdictional friction with the Roman see over the basis of papal primacy (succession vs imperial prestige). The council is accepted as the **second ecumenical council** by Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Anglican, and most Protestant traditions.
