The Second Vatican Council (1962-1965 CE) was convoked by [[john-xxiii]] (Angelo Roncalli) in a surprise announcement on 25 January 1959, less than three months into his pontificate. It met in four autumn sessions (1962-1965) under John XXIII (first session) and [[paul-vi]] (Giovanni Battista Montini, after John's death in June 1963). Roughly 2,500 bishops attended — the largest council in Christian history, with substantial representation from the global South for the first time, including bishops from newly decolonized Africa and Asia.

The council was deliberately **pastoral rather than dogmatic**. John XXIII famously rejected the prepared schemas (drafted by curial offices in continuity with [[vatican-i]] and Counter-Reformation idioms) as inadequate, asking the council for *aggiornamento* — bringing the church up to date — through *ressourcement* — return to the deepest sources (scripture, patristics, liturgy). No new dogmas were defined; no heresies were anathematized. Sixteen documents were promulgated: four constitutions, nine decrees, three declarations.

**Lumen Gentium** (Dogmatic Constitution on the Church, 1964) — the ecclesiological centerpiece. The church is described primarily as the "people of God" before the hierarchical structures; **collegiality** of bishops with the pope was affirmed (the pope governs *with* the college of bishops, not as a solitary monarch — a balancing of [[vatican-i]]'s papal-jurisdiction emphasis); the universal call to holiness for all the baptized was central; the relation of the Catholic Church to other Christian communities was reframed as "subsists in" (the Catholic Church *subsistit in* the church of Christ — leaving theological space for genuine ecclesial reality in other Christian bodies).

**Dei Verbum** (Dogmatic Constitution on Divine Revelation, 1965) — reframed scripture and tradition not as two parallel sources (the older "partim ... partim" reading of [[trent]]) but as one stream of divine revelation transmitted through interrelated forms; mandated historical-critical biblical scholarship within faith; reasserted scriptural primacy in theology.

**Sacrosanctum Concilium** (Constitution on the Sacred Liturgy, 1963) — mandated the **liturgical reform**: vernacular languages permitted in liturgy (rapidly extended in practice to the whole Mass), restored congregational participation, simplification of rites, expanded scriptural readings. The post-conciliar Missal of Paul VI (1969-1970) replaced the Tridentine Missal of [[trent]] (Pius V, 1570) as the ordinary form. This produced the council's most contested and visible practical change.

**Gaudium et Spes** (Pastoral Constitution on the Church in the Modern World, 1965) — engaged modernity not as enemy but as interlocutor, addressing marriage and family, culture, economics, politics, and war (including a careful but unambiguous condemnation of total war and indiscriminate destruction of cities — drafted in the shadow of nuclear weapons).

**Dignitatis Humanae** (Declaration on Religious Freedom, 1965) — affirmed the **right of every human person to religious liberty**, grounded in the dignity of the human person, against the state's coercion. This declaration explicitly broke with the 19th-century papal "Catholic confessional state" doctrine (Gregory XVI's *Mirari vos*, Pius IX's *Quanta cura* and *Syllabus of Errors*) and is the central exhibit in the post-conciliar continuity-versus-rupture debate.

**Nostra Aetate** (Declaration on the Relation of the Church to Non-Christian Religions, 1965) — rejected the charge of collective Jewish guilt for the death of Christ ("what happened in his passion cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today"), repudiated antisemitism, and affirmed the abiding spiritual heritage shared with the Jewish people. It also addressed positive engagement with Islam, Hinduism, and Buddhism. This declaration revolutionized Catholic-Jewish relations.

**Theological architects.** The drafters and *periti* included [[karl-rahner]] (SJ), [[yves-congar]] (OP), Henri de Lubac (SJ), Edward Schillebeeckx (OP), Hans Küng, and the young [[joseph-ratzinger]] (then a peritus for Cardinal Frings of Cologne; later Pope Benedict XVI). These represented the *nouvelle théologie* and ressourcement movements suppressed under Pius XII (whose *Humani generis* of 1950 had targeted them) and rehabilitated under John XXIII.

**Reception.** Vatican II's reception is contested. The mainstream Catholic interpretation (articulated by [[paul-vi]], John Paul II, and Benedict XVI in his 2005 "hermeneutic of reform in continuity") reads the council as developing, not contradicting, the prior tradition. Progressive interpretations (e.g., the *Concilium* journal current) read it as inaugurating an ongoing reformation. Traditionalist critique (the [[society-of-saint-pius-x]] under Marcel Lefebvre, schismatic since 1988) rejects key documents (especially *Dignitatis Humanae* and *Nostra Aetate*) as incompatible with prior magisterium. The "spirit of Vatican II" — appeals to the council to authorize changes not in its texts — has been a persistent point of dispute.
