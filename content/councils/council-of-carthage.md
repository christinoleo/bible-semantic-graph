The Council of Carthage (397 CE; with further confirming sessions in 419) was a regional North African synod that ratified the canon list promulgated at [[council-of-hippo]] (393). The 397 session, sometimes called the Third Council of Carthage, was presided over by Aurelius of Carthage, primate of the African church. [[augustine-of-hippo]], by then bishop of Hippo Regius, attended and was a major theological influence on its deliberations.

Canon 24 of Carthage 397 (numbering varies; canon 36 in some recensions) enumerates the canonical books explicitly: the full Old Testament with deuterocanonicals — Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, 1-2 Maccabees, Baruch — and the 27 books of the New Testament including [[hebrews]] (whose Pauline authorship was still disputed in the East), the [[catholic-epistles]], and [[revelation]] (still disputed in parts of the East). This is the **first surviving Christian conciliar list containing exactly the 27 NT books in their later canonical form**.

Carthage 397 ordered the canon to be confirmed by the "transmarine church" — i.e., referred to Rome and the wider Christian world — but did not subordinate African judgment to Roman confirmation. The further session of 419 (Carthage VI) repeated and strengthened the list.

The council is regional, not ecumenical. Its canon decisions become normative for the Latin West through their reception by popes (Innocent I, Damasus indirectly via the Decretum Gelasianum), through [[jerome]]'s [[vulgate]] (which despite Jerome's own reservations included the deuterocanonicals), and ultimately through dogmatic reaffirmation at [[trent]] (1546).

Protestant Reformers in the 16th century rejected Carthage's deuterocanonical inclusions, restoring the narrower Hebrew canon. Eastern Orthodox traditions accept a broader OT than Carthage (adding 1 Esdras, Psalm 151, 3 Maccabees, Prayer of Manasseh). Carthage thus marks the canonical center of the Western tradition, not a universally agreed list.
