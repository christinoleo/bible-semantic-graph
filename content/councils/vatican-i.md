The First Vatican Council (1869-1870 CE) was convoked by Pope Pius IX and met in St Peter's Basilica beginning 8 December 1869. About 700 of the 1050 entitled bishops attended — the most representative council to date in terms of global Catholic episcopate (with significant participation from the Americas, missionary territories, and Eastern Catholic churches). It was the **first ecumenical council in over 300 years**, since [[trent]] closed in 1563, reflecting the long Counter-Reformation Catholic consolidation and the post-Trent reluctance to convene further councils.

The council had a vast intended agenda (drawn up in extensive preparatory commissions) covering doctrine, ecclesiology, ecclesiastical law, religious life, missions, and church-state relations. It completed only two dogmatic constitutions before being interrupted.

**Dei Filius (24 April 1870)** — the dogmatic constitution on the Catholic faith — defined the relationship of faith and reason against the rationalism, materialism, and pantheism Pius IX had condemned in his *Syllabus of Errors* (1864). It affirmed: the natural knowability of God through reason (against fideism); the necessity of supernatural revelation for saving knowledge of the trinitarian mystery (against rationalism); the harmony of faith and reason (neither contradicts the other, since both come from God); and the legitimate autonomy of the sciences within their proper domain.

**Pastor Aeternus (18 July 1870)** — the dogmatic constitution on the Church of Christ — defined two contested doctrines about the papacy:

1. **Papal universal jurisdiction**: the Roman Pontiff has not merely a primacy of honor but "ordinary, immediate, and universal" jurisdiction over the whole church and every individual member, "in matters of faith and morals as well as in those that pertain to the discipline and government of the church."

2. **Papal infallibility**: when the Roman Pontiff speaks *ex cathedra* — i.e., "when, exercising the office of pastor and teacher of all Christians, he defines, by his supreme apostolic authority, a doctrine concerning faith or morals to be held by the whole church" — he is endowed with that infallibility with which Christ willed the church to be equipped in defining doctrine concerning faith or morals, and such definitions are **irreformable of themselves and not from the consent of the church** (*ex sese non autem ex consensu ecclesiae irreformabiles*).

The infallibility definition was hotly debated. A minority (about 140 bishops) opposed the definition or its timing, including [[john-henry-newman]] (privately critical of the maximalist faction although ultimately accepting the dogma), August Strossmayer of Đakovo, and Felix Dupanloup of Orléans. After the definitive vote (533 to 2; most opponents had left Rome rather than vote against), the [[old-catholic-church]] broke off in protest, principally in Germany, Switzerland, Austria, and the Netherlands.

The council was **interrupted indefinitely** by the Franco-Prussian War and the Italian seizure of Rome (20 September 1870), which ended the Papal States. The remaining agenda items were never completed; Vatican I was not formally closed until [[vatican-ii]] was convoked in 1959. The historical question whether **Pope Honorius I** (condemned at [[constantinople-iii]]) constitutes a counterexample to infallibility was extensively debated; the standard Catholic answer is that Honorius's letter was not *ex cathedra* by the Vatican I definition's strict criteria.
