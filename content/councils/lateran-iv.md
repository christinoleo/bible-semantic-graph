The Fourth Lateran Council (1215 CE) was the great medieval council, convoked by Pope [[innocent-iii]] — the most powerful pope of the Middle Ages — and attended by some 1200 prelates including 71 metropolitans, 412 bishops, 900 abbots and priors, plus delegations from Eastern patriarchates and most Christian kingdoms. It is the most important Western council between [[chalcedon]] and [[trent]].

The council's 70 constitutions reshaped Latin Christianity at every level. **Constitution 1** (*Firmiter credimus*) is the central dogmatic statement: a comprehensive creed affirming Trinity, incarnation, and explicitly defining the eucharist in terms of **[[transubstantiation]]** — "his body and blood are truly contained in the sacrament of the altar under the species of bread and wine, the bread being transubstantiated (*transsubstantiatis*) into the body and the wine into the blood by divine power." This is the first formal conciliar use of the term, codifying the Aristotelian-substance-and-accidents framework that scholastic theology was developing.

**Constitution 21** (*Omnis utriusque sexus*) mandated **annual sacramental confession** to one's own parish priest and **annual eucharistic communion** at Easter for every baptized adult of either sex on pain of being barred from entering a church alive and from Christian burial. This single canon reshaped pastoral practice in Latin Christianity, producing the medieval confessional manuals (penitentials), the apparatus of cases of conscience, and ultimately the late-medieval pastoral system that the Reformation both critiqued and built upon.

Constitution 2 condemned the trinitarian speculation of [[joachim-of-fiore]] (his attack on [[peter-lombard]]). Constitution 3 (*Excommunicamus*) systematized procedures against heretics, requiring secular authorities to assist in their suppression — the canonical foundation of the later inquisitorial apparatus. Constitutions 67-70 imposed restrictions on Jews (distinctive dress, exclusion from public office, regulation of usury) that became infamously normative in late-medieval Christian-Jewish relations.

The council also called the **Fifth Crusade** (which launched in 1217 against Egypt) and ratified [[innocent-iii]]'s approval of new religious orders, including the [[franciscans]] (Innocent had approved [[francis-of-assisi]]'s rule orally in 1209) and the [[dominicans]] (formally approved 1216 by Honorius III). Constitution 13 forbade the founding of new religious orders without papal approval — a measure that would shape mendicant history for centuries.

Lateran IV is rightly called the **medieval council par excellence**: its dogmatic definition of transubstantiation, its disciplinary mandates on confession and communion, its anti-heresy juridical machinery, and its broad ecclesial vision under papal headship together constitute the institutional fabric of late-medieval Catholicism that the 16th-century reformers would inherit and contest.
