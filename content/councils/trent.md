The Council of Trent (1545-1563 CE) was the **Counter-Reformation council** — the Catholic doctrinal and disciplinary response to the Protestant Reformation. Convoked by Pope Paul III at the imperial city of Trent (Trento) in the Tyrol after decades of demands by Emperor Charles V, the council met in three discontinuous periods (1545-1547, 1551-1552, 1562-1563) under five popes. Attendance was modest — typically 30-80 bishops, predominantly Italian, with significant Spanish, Portuguese, and (in the third period) French participation. The **Jesuit theologians [[diego-lainez]] and [[alfonso-salmerón]]** (founding companions of [[ignatius-of-loyola]]) were major theological influences as papal theologians.

**Canon and scripture (Session IV, 1546).** Trent defined the biblical canon dogmatically: 46 OT books including the **deuterocanonicals** (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, 1-2 Maccabees, Baruch — rejected by [[martin-luther]] and the Reformed) and 27 NT books. The [[vulgate]] was declared the **authentic** edition for public reading and teaching (not, contrary to common misreading, the only legitimate text — but the official Latin standard). Scripture and **unwritten tradition** were affirmed as twin sources of revelation, against Luther's *sola scriptura*.

**Justification (Session VI, 1547).** The Decree on Justification (33 chapters and 33 canons) is Trent's most theologically elaborate document. It rejected both Pelagianism (justification by works alone, without grace) and the Lutheran *sola fide* (justification by faith alone, imputed, with works as the fruit but not the substance). The Tridentine doctrine: justification is by grace through faith **and inwardly transforming**; baptism is the instrumental cause; faith, hope, and charity are infused; merit is real (though grace-enabled); justification can be lost through mortal sin and restored through the sacrament of penance. The decree carefully avoided endorsing any of the rival Catholic schools (Thomist vs Scotist vs Augustinian).

**Sacraments (Sessions VII-XXIV, 1547-1563).** Trent defined and defended the seven sacraments against Protestant reductions. Eucharist: real presence and **[[transubstantiation]]** reaffirmed (Session XIII); sacrifice of the Mass affirmed against Zwingli and Reformed memorialism (Session XXII); communion under one species (bread only for laity) affirmed (Session XXI). Penance: full Tridentine articulation of contrition, confession (auricular, to a priest, specifying mortal sins by kind and number), absolution, and satisfaction (Session XIV). Holy orders: sacramental priesthood, hierarchy of bishop-priest-deacon (Session XXIII). Matrimony: sacramentality and the **Tametsi decree** requiring marriage before a priest and witnesses for validity (Session XXIV).

**Disciplinary reform.** Trent's enduring institutional legacy lies in the disciplinary decrees: residence of bishops in their dioceses (against absenteeism and pluralism), establishment of **diocesan seminaries** for clerical formation (the foundational structure of post-Tridentine clergy training), regulation of episcopal visitations, condemnation of various financial abuses surrounding indulgences and benefices, mandatory diocesan synods, regulation of religious orders.

Trent **condemned** [[martin-luther]], [[john-calvin]], [[ulrich-zwingli]], and the broader Protestant theological positions explicitly, while studiously avoiding many particular Catholic-internal disputes. Its decrees defined Catholic identity until [[vatican-ii]] and structurally remain in effect; the post-Tridentine Catholic Church (1564-1962) is sometimes called the "Tridentine Church." The Roman Catechism (1566), the revised Vulgate (1592 Clementine), the post-Tridentine Missal (1570, Pius V's *Quo primum*), and the revised Breviary are all direct products of Trent's mandates.
