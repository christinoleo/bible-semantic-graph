The Second Lateran Council (1139 CE) was convoked by Pope Innocent II at the Lateran basilica to close the long papal schism of 1130-1138 between Innocent and the antipope Anacletus II (Pietro Pierleoni). Anacletus had died in 1138; his successor Victor IV had submitted. About 500 bishops attended, drawn from across Latin Christendom.

The council annulled all the ordinations performed by Anacletus and his supporters, deposed those bishops who had taken his side, and excommunicated Roger II of Sicily, who had backed the Pierleoni faction. The radical preacher **[[arnold-of-brescia]]** — a disciple of [[abelard]] who attacked the temporal power and wealth of the church — was condemned and forced into exile (he was later executed in 1155 under Hadrian IV).

Lateran II promulgated 30 disciplinary canons consolidating the Gregorian reform program. Canon 6 declared the marriages of clergy in major orders **invalid** (not merely illicit), tightening the celibacy discipline from the prohibitive canons of [[lateran-i]] and earlier reform councils. This is widely regarded as the council that turned clerical celibacy into a hard juridical bar in the Latin church, although the practice had been promoted at the regional level for centuries. The Eastern churches retained the older discipline of married clergy in major orders below the episcopate.

Other canons condemned usury, prohibited tournaments (later largely ignored), banned crossbows and archers against Christians (the "humanitarian" canon, also widely ignored), and reaffirmed protections for crusaders and clergy. The council also condemned the followers of Peter of Bruys and Henry of Lausanne — anti-sacramental popular preachers — anticipating the harder line against later 12th- and 13th-century heretical movements (Cathars, Waldensians) at [[lateran-iii]] and [[lateran-iv]].

Lateran II's enduring historical significance lies less in dramatic doctrinal definition than in the consolidation of clerical celibacy as a structural feature of Latin ecclesiology — a fact that would become a permanent point of disagreement with Eastern churches and a target of Reformation polemics.
