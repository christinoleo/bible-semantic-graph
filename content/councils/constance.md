The Council of Constance (1414-1418 CE) was the most consequential medieval council after [[lateran-iv]] and the one that most directly shaped the late-medieval crisis from which the Reformation emerged. It was convoked by Emperor-elect Sigismund and (under intense pressure) by John XXIII, one of three claimants to the papacy. About 600 prelates attended over four years; the gathering was the largest international assembly of the late Middle Ages.

The council had three explicit aims: *causa unionis* (ending the [[western-schism]]), *causa reformationis* (reform of the church in head and members), and *causa fidei* (suppression of heresy, especially Wycliffism and Hussitism). It achieved the first dramatically, the second negligibly, and the third with notorious bad faith.

**Ending the Western Schism.** Since 1378 there had been two — and from 1409, three — competing papal lines (Roman: Gregory XII; Avignon: Benedict XIII; Pisan: John XXIII). Constance deposed John XXIII (April 1415), accepted Gregory XII's voluntary abdication (July 1415), and deposed Benedict XIII (1417). It then elected Martin V (November 1417), restoring a single papacy. This is Constance's lasting historical achievement.

**Conciliarism.** The decree *Haec Sancta* / *Sacrosancta* (April 1415) declared that the council, "legitimately assembled in the Holy Spirit, constituting a general council and representing the catholic church militant, has its authority immediately from Christ; and that everyone, of whatever state or dignity, even if of papal dignity, is bound to obey it in those matters which pertain to the faith, the extirpation of the said schism, and the general reformation of the said church of God in head and members." The decree *Frequens* (1417) mandated regular convocation of general councils. Together these are the high-water mark of [[conciliarism]] — the doctrine that the council, not the pope, holds supreme authority in the church. Their dogmatic status has been disputed ever since: defenders argue they bound the universal church; opponents (especially after [[basel-ferrara-florence]]) read them as emergency measures during schism, lacking ordinary force.

**Hussite trials.** [[jan-hus]] — Bohemian reformer and rector of Charles University, influenced by [[john-wycliffe]] — came to Constance under imperial safe-conduct (a *salvus conductus* from Sigismund) to defend his teachings. He was nonetheless arrested, tried, and **burned at the stake on 6 July 1415**. His follower Jerome of Prague was burned in 1416. Wycliffe was condemned posthumously and his bones exhumed and burned in 1428. The Hussite reaction in Bohemia became a religious-military revolt (the Hussite Wars, 1419-1434), an unmistakable precursor of the Reformation.

The council failed at substantive reform. Martin V's *Concordats* with the various nations after Constance addressed only the most surface-level abuses, leaving the structures of late-medieval ecclesiastical finance, benefices, and pluralism essentially intact. The next century of reform pressure — culminating in 1517 — built directly on the dissatisfaction with Constance's reform failure.
