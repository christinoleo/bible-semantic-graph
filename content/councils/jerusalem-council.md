The Council of Jerusalem (~50 CE) is the apostolic assembly narrated in [[acts]] 15, convened to settle whether gentile converts to the Jesus movement must accept circumcision and the Mosaic law. The crisis was provoked by Judaizers from Judea teaching circumcision as necessary for salvation in [[antioch]]; [[paul]] and [[barnabas]] travelled to [[jerusalem]] to dispute the claim with the apostles and elders.

[[peter]] addresses the assembly first, citing the [[cornelius]] episode (Acts 10) as divine validation of uncircumcised gentile inclusion. [[paul]] and [[barnabas]] then recount signs and wonders among the gentiles. [[james-brother-of-jesus]], presiding as leader of the Jerusalem church, renders the decision, citing [[amos]] 9:11-12 (LXX) on the restoration of David's tent including gentiles.

The decree (Acts 15:23-29; the so-called Apostolic Decree) imposes four prohibitions on gentile believers — abstaining from food sacrificed to idols, blood, things strangled, and porneia — while exempting them from circumcision and the broader Torah. The minimal restrictions roughly align with the [[noachide-laws]] tradition for resident aliens.

The council is **not** counted among the 21 ecumenical councils of Catholic reckoning but is universally treated as the apostolic precedent for conciliar resolution of doctrinal disputes. Every later council from [[nicaea-i]] onward looks back to Acts 15 as the warrant for collective episcopal authority binding the whole church.

Critical scholarship debates the relationship between the Acts 15 narrative and [[paul]]'s independent account in [[galatians]] 2:1-10. The reconciliations range from harmonization (same event, different rhetorical purpose) to two-event theories (Galatians 2 = Acts 11 famine visit) to outright contradiction (Luke smoothing a sharper Pauline conflict). The decree's four prohibitions are absent from Galatians, fueling the debate.
