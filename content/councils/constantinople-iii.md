The Third Council of Constantinople (680-681 CE) was convoked by Emperor Constantine IV in the Trullan Hall (Domed Hall) of the imperial palace, attended by some 165 bishops over 18 sessions. It resolved the **Monothelite controversy** — a 7th-century imperial-theological attempt to reunite the [[chalcedon]]ian and non-Chalcedonian (miaphysite) churches by affirming that [[christ]] has two natures but one will (*thelēma*) and one operation (*energeia*).

The compromise had been pushed by Patriarch Sergius of Constantinople and Emperor Heraclius (with the *Ekthesis* of 638) and was supported, fatefully, by Pope Honorius I, whose 634 letter to Sergius spoke of "one will" in Christ. The principal opponent was [[maximus-the-confessor]] — the greatest theologian of the 7th-century East — who argued (with Pope Martin I at the Lateran Synod of 649) that the two natures must each have their proper natural will and operation, lest Christ's humanity be a mere instrument lacking real human agency. Both Maximus and Martin were arrested, tried, and mutilated; Maximus died in exile (662). They were posthumously vindicated at Constantinople III.

The council defined **dyothelitism**: Christ possesses "two natural wills and two natural operations, without division, without change, without partition, without confusion ... the human will follows, not as resisting or reluctant, but rather as subject to his divine and almighty will." Christ's Gethsemane prayer ("not my will, but yours be done", Lk 22:42) was read as evidencing exactly this submission of a real human will to the divine.

Strikingly, the council pronounced anathema on **Pope Honorius I** by name as a heretic ("we cast out of the holy Church of God and anathematize Honorius the former pope of old Rome"). Pope Leo II ratified the council and confirmed the condemnation of his predecessor, though softening it: Honorius was condemned for "negligence" in failing to defend the apostolic faith rather than for active heresy. This condemnation became a major embarrassing exhibit during the 19th-century debate over [[vatican-i]]'s definition of papal infallibility.

The council is accepted as the **sixth ecumenical council** by Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Anglican, and most Protestant traditions. The disciplinary canons attributed to the Quinisext or "Trullan" council (692) are a separate assembly (also held in the Trullan hall) often confused with Constantinople III; Quinisext is accepted by the Orthodox but not by Rome.
