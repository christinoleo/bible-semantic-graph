The Council of Ephesus (431 CE) was convoked by Emperor Theodosius II at the basilica of St Mary in [[ephesus]] to resolve the controversy between [[cyril-of-alexandria]] and [[nestorius]], Patriarch of Constantinople. The council met in chaotic conditions: Cyril opened the proceedings on 22 June before the arrival of John of Antioch's Syrian delegation (sympathetic to Nestorius) and the Roman legates, condemning Nestorius in absentia.

[[nestorius]], a former student of the Antiochene school under [[theodore-of-mopsuestia]], had objected to the popular Marian title [[theotokos]] ("God-bearer"), preferring *Christotokos* on the grounds that Mary bore the human nature, not the divine Word. Critics — preeminently [[cyril-of-alexandria]] — read this as splitting [[christ]] into two persons (subjects), a "moral" union rather than a substantial one. Cyril's *Twelve Anathemas* (sent in his third letter to Nestorius) formed the polemical core of the Cyrillian case.

The council affirmed [[mary-mother-of-jesus]] as [[theotokos]] and confessed that the divine Word and the man Jesus are one person (μία ὑπόστασις) — the foundation of the doctrine of the [[hypostatic-union]]. It deposed Nestorius and ratified Cyril's second letter to Nestorius (the Cyrillian "Twelve Anathemas" received more equivocal status, never being formally promulgated as a creedal text by the council).

When John of Antioch arrived several days later with his delegation, they held a counter-synod that deposed Cyril. The schism between Alexandria and Antioch was patched up in 433 via the *Formula of Reunion* — a compromise document containing the two-natures language that would become central at [[chalcedon]].

The council also condemned [[pelagianism]] in passing (canon 1 attached the anti-Pelagian condemnation of Caelestius). It is accepted as the **third ecumenical council** by Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and most Protestant traditions. The [[church-of-the-east]] (Assyrian) rejected the council and retained Nestorian-style Christology; the [[oriental-orthodox]] later split with Chalcedon over what they saw as a betrayal of Cyrillian Christology.
