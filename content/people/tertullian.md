Tertullian (~155-220) — Carthaginian; first major Latin Christian writer, coining a theological vocabulary that shaped the Western tradition: trinitas, persona, substantia, sacramentum, vetus / novum testamentum. Possibly trained in law (the legalist cast of his rhetoric is conspicuous); converted as an adult; the Eusebian report that he was a presbyter is disputed.

Prolific corpus across apologetic, polemical, ascetical, and disciplinary genres. The Apologeticum (197) is a forensic defense of Christians against Roman charges. Against Marcion (five books) is the most extensive ancient refutation of [[marcion]], preserving much of the Marcionite gospel and apostolikon. Against Praxeas develops a sophisticated theology of the Trinity — "three persons, one substance" (tres personae, una substantia) — and coins economic Trinitarianism, though his subordinationist tendencies remain.

Around 207 he aligns with the New Prophecy (Montanism), a rigorist Asian movement emphasizing ongoing prophecy, strict ascetic discipline, and refusal of second marriage and post-baptismal forgiveness for major sins. The break is doctrinal rather than against the great church — Tertullian retains Nicene-style Christology — but cost him later canonization. His De pudicitia attacks a Roman bishop (Callistus? unclear) for laxity over reconciling adulterers.

"Credo quia absurdum" is a later paraphrase of De carne Christi 5 ("credibile est, quia ineptum est"), not a verbatim quote — paradoxical embrace of the scandal of the incarnation, not anti-rationalism. Influence on [[cyprian-of-carthage]] (who called him "the master"), [[jerome]], [[augustine-of-hippo]] enormous despite the Montanist taint. Decisively shaped Latin theological style: terse, juridical, polemical, witty.
