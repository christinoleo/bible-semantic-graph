Ignatius of Loyola (1491-1556) — Basque founder of the Society of Jesus (Jesuits); the most consequential figure of the Catholic Counter-Reformation alongside the Council of [[trent]]. Born Íñigo López de Loyola, youngest of thirteen children of minor Basque nobility; courtier and soldier through his twenties. The hinge event was a French cannonball at the siege of Pamplona (1521) that shattered his right leg, ending his military career and prompting a long convalescence at Loyola during which the only available reading material — a life of Christ by Ludolph of Saxony and the Golden Legend — redirected him decisively.

Stages of conversion: pilgrim asceticism at Manresa (1522-1523, where the core of the Spiritual Exercises was drafted); pilgrimage to Jerusalem (1523-1524); compensatory return to formal education in his early thirties at Barcelona, Alcalá, Salamanca, and finally Paris (1528-1535), where he gathered the first companions — Francis Xavier, Peter Faber, Diego Laínez, Alfonso Salmerón, Nicolás Bobadilla, Simão Rodrigues. The Montmartre vows (1534) committed them to poverty, chastity, and either Jerusalem or papal disposal. War on the Mediterranean closed the Jerusalem option; offered themselves to Paul III; the Society of Jesus formally approved by Regimini militantis Ecclesiae (1540), with the famous fourth vow of special obedience to the pope regarding mission.

Spiritual Exercises — not a treatise but a directed retreat manual, structured into four "weeks" walking through sin, the life of Christ, the passion, and the resurrection, with detailed examens, meditations, and discernment of spirits. The "principle and foundation," the meditation on the Two Standards, the Contemplation to Attain Love — central pivots. The methodology of discernment between consolation and desolation, of finding God in all things, set a distinctive Ignatian spirituality detachable from the institutional order and influential across Catholic devotion and beyond.

Constitutions of the Society of Jesus — drafted over the 1540s and 1550s in close collaboration with Juan Alfonso de Polanco. Structural innovations against the medieval monastic template: no choral office; mobility; centralized monarchical governance under a General with permanent tenure (the Black Pope, by later anti-Jesuit lore); ministries in education (the Ratio Studiorum codified 1599), foreign missions ([[francis-xavier]] forward ref to Asia, the Paraguay reductions), pastoral work, and intellectual combat with Protestantism. The Spiritual Diary (1544-1545) survives as direct evidence of Ignatius's own mystical experience — Trinitarian visions, tears, consolations — strikingly intense for a man whose public reputation was the cool organizer. Canonized 1622 with Francis Xavier and Teresa of Ávila.
