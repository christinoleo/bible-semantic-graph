John Calvin (1509-1564) — second-generation reformer; legal and humanist scholar whose Institutes and Geneva ministry shaped the Reformed branch of Protestantism distinctively from the Lutheran. Born at Noyon in Picardy; trained in arts at Paris, then law at Orléans and Bourges; humanist commentary on Seneca's De clementia (1532) his first publication, before his conversion to evangelical theology — date and circumstances famously private; the only autobiographical hint is the brief retrospective in the preface to the Psalms commentary (1557) referring to a "sudden conversion" (subita conversione).

Fled France after the Affair of the Placards (1534) made Protestant residence dangerous; first edition of the Institutes published at Basel 1536 in six chapters — a compact apologia for French evangelicals presented as if addressed to Francis I. Detained in Geneva while traveling through 1536 by Guillaume Farel ("if you do not stay here, God will curse you"); first Geneva ministry 1536-1538 ended with expulsion in disputes over discipline; pastoral years in Strasbourg under [[martin-bucer]] (forward ref) 1538-1541 formed his ecclesiology; recalled to Geneva 1541 and remained until death.

Institutes of the Christian Religion grew through five editions across a quarter century, the final 1559 Latin version restructured into four books following the Apostles' Creed pattern (God the Creator, God the Redeemer, the means of receiving grace, the church and civil order). Distinctively Calvinist emphases: divine sovereignty as the encompassing frame of soteriology; double predestination treated as a humbling pastoral implication of grace rather than its starting point (Book 3); covenant theology integrating Old and New Testaments more tightly than Luther's law/gospel dichotomy; presbyterian-conciliar ecclesiology of pastor, doctor, elder, deacon under the [[ecclesiastical-ordinances]] (1541). Real presence in eucharist via spiritual ascent of the believer to heaven by the Spirit — distinct from Lutheran ubiquity and Zwinglian memorialism alike, the latter often loosely conflated with the Reformed view.

Geneva regime: theocratic in popular memory, but in juridical fact Calvin was a pastor without civic citizenship until 1559 and without vote on the city councils that enforced moral discipline. The condemnation and burning of [[michael-servetus]] (1553) for antitrinitarian heresy — Calvin pressed the trial but advocated beheading rather than burning, and the unanimous endorsement of Reformed and Lutheran cities followed — has rightly remained a moral indictment of the era's church-state alliance. Commentaries on almost every biblical book — terse, philological, christologically restrained on prophetic typology — set a methodological standard. Reformed orthodoxy and Westminster theology, Puritanism, Dutch Reformed and Hungarian Reformed traditions, much of nineteenth-century evangelical thought, all trace ancestry through him.
