Naomi is the matriarch around whom the [[book-of-ruth]] turns. Driven from Bethlehem to [[moab]] by famine with her husband Elimelech and sons Mahlon and Chilion, she returns a decade later widowed and childless, accompanied only by her Moabite daughter-in-law [[ruth]]. She arrives demanding to be renamed Mara ("bitter") because "the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me" (Ruth 1:20) — one of the Hebrew Bible's sharpest indictments of providence from inside a canonical text.

She is the strategic intelligence of the narrative. Ruth gleans where Naomi directs, approaches [[boaz]] at the threshing floor on Naomi's coaching, and the legal mechanism that secures the family's redemption is set in motion by Naomi's knowledge of kinship law. By Ruth 4, the women of Bethlehem hand the newborn Obed to her: "a son has been born to Naomi" (Ruth 4:17), folding her loss into Israel's royal line.

Critical readings often place Naomi at the structural center: the book opens and closes with her, and Ruth's spectacular loyalty serves Naomi's restoration. Feminist scholarship has explored the women-centered economy of the book — the framing chorus is "the women" of Bethlehem, and the levirate-adjacent redemption operates around a household with no surviving men of the immediate line.

Theologically, Naomi's bitter prayer is rare permission within the canon to name divine action as personal injury — a counterpoint to the more pious idiom of the [[psalms]] of complaint.
