Adam is the first human in the Hebrew Bible. The name `ʾādām` puns on `ʾădāmâ` ("ground") — etiological, not a proper name in Gen 1-3 (where the article `hāʾādām`, "the human," dominates).

Two creation accounts coexist: Gen 1:1-2:4a (P) — humanity created `bĕṣelem ʾĕlōhîm` ("in the image of God"), male and female together, on day six; Gen 2:4b-3:24 (J) — `hāʾādām` formed from dust, [[eve]] taken from his side, Eden, the talking serpent, the expulsion. See [[documentary-hypothesis]]. The two accounts have different theological centers (P: ordered cosmos and human dignity; J: agrarian theodicy and the origin of death-toil-childbirth).

Father of [[cain]], [[abel]], and [[seth]]; through the Sethite line to [[noah]] and onward (Gen 5). Lives 930 years in the antediluvian chronology — likely literary/symbolic, with ANE parallels in the Sumerian King List's pre-flood reigns.

Reception is enormous. Second Temple literature (1 Enoch, 4 Ezra, 2 Baruch) inflates Adam into a cosmic figure whose sin propagates universal death — the seedbed of [[paul]]'s "Adam-Christ" typology in [[romans]] 5 and [[1-corinthians]] 15, where Adam prefigures [[jesus]] as the head of a fallen humanity overturned by a "second Adam." Augustine then welds this into a doctrine of inherited original sin; Eastern Orthodoxy reads the same texts more weakly as inherited mortality rather than inherited guilt. Modern paleoanthropology and the Genesis genealogies are routinely harmonized via figurative readings (federal headship, archetypal Adam) or rejected as incompatible.
