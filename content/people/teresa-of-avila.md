Teresa of Ávila (1515-1582) — Carmelite nun; mystic, foundress, and (with [[catherine-of-siena]]) the first woman declared a Doctor of the Church (1970, by Paul VI). Born Teresa de Cepeda y Ahumada to a wealthy Ávila family of partly converso (Jewish-convert) ancestry on her paternal grandfather's side — a fact concealed at the time but historiographically significant for understanding the limpieza de sangre pressures shaping Spanish religious life. Entered the Carmelite convent of the Incarnation in Ávila at twenty (1535) against her father's wishes; spent twenty years in what she later described as religious mediocrity, interrupted by a serious illness in 1538 that nearly killed her and a partial paralysis lasting three years.

The "second conversion" around 1554, before an image of the wounded Christ, opened the period of intense mystical experience documented in the Book of Her Life (Libro de la vida, 1565) at the request of her confessors — among them the Dominican Domingo Báñez and the Jesuits Diego de Cetina and Baltasar Álvarez. The book was scrutinized by the Spanish Inquisition; Teresa wrote under careful self-censorship in a deliberately self-deprecating, conversational, anti-rhetorical register. Reform of the Carmelite Order from 1562: founding of the Discalced Carmelite convent of San José in Ávila — strict observance of poverty, enclosure, the white mantle and rough sandals (discalced) — followed by sixteen more foundations over the next two decades chronicled in the Book of Foundations.

The Interior Castle (Castillo interior, also Las moradas — "The Mansions," 1577) — her most systematic mystical work, structured as a journey through seven concentric mansions of the soul-castle toward the central mansion where spiritual marriage with God occurs. Earlier mansions concern prayer and self-knowledge; the middle the dark night and mystical betrothal; the seventh the union characterized by simultaneous interior stability and external activity (Martha and Mary as the same person). The Way of Perfection (1566), written for her nuns, is a more pastoral handbook on prayer focused on the Pater Noster.

Collaborated closely with [[john-of-the-cross]] from 1568, when the young Carmelite became confessor and reform partner — the male branch of the Discalced reform extends from this partnership. Persecuted within her order: imprisoned briefly, repeatedly investigated, surveilled by the Inquisition (her Life was sequestered for fifteen years), the Discalced reform briefly suppressed in 1577 before separation as a province (1581). Died in 1582 at Alba de Tormes en route home from a foundation; her grave became a major pilgrimage site, her relics dispersed contentiously. Canonized 1622. Mystical theology has remained continuously productive across the Catholic, Protestant, and Orthodox interpretive traditions.
