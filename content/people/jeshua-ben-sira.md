Jeshua ben Eleazar ben Sira, Jerusalemite sage of the early second century BCE, is the named author of [[sirach]] (Ecclesiasticus) — uniquely among Hebrew Bible / deuterocanonical sapiential literature, the work is signed by its composer (Sir 50:27, "instruction in understanding and knowledge I have written in this book, Jeshua ben Eleazar ben Sira of Jerusalem"). The book is composed in Hebrew c. 196-175 BCE under the high priesthood of Simon II, son of [[onias-iii|Onias II]], who is praised in the climactic encomium of Sir 50 — providing the chief internal dating evidence.

His grandson translates the work into Greek in Egypt under Euergetes II (Ptolemy VIII Physcon) — the prologue dates the grandson's arrival in Egypt to the thirty-eighth year of Euergetes, conventionally 132 BCE, with the translation completed thereafter (c. 117 BCE). The prologue is one of the earliest extant statements of Jewish-Hellenistic translation theory and the earliest external witness to a tripartite Hebrew canon ("the Law and the Prophets and the others that followed them"). The original Hebrew, lost in late antiquity, was partially recovered from the [[cairo-genizah]] (1896 onwards) and from [[qumran]] and Masada — c. two-thirds of the book now extant in Hebrew.

Ben Sira's sapiential program fuses [[proverbs]]-style wisdom poetry, [[deuteronomy]]-style covenantal piety, and a deliberate identification of Wisdom (Sir 24) with [[torah]] — a step beyond [[proverbs]] 8 and foundational for later [[philo|Philonic]] and Christian Logos-theology. The "Praise of the Fathers" (Sir 44-50) is the first sustained Jewish reception-history of the biblical figures, ending with the high priest Simon — anchoring the Second-Temple cult as the telos of biblical history. Excluded from the [[tanakh]] (though widely cited in [[talmud|rabbinic literature]]) and from [[protestant]] canon; included in [[catholic]] and [[orthodox-eastern]] canons.
