Ham, second son of [[noah]] (per Gen 9:24, "his youngest son" — ordering varies). Eponym of Hamitic peoples: in the Table of Nations (Gen 10:6-20) his line includes Cush (Nubia/Ethiopia), Mizraim (Egypt), Put (Libya), and [[canaan]] — i.e., the great African and Levantine powers surrounding Israel.

The vineyard episode (Gen 9:20-27): Noah drunk and naked in his tent; Ham "saw the nakedness of his father" and told his brothers outside. [[shem]] and [[japheth]] cover Noah without looking. Noah wakes, knows "what his youngest son had done to him" (Gen 9:24), and curses — not Ham, but Ham's son Canaan: "a slave of slaves shall he be to his brothers" (Gen 9:25). The displacement of the curse onto Canaan, plus the euphemistic "saw the nakedness of" (elsewhere a Levitical idiom for incest, Lev 18 / 20), drives an enormous interpretive literature: was it voyeurism, mockery, paternal rape, maternal incest, castration? The text refuses to specify.

Reception is catastrophic. The "Curse of Ham" was weaponized from late antiquity through the 19th century — first in some rabbinic strands (b. Sanhedrin 108b speculates about Ham's punishment in skin-darkening), then massively in Christian apologetics for chattel slavery and apartheid, conflating "Ham" with "Black African" and reading Noah's curse as divine sanction for the enslavement of Black peoples. This reading is exegetically indefensible: the curse falls on [[canaan]] (a Levantine group, biblically subjugated by Israel under [[joshua]]), not on Ham, and there is no racial content in the text. Modern critical and confessional scholarship across traditions repudiates it. The catastrophic afterlife of the passage is itself a primary case-study in misuse of scripture.
