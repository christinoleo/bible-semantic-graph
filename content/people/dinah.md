Dinah, daughter of [[jacob]] and [[leah]], named in passing (Gen 30:21) and then driver of Gen 34. The only named daughter in the Jacob cycle; her appearance and disappearance frame one of the canon's most disturbing chapters.

Gen 34: "Dinah went out to see the daughters of the land." Shechem son of Hamor, prince of the city of Shechem, "saw her, took her, and lay with her, and humbled her" — `wayyiqqaḥ ʾōtāh wayyiškab ʾōtāh wayĕʿannehā`. The verbs are coercive; older translations soften to "defiled," but modern philology reads rape or at minimum abduction. Shechem then "loved" her and asked Hamor to negotiate marriage.

The narrative gives Dinah no speech. Negotiations occur between Hamor, Shechem, Jacob, and her brothers. The brothers agree to intermarriage on condition that every male of the city be circumcised; on the third day, when the men were in pain, [[simeon]] and [[levi]] entered the city, killed every male, "took Dinah out of Shechem's house," and the other brothers plundered. Jacob's rebuke is pragmatic-political; the brothers' last line, "should he treat our sister like a whore?", closes the chapter.

Gen 46:15 lists her — without husband or child — among those descending to Egypt. She never speaks. Her later life is canonically absent.

Reception is contested and intense. Pre-modern tradition mostly read Shechem's act as straightforward rape requiring blood vengeance, with the brothers as flawed-but-righteous avengers. Modern philological scholarship (Lyn Bechtel, Mary Anna Bader, et al.) has reopened nearly every question — consent, the legal status of "humbled," the brothers' motives (honor vengeance, anti-intermarriage polemic, economic seizure), the ethics of the deception, the silence of the female voice. Anita Diamant's 1997 novel *The Red Tent* refracted the chapter into popular feminist retelling. The Dinah narrative remains a primary case-study for biblical hermeneutics on gender, violence, and silence.
