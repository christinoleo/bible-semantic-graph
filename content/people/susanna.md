Susanna ("lily"), pious wife of Joakim in [[babylon]] under exile, is the protagonist of [[susanna]] — the deuterocanonical narrative attached as Daniel 13 in the [[catholic]] [[vulgate]] order, prefixed to Daniel in the Old Greek and Theodotion versions. Two elders appointed as judges conceive lust for her as she bathes in her garden, ambush her, and when she refuses them, falsely accuse her of adultery with a young man (Sus 19-27). On their testimony she is condemned to death.

[[daniel]], "a young lad" at this point in the narrative (Sus 45), interrupts the sentencing in spirit-prompted intervention. He has the elders separated and cross-examined on a single detail: under which tree did the adultery occur? One says a mastic (σχῖνος), the other a holm-oak (πρῖνος) — Greek puns linking the tree names to "to cleave" (σχίσει) and "to saw" (πρίσει), the punishments the elders will suffer. Their contradiction exposes the perjury; under [[deuteronomy]] 19:18-19 they are executed in her place.

The narrative is a forensic-procedure exemplar — the earliest extant Jewish text dramatizing the cross-examination of witnesses — and was cited by Christian and Jewish jurists into the early modern period as a foundation for rules of separated testimony. Sextus Julius Africanus famously rejected its canonicity to Origen on philological grounds (the Greek tree-puns presuppose a Greek original, not a Hebrew Vorlage); Origen defended it. Excluded from the [[tanakh]] and [[protestant]] canons; canonical in [[catholic]] and [[orthodox-eastern]] traditions. Reception history is dense: Rembrandt, Tintoretto, Artemisia Gentileschi (1610), and the figure becomes shorthand for unjust accusation and vindicated chastity.
