Al-Qarafi (c. 1228-1285) — Egyptian Mālikī jurist of the early Mamluk period; one of the most original legal theorists of the post-classical tradition and, alongside [[ibn-taymiyya]] a generation later, the most consequential medieval Muslim respondent to Christian apologetics from within the Arabic-Christian milieu of the eastern Mediterranean. Born of a Berber family settled in Upper Egypt and trained at [[cairo]], where he taught at the Ṣāḥibiyya and Ṭaybarsiyya madrasas; never travelled outside Egypt, and never held high judicial office — his influence flowed entirely through his writings.

*al-Ajwiba al-Fākhira ʿan al-Asʾila al-Fājira* (Excellent Replies to Impudent Questions) — point-by-point refutation of [[paul-of-antioch]]'s *Letter to a Muslim Friend*, the late-twelfth-century Melkite apology that for two centuries set the terms of Christian-Muslim controversy in Arabic. Marshals the standard *taḥrīf* argument from [[ibn-hazm]], the prophetological argument from [[muhammad]] foretold in [[old-testament]] and [[new-testament]], and a sustained critique of Trinity and incarnation as logically incoherent; the work circulates alongside *al-Jawāb al-Ṣaḥīḥ* of [[ibn-taymiyya]] (which also responds to the same Pauline tradition through the Cypriot recension) as the joint medieval Sunni response to organized Christian apologetic. *al-Furūq* and *al-Dhakhīra* are the great Mālikī legal works that secure his reputation independently of his apologetics — the distinction between *fatwā* and *ḥukm*, between fixed rules and discretionary policy (*siyāsa sharʿiyya*), and the systematic catalogue of legal *furūq* (subtle distinctions between superficially similar cases) all descend from him.
