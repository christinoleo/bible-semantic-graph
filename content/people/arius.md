Arius (~256-336) — Alexandrian presbyter whose teaching on the relation of the Son to the Father precipitated the longest and most consequential doctrinal controversy of the fourth century. Probably Libyan by birth; trained as presbyter at Alexandria, possibly under Lucian of Antioch (with whom many later Arian sympathizers — Eusebius of Nicomedia foremost — also studied). Held the parish church of Baucalis in Alexandria.

Core thesis: the Son is a creature, brought into existence by the Father's will out of nothing as the first and highest of all creatures, before time but not eternal — "there was when he was not" (ἦν ποτε ὅτε οὐκ ἦν). The Son is divine in a derivative, subordinate sense, not consubstantial with the Father (homoousios) and not of the same essence. Motivated by a strict monotheism: only the Father is unbegotten (agennētos), and being unbegotten is the defining mark of divinity; the Son, since begotten, cannot share that essence. Conflict with bishop Alexander of Alexandria (~318), excommunication by an Egyptian synod (~321), expansion of the controversy across the Greek East.

The Thalia (Banquet) — Arius's chief work, an extended doctrinal poem in popular Sotadean meter — survives only in hostile excerpts in [[athanasius]] (De synodis 15, Orationes contra Arianos 1.5-6). Polemic and parody color the extant fragments, complicating reconstruction. Some recent scholarship (Rowan Williams) reads Arius as a serious scriptural conservative engaged with the unintended implications of pre-Nicene subordinationism rather than as the cartoon heretic of orthodox memory.

Condemned at [[nicaea-i]] (325), which produced the homoousios creed and the formula "true God from true God, begotten not made." Exiled briefly, rehabilitated under Constantine's pro-Arian later policy, summoned to Constantinople in 336 for re-communion — died abruptly the day before (Athanasius reports lurid scatological details, doubtless polemical embellishment). Theology lived on through Eusebius of Nicomedia and successors — Homoian, Anomoean, Homoiousian — and dominated court Christianity for half a century until [[constantinople-i]] (381) closed the controversy in the East. Gothic Christianity remained Arian for centuries; Vandal and Visigothic kingdoms transmitted the tradition into the early medieval West.
