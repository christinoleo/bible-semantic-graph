Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906-1945) — German Lutheran pastor and theologian; executed by the Nazi regime at Flossenbürg on 9 April 1945, two weeks before American liberation. Born in Breslau to a prominent academic family — father Karl Bonhoeffer a leading psychiatrist, mother Paula daughter of a court chaplain. Six siblings, several of whom were also drawn into anti-Nazi resistance. Brilliant trajectory: doctorate from Berlin at twenty-one on the church as Christus als Gemeinde existierend (Christ existing as community), Sanctorum Communio (1927); habilitation Act and Being (1930) on theological epistemology, engaging both Karl Heim and dialectical theology.

Year as visiting student at Union Theological Seminary, New York (1930-1931) — encountered American liberal Protestantism (which he found theologically thin), met the African-American Christianity of Harlem (which marked him decisively on race and ecclesiology through Frank Fisher and Adam Clayton Powell Sr.), met Jean Lasserre (French pacifist Reformed pastor) who shifted him on the Sermon on the Mount and nonviolence. Returned to Berlin radicalized. Active from 1933 in the Confessing Church (Bekennende Kirche) against the Deutsche Christen, alongside [[karl-barth]], Martin Niemöller, and others; co-drafted the Bethel Confession; became director of the illegal Confessing Church seminary at Finkenwalde from 1935 until its Gestapo closure in 1937.

Cost of Discipleship (Nachfolge, 1937) — exposition of the Sermon on the Mount as the call to costly grace against the "cheap grace" of Protestant cultural Christianity that "preaches forgiveness without requiring repentance, baptism without church discipline, communion without confession." Life Together (Gemeinsames Leben, 1939) — small classic on Christian community drawing on the Finkenwalde experience.

Second American visit (June 1939) at Union Theological Seminary — refused asylum and returned to Germany before the war broke out: "I cannot make this choice in security." Joined the Abwehr (military intelligence) on the strength of brother-in-law Hans von Dohnányi, becoming part of the inner conspiracy against Hitler that culminated in the failed 20 July 1944 bomb plot. Arrested April 1943 on connection with currency irregularities used to smuggle Jews to safety; held at Tegel military prison while writing the Ethics fragments and the letters that became Widerstand und Ergebung (Letters and Papers from Prison, posth. 1951). After the 20 July failure exposed the wider conspiracy, transferred to Gestapo prison, then to concentration camps; hanged at dawn at Flossenbürg.

The prison letters — especially the April-August 1944 sequence to Eberhard Bethge — opened the questions of "religionless Christianity," "world come of age," "God as the one who suffers powerlessly with us in the world," that have shaped postwar Protestant theology from Robinson's Honest to God (1963) through liberation theology to post-secular and pacifist readings. The tension between the Nachfolge ethic of discipleship and the Ethics fragments' acceptance of involvement in tyrannicide remains the productive scandal of Bonhoeffer reception.
