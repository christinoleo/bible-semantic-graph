Pelagius (~360-420) — British or Irish monk, active in Rome from the 380s as a celebrated ascetic teacher among the senatorial Christian aristocracy. The name attaches to a contested theological tendency — Pelagianism — more than to a doctrinaire system; whether Pelagius himself held the views condemned in his name in their starkest form is genuinely debated. His chief surviving works are exegetical (Commentary on the Thirteen Pauline Epistles) and ethical-formative (Letter to Demetrias).

The central claims credited to Pelagius and his sharper disciple Caelestius: human nature is created good and remains essentially intact after the fall; [[adam]] sinned only for himself, not for the whole human race (no inherited original sin or guilt); newborn infants are in the state Adam was created in; death is natural to humanity, not a penalty for sin; free will retains the genuine capacity to choose the good without prior interior grace, though grace assists by external means (law, teaching, Christ's example, scripture). Justification comes through actual moral obedience made possible by uncorrupted free will.

The trigger was Rome's fall to Alaric (410) and the flight of Pelagius and Caelestius to Carthage and then the east. [[augustine-of-hippo]], who knew Pelagius slightly and respected him personally, mobilized once Caelestius's blunter formulations surfaced. Caelestius condemned at Carthage (411) over original sin; Pelagius examined by an eastern synod at Diospolis (415) — acquitted after careful affirmations more orthodox than his Latin opponents thought sincere; African councils of Carthage and Milevis (416) condemned him; Pope Innocent I confirmed; his successor Zosimus initially reversed, then under African pressure confirmed condemnation (Tractoria, 418).

[[ephesus]] (431) reaffirmed the condemnation of Pelagianism in passing. Pelagius disappears from records after his expulsion from Palestine around 418. The condemned form is genuine to his disciples (Caelestius, Julian of Eclanum) and detectable but more ambiguous in his own writings — modern study notes that Augustine's anti-Pelagian theology, in its late predestinarian sharpness, was itself controversial in the Greek East and within Latin Christianity (see the "Semi-Pelagian" controversy resolved at Orange in 529). The recurring problem of where to draw the line between defending grace and denying human responsibility, which the controversy crystallized, has never since been comfortably settled.
