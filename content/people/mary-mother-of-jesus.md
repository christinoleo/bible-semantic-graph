Mary of Nazareth — mother of [[jesus]], wife of [[joseph-of-nazareth]]. Annunciation by Gabriel ([[gospel-of-luke]] 1:26-38) and the Magnificat ([[gospel-of-luke]] 1:46-55, structurally echoing Hannah's song in [[1-samuel]] 2) frame her as covenant-fulfilling handmaid. Infancy narratives in [[gospel-of-matthew]] 1-2 and [[gospel-of-luke]] 1-2 diverge in setting (Bethlehem visit vs. Bethlehem residence), supporting cast (magi vs. shepherds), and aftermath (flight to Egypt vs. presentation in temple).

Synoptic appearances during the ministry are sparse and ambivalent: [[gospel-of-mark]] 3:31-35 has Jesus relativizing kinship in favor of disciples; [[gospel-of-luke]] 11:27-28 redirects a blessing on her womb to obedience. Johannine portrait elevates her: Cana wedding ([[gospel-of-john]] 2) and the cross scene ([[gospel-of-john]] 19:25-27) where Jesus entrusts her to the beloved disciple ([[john-apostle]] in tradition). Present in the Jerusalem upper room post-ascension ([[acts]] 1:14).

Marian dogmas develop across centuries: Theotokos (Ephesus 431) against Nestorius; perpetual virginity (Lateran 649); immaculate conception (Pius IX, 1854); bodily assumption (Pius XII, 1950). Catholic and Orthodox traditions affirm perpetual virginity, reading Jesus' "brothers" ([[james-brother-of-jesus]], [[jude-brother-of-jesus]]) as cousins (Jerome) or stepbrothers from a prior marriage of [[joseph-of-nazareth]] (Epiphanius). Protestant and most critical scholarship read them as full siblings, consistent with [[gospel-of-mark]] 6:3.

Reception history saturates Christian art, liturgy, and popular devotion — rosary, Marian apparitions (Guadalupe, Lourdes, Fátima), title Queen of Heaven. Feminist theology reads the Magnificat as a manifesto of reversal; Islamic tradition venerates Maryam as the only woman named in the Quran with a sura bearing her name.
