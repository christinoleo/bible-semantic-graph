Miriam, sister of [[moses]] and [[aaron]], Levite. First named at Ex 15:20 — already older than both brothers (the watching girl of Ex 2:4-8, who arranges Moses' nursing by his own mother, is unnamed but traditionally identified with her).

Called `nĕbîʾâ` ("prophetess," Ex 15:20) — the first woman in the canon given the title (before [[deborah]]). Leads the women in song and timbrel-dance after the crossing of the Sea: "Sing to YHWH, for he has triumphed gloriously; horse and rider he has thrown into the sea" (Ex 15:21). The "Song of Miriam" is widely held by source-critics to be one of the oldest fragments of Hebrew poetry preserved — possibly the original Sea-victory hymn from which the longer "Song of Moses" (Ex 15:1-18) is expanded. Mic 6:4 places her with Moses and Aaron as one of the three sent before Israel out of Egypt.

Num 12. Challenges Moses with Aaron over his Cushite wife: "Has YHWH spoken only through Moses? Has he not spoken also through us?" — a claim of equivalent prophetic authority. YHWH summons all three to the tent of meeting and rebukes them, distinguishing Moses (face-to-face) from ordinary prophets (dreams and visions). Miriam alone is struck with `ṣāraʿat` (often "leprosy," a skin condition rendering her ritually impure), white as snow. Aaron pleads; Moses intercedes ("`ʾēl nāʾ rĕpāʾ nāʾ lāh`"); she is shut outside the camp seven days, then restored. The asymmetry of punishment (Aaron unscathed) is a long-standing exegetical sore — readings include P-bias protecting the priesthood, gendered double standard, and a more technical priestly logic (a priest cannot be made impure).

Dies at Kadesh (Num 20:1); the people then have no water — and traditional reading (developed in midrash) attaches a wandering well, the "well of Miriam," to her presence. Buried in Kadesh.

Reception. Rabbinic tradition reclaims her significantly: the well, the merit of the Hebrew midwives, the model of female prophetic leadership. Modern feminist exegesis treats Miriam as a major recovered figure — possibly a leader of equal status with Moses in some earlier strand of the tradition, edited down by later redactors who centralized authority in the male Moses/Aaron pair.
