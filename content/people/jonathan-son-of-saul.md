Jonathan is the eldest son of [[saul]] and crown prince of Israel's first dynasty, prevented from the throne by his father's rejection. His early career is a sequence of audacious military feats — most famously the assault on the Philistine garrison at Michmash with only his armor-bearer (1 Sam 14), a near-defeat of his father's army by Saul's rash oath forbidding food, and his rescue from execution only by popular outcry.

The covenant friendship with [[david]] (1 Sam 18-20) is one of the canon's most charged relational portraits. Jonathan strips off his robe, armor, sword, bow, and belt and gives them to David — the symbolic transfer of princely identity. He repeatedly mediates between Saul and David, takes lethal personal risk to warn David of his father's intent, and ratifies a covenant that David honors decades later in his treatment of [[mephibosheth]] (2 Sam 9). Modern readings span romantic-erotic interpretations to readings centered on political covenant and royal succession — debate that the text itself does not settle.

He dies with two brothers and his father on Mt. Gilboa in the Philistine rout (1 Sam 31). David's lament — "your love to me was wonderful, passing the love of women" (2 Sam 1:26) — frames his memory across the rest of [[2-samuel]] and is one of the early poetic survivals in the Davidic corpus, plausibly old enough to be near-contemporary.
