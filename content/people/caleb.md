Caleb, son of Jephunneh, of the tribe of [[judah]] (Num 13:6). The text consistently calls him "the Kenizzite" (Num 32:12, Josh 14:6, 14) — meaning his clan is integrated into Judah from a non-Israelite source (Kenaz, a Horite/Edomite line, Gen 36:11). A reminder that "Israel" in the conquest period is more porous than the genealogical schema suggests.

The faithful spy. With [[joshua]] alone, among the twelve sent to scout [[canaan]] (Num 13), brings back a confident report: "let us go up at once and possess it, for we are well able to overcome it" (Num 13:30). When the people side with the other ten and rebel, Caleb and Joshua tear their clothes; the congregation tries to stone them. YHWH condemns the wilderness generation to die before entering the land — exempting Caleb (`rûaḥ ʾaḥeret ʿimmô`, "a different spirit was with him") and Joshua (Num 14:24, 30, 38).

Forty-five years later — at eighty-five, still claiming the strength of his fortieth year (Josh 14:10-11) — Caleb claims [[hebron]] as his inheritance, the land Moses promised him. Drives out the Anakim (the legendary giants whose presence had terrified the other ten spies) and takes the city. Hebron, the burial place of the patriarchs, becomes his.

The Achsah episode (Josh 15:16-19, Judg 1:12-15). Caleb offers his daughter Achsah to whoever takes Debir; his nephew Othniel takes it and her. She presses her father for a wedding gift — springs of water in addition to the dry land — and gets them. A small but vivid female-agency vignette in the conquest material. Othniel becomes the first of the judges ([[judges]] 3:9-11), making Caleb's line foundational to the period.

Reception. Caleb functions as the wholehearted minority — "wholly followed YHWH" is the recurring formula (Num 14:24, 32:12, Deut 1:36, Josh 14:8-9, 14). In rabbinic tradition he becomes a paradigm of faithful dissent against majority pressure; in Christian devotional literature, a model of late-life claiming of divine promises.
