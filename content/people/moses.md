Moses (`Mōšeh`) is the central human figure of the Torah's narrative spine. Levite (descendant of [[levi]] via Kohath, Ex 6:16-20), brother of [[aaron]] and [[miriam]], husband of [[zipporah]] and son-in-law of [[jethro]]. Name etymologized in Ex 2:10 from `māšâ` ("to draw out" — Pharaoh's daughter "drew him from the water"); historically more likely Egyptian `ms` ("son / born of," as in Thutmose, Ramses) — a bilingual joke the narrative leaves hanging.

Born in [[egypt]] under Pharaoh's edict to kill Hebrew male infants; hidden in a basket on the Nile, adopted into the royal household. Kills an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, flees to [[midian]], shepherds [[jethro]]'s flock for forty years. The burning-bush theophany (Ex 3) — divine self-naming as `ʾehyeh ʾăšer ʾehyeh` ("I AM THAT I AM") and the revelation of the Tetragrammaton to [[moses]] — inaugurates his prophetic mission. Returns to Egypt with Aaron as his mouth; confronts Pharaoh through ten plagues; leads the Exodus.

Sinai. Receives the covenant, the Decalogue, and the bulk of the Torah's legal material across Ex 19-Num 10. Intercedes for Israel after the golden calf, breaks and renews the tablets. Leads forty years of wilderness wandering (Numbers). Struck down at Meribah for striking the rock instead of speaking to it (Num 20), barred from [[canaan]]. Delivers Deuteronomy from the plains of Moab — a covenantal farewell sermon — and dies on Mt. Nebo, viewing the land he will not enter (Deut 34). Buried by YHWH; "no one knows his grave to this day."

Tradition makes him the author of the Pentateuch (whence "the Law of Moses"). Source-critical scholarship dissolves that authorship — Wellhausen's [[documentary-hypothesis]] (J, E, D, P) reads the Torah as a redactional composite from c. 9th-5th c. BCE, with the historical Moses (if any) deeply obscured. Conservative scholarship maintains substantial Mosaic authorship; mediating positions accept later editing of Mosaic-core material.

Theological footprint is the largest in the OT. The "Mosaic" covenant is the constitutive event of Israel as a people. Prophetic and apocalyptic traditions invoke "a prophet like Moses" (Deut 18:15-18) as messianic template; NT writers — especially [[gospel-of-matthew]] (five discourses, mountain delivery, infancy-and-flight parallels) and Hebrews (11:23-29; superseding-yet-honoring framework) — explicitly cast [[jesus]] as a new Moses. Islamic tradition makes Mūsā the most-mentioned figure in the Qurʾān after God.
