Daniel is the central figure of the [[book-of-daniel]], a Judahite exile taken to Babylon in Nebuchadnezzar's first deportation, trained for royal service, and given the Babylonian name Belteshazzar. The book's structure is famously bipartite: chs. 1-6 are third-person court tales (the fiery furnace, the writing on the wall, the lions' den) set in the Babylonian and Persian courts; chs. 7-12 are first-person apocalyptic visions (the four beasts, the ram and goat, the seventy weeks, the resurrection passage 12:1-3).

Critical scholarship is broadly unified in dating the book in its present form to the mid-2nd c. BCE under the Seleucid persecutions of Antiochus IV Epiphanes (c. 167-164 BCE). The argument: the visions are accurate down to the persecution but vague and incorrect thereafter; the language mixes Hebrew and Aramaic in ways diagnostic of late composition; Daniel is unknown to Ben Sira's praise of ancestors (Sirach 49) where one would expect him; the genre, theology, and worldview are at home in Maccabean apocalyptic but not in 6th-c. Babylonian setting. Traditional and conservative scholarship defend a 6th-c. core or attempt to argue the textual data otherwise.

The reading determines almost everything. Under the late dating, the book is encouragement literature for Jews suffering under Antiochus IV, presenting earlier exilic survival as paradigm and projecting deliverance imminent. The "abomination of desolation" (Dan 9:27, 11:31) is Antiochus's altar to Olympian Zeus in the [[jerusalem]] [[temple]]. The "anointed one" cut off after 62 weeks is the murdered high priest Onias III. Resurrection theology emerges canonically in Dan 12:2 — the first unambiguous canonical statement of bodily resurrection — and is best explained by the martyrological pressures of the Maccabean crisis.

Daniel's afterlife in the canon is immense. The [[son-of-man]] figure of Dan 7 becomes a central NT christological designation; the 70 weeks of Dan 9 underwrite millennia of eschatological calculation; Antichrist typology draws from Dan 7-8 and 11. Jewish canonical placement is among the Writings (Ketuvim), not the Prophets — partly because of late dating in the formation of the canon — while Christian Bibles place Daniel among the major prophets.
