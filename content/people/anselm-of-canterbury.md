Anselm of Canterbury (1033-1109) — Benedictine theologian; abbot of Bec in Normandy, archbishop of Canterbury from 1093; pioneer of high-medieval rational theology and originator of the ontological argument. Born in Aosta in the Italian Alps; entered Bec under Lanfranc, his teacher and predecessor at Canterbury; succeeded Lanfranc both as prior of Bec and (a quarter century later, reluctantly) as primate of England.

Monologion (1076) — a meditation aiming to demonstrate the existence and attributes of God without appeal to Scripture, by reason alone (sola ratione). Proslogion (1077-1078) — the famous chapter 2 argument: God is "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" (id quo nihil maius cogitari possit); to conceive him as not existing is to contradict the concept, since existence in reality is greater than existence in the understanding alone. The objection of Gaunilo of Marmoutiers (the Lost Island reductio) was answered in Anselm's Reply; the argument has been debated continuously from [[thomas-aquinas]] (who rejected it) through Descartes, Kant, Plantinga, and contemporary modal logic.

Cur Deus Homo (1094-1098) — "Why God Became Man," in dialogue form with the monk Boso. Argues the satisfaction theory of the atonement: human sin contracts an infinite debt of honor against God; only a being both human (whose offense it is) and divine (whose recompense alone could be infinite) could satisfy. Replaces (without entirely displacing) the older patristic Christus Victor and ransom-from-Satan accounts; foundational for Latin scholastic atonement theology and via [[thomas-aquinas]] for Tridentine Catholic and Protestant penal-substitutionary developments alike.

Archbishop years (1093-1109) were politically turbulent: two exiles under William II and Henry I over the lay investiture controversy, paralleling [[gregory-vii]]'s (forward ref) Roman quarrel. "Faith seeking understanding" (fides quaerens intellectum) — programmatic for medieval theology after him. Declared Doctor of the Church by Clement XI (1720).
