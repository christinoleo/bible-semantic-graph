Habakkuk is the prophet of the short, theologically charged [[book-of-habakkuk]] (three chapters), prophesying during the rise of [[babylon]] and the late decline of Judah — most critical scholarship places him around 605 BCE, between the Battle of Carchemish and the first deportation, contemporary with the early career of [[jeremiah]]. The book stands out for its formal structure: prophet-to-deity dialogue (chs. 1-2) followed by a theophanic hymn (ch. 3) marked with the liturgical superscription and selah notations characteristic of [[psalms]].

The first complaint is internal: how long will [[yhwh]] tolerate violence and injustice within Judah without acting (Hab 1:2-4)? The first answer is jarring: YHWH is raising up the Chaldeans (Babylonians), "that bitter and hasty nation," to act as instrument of judgment (Hab 1:5-11). This prompts the second, more anguished complaint: how can a holy God use an even more wicked nation to punish the relatively less wicked? "You who are of purer eyes than to see evil and cannot look at wrong, why do you idly look at traitors and remain silent when the wicked swallows up the man more righteous than he?" (Hab 1:13).

The answer in ch. 2 includes the verse with the most outsized canonical afterlife of the book: "the righteous shall live by his faith / faithfulness" (Hab 2:4). The Hebrew *emunah* can mean either steadfast trust or steadfast faithfulness, and the translation choice colors the Pauline reception substantially. Paul cites it as proof-text for justification by faith (Rom 1:17, Gal 3:11); [[hebrews]] 10:38 uses it for endurance in persecution. The Habakkuk Pesher from Qumran (1QpHab) interprets the same verse for the Qumran community's Teacher of Righteousness, providing crucial pre-Christian Jewish reception data.

The closing hymn (ch. 3) is one of the canon's most vivid theophanies, with [[yhwh]] coming from Teman in earthquake and storm. The closing affirmation — "though the fig tree should not blossom… yet I will rejoice in YHWH" (Hab 3:17-18) — turns the book on its second theological move: the prophet's complaint resolves not in answered metaphysical question but in chosen praise under unresolved suffering.
