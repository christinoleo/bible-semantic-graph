Ulrich Zwingli (1484-1531) — leader of the Reformation in German-speaking Switzerland; people's priest at the Grossmünster in Zürich from 1519; the third figure (with [[martin-luther]] and [[john-calvin]]) of the first-generation Magisterial Reformation, though representing a Reformed trajectory distinct from both. Born in Wildhaus in the Toggenburg; educated at Basel, Bern, and Vienna; humanist by training, deep reader of [[erasmus-of-rotterdam]] (forward ref); ordained 1506; chaplain to Swiss mercenaries at Marignano (1515), an experience that turned him decisively against the mercenary trade and the political alliances that depended on it.

Arrived in Zürich 1 January 1519 — his thirty-fifth birthday — and immediately broke with the medieval lectionary by preaching continuously through [[gospel-of-matthew]], then through the other gospels, the Acts, and the Pauline epistles. Sources for his theological development distinct from Luther's: humanist exegesis, Augustinian sources, Erasmus, and (he claimed) independent biblical reflection. Public break with the Roman Church 1522 over Lenten fasting (the Affair of the Sausages); the Zürich council adopted the Reformation in stages 1523-1525 — Sixty-Seven Articles disputed publicly (January 1523), mass abolished (April 1525), images removed (1524), monasteries dissolved.

Eucharistic theology: signifies but does not contain the body and blood; "is" in "this is my body" reads as "signifies" (est = significat). Marburg Colloquy with Luther (October 1529), called by Philip of Hesse to unify Protestant Switzerland and Germany against imperial Catholicism, foundered on this single point — Luther chalked "Hoc est corpus meum" on the table and refused communion. The schism between Lutheran and Reformed traditions on the Lord's Supper dates from this moment. Later Reformed thinkers ([[bucer]], [[calvin]]) refined the position toward spiritual real presence, separating mature Reformed doctrine from a pure memorialism — but Zwingli's reputation for the latter persists.

Conflict with the radical Reformation (the so-called Anabaptists, including Conrad Grebel and Felix Manz, originally his disciples) over infant baptism, sword, and civil enforcement of religion — disagreements that culminated in the drowning execution of Manz in the Limmat (1527) under Zwingli's endorsement. Killed at the Battle of Kappel (October 1531) as field chaplain of the Zürich forces against the Catholic Forest Cantons — his body recovered, quartered, and burned with dung by the victors. Heinrich Bullinger succeeded him at the Grossmünster and brought Zürich into the Consensus Tigurinus (1549) with Calvin's Geneva, unifying Reformed practice in Switzerland.
