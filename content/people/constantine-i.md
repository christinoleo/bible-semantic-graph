Constantine I (~272-337) — Roman emperor 306-337; the figure under whose reign Christianity passed from a persecuted illicit cult to an imperially favored religion, inaugurating the so-called Constantinian shift. Born at Naissus (modern Niš, Serbia) to Constantius Chlorus, who would become Caesar then Augustus in the West, and Helena of obscure (probably innkeeper-class) origin who became Saint Helena of the True Cross legend. Spent his youth at the court of Diocletian in Nicomedia; rose to military command in the Tetrarchic system.

Battle of the Milvian Bridge (28 October 312) — the decisive engagement against Maxentius for control of Rome and the western empire. Lactantius (De mortibus persecutorum 44) reports a dream the night before in which Constantine was instructed to mark his troops' shields with a heavenly sign (Greek X-P, Chi-Rho, or the staurogram, depending on source); Eusebius (Vita Constantini 1.27-32) reports a later, more elaborate account given by Constantine under oath of a daytime vision of a cross in the sky with the words ἐν τούτῳ νίκα — "in this conquer." The two accounts cannot be exactly reconciled and have been read variously as separate visions, embellishment, political theology, or invention; the conversion's date and depth remain debated. He was baptized only on his deathbed in 337, by Eusebius of Nicomedia, whose Arian sympathies are themselves contested data about Constantine's late theology.

Edict of Milan (313, jointly with Licinius) — legalized Christianity throughout the empire, restored confiscated property, and extended general toleration. Patronage rapidly intensified: imperial endowment of basilicas (the Lateran, St. Peter's, Holy Sepulchre); tax exemption for clergy; appropriation of judicial functions; presence of bishops at court. Sole emperor after defeating Licinius (324).

Convened [[nicaea-i]] (325) at his summer residence after the Arian controversy threatened imperial cohesion — paying travel expenses, presiding ceremonially at the opening, and pressing for unanimity behind the homoousios formula even though his own theological commitments were vague and shifted afterward toward the Eusebian (later mildly Arianizing) party. Later exiled [[athanasius]] in 335 and accepted Arius back into communion shortly before both Arius and his own death.

Refounded Byzantium as Constantinople (consecrated 330) on the Bosporus — a Christian capital from its inception, freighted with Christian iconography and ritual, though Constantine kept a foot in traditional Roman religion (Sol Invictus iconography on coinage well into the 320s, the title pontifex maximus). Killed his own son Crispus and second wife Fausta in 326 in still-unexplained circumstances. Burial in the Church of the Holy Apostles surrounded by cenotaphs of the Twelve — claim to the title isapostolos, "equal to the apostles," in the Greek Christian tradition that has canonized him since.
