Judith ("Jewess"), wealthy widow of Manasseh in the (fictive) town of [[bethulia]], is the deliverer-heroine of [[book-of-judith]]. The narrative places her in a deliberately impossible historical setting — Nebuchadnezzar reigning in [[nineveh]] over the Assyrians and dispatching [[holofernes]] against post-exilic Judah — flagging the book as theological fiction rather than chronicle. The town stands as the last barrier protecting [[jerusalem]] and the rebuilt Temple.

When the elders of Bethulia agree to surrender in five days for lack of water, Judith rebukes them as testing God (Jdt 8) and proposes her own plan. Adorned and accompanied only by her maid, she enters the Assyrian camp, gains [[holofernes]]' trust over four days, and on the fourth night — alone in his tent, he too drunk to act — beheads him with his own sword (Jdt 13). She returns to Bethulia with the head in her food-bag; the Assyrians, finding their general decapitated at dawn, rout in panic.

The figure is a deliberate inversion of [[jael]] (Judg 4-5) and of [[esther]]: a woman alone in an enemy court, defeating empire through deception and theological fidelity. The book closes with her canticle (Jdt 16) in the [[song-of-deborah]] tradition. She refuses remarriage, frees her maid, and dies at 105.

Reception history is disproportionate to canonical status: Donatello's bronze (1457-64), Botticelli, Caravaggio (1599), Artemisia Gentileschi (1612 and 1620, freighted with autobiographical violence), Klimt (1901). The deliberately untenable historicity — no Assyrian general named Holofernes in the relevant period, Bethulia unidentified, geography incoherent — frames the book as midrashic novella, plausibly Hasmonean-era response to Hellenistic crisis. Excluded from the [[tanakh]]; in [[catholic]] and [[orthodox-eastern]] canon.
