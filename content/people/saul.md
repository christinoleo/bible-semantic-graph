Saul is Israel's first king (late 11th c. BCE), a Benjaminite of striking physical stature anointed by [[samuel]] under the pressure of popular demand (1 Sam 8-10). His reign is presented in [[1-samuel]] as a sustained tragedy — initial military successes against [[ammonite]] and [[philistine]] aggressors give way to a series of cultic infractions and a long deterioration into paranoia and prophetic abandonment.

The two pivotal rejections come at Gilgal, where Saul offers sacrifice without waiting for Samuel (1 Sam 13), and at Amalek, where he spares king Agag and the best livestock against the [[herem]] command (1 Sam 15). [[samuel]] pronounces the dynastic line forfeited — "the kingdom is torn from you" — and [[david]] is secretly anointed shortly after (1 Sam 16). The remainder of the book traces Saul's obsessive pursuit of David through the southern wilderness, punctuated by David's two demonstrative refusals to kill him.

Saul's interior life is given more space than most biblical kings — the "evil spirit from the LORD" that torments him (1 Sam 16:14), his oscillations between affection and murderous rage toward David, his consultation of the [[witch-of-endor]] in defiance of his own earlier ban on necromancers (1 Sam 28). He dies on Mt. Gilboa with his sons including [[jonathan-son-of-saul]], falling on his own sword (1 Sam 31). David's elegy in 2 Sam 1 ("how the mighty have fallen") preserves a generous public reading of his rival.

Source-critically, the Saul material shows seams between an "early source" sympathetic to monarchy and a Deuteronomistic frame that reads his fall as warning. The figure that emerges is theologically and psychologically more interesting than the flat villain some later traditions made of him.
