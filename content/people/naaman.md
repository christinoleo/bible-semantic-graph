Naaman is the commander of the army of Aram-Damascus under an unnamed king (probably Ben-Hadad II, mid-9th c. BCE) — "a great man with his master, and in high favor, because by him YHWH had given victory to Aram" (2 Kings 5:1). He is afflicted with [[tzaraat]] (the skin condition translated "leprosy" but covering a wider category in biblical usage). An Israelite slave girl in his household, captured in a raid, suggests he seek out the prophet in Samaria.

The pivot of the story is humility. [[elisha]] refuses to come out and meet the foreign general, sending instead a messenger with instructions to wash seven times in the [[jordan-river]]. Naaman rages — "Are not Abana and Pharpar, the rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel?" (2 Kings 5:12) — until his servants persuade him. He washes; his flesh is restored "like the flesh of a little child"; he returns and confesses [[yhwh]] as the only true deity in all the earth.

His petition to take two mule-loads of Israelite earth back to Damascus — so he can worship [[yhwh]] on Israelite soil even within the temple of Rimmon when accompanying his master — and Elisha's terse "go in peace" (2 Kings 5:18-19) is one of the canon's most striking accommodations of a Gentile worshipper still embedded in foreign cult. The contrast with Elisha's servant [[gehazi]], who chases Naaman down to extort gifts and contracts the disease as judgment, frames the chapter as a meditation on the inversion of insider/outsider status.

[[jesus]] mobilizes the episode in his Nazareth sermon ([[gospel-of-luke]] 4:27) as one of two case studies — Naaman the Syrian and the widow of [[zarephath]] — of [[yhwh]]'s favor passing over Israel to foreign recipients. The congregation tries to throw him off a cliff. The pattern is a foundational text for the Lukan and Pauline universalist mission to Gentiles.
