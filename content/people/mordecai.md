Mordecai is the cousin and adoptive guardian of [[esther]], a Benjaminite of the exilic community resident in Persian Susa. The genealogy of Esth 2:5 traces him back to Kish — the same name as [[saul]]'s father — and his Agagite antagonist [[haman]] is similarly drawn back to the Amalekite king Agag (cf. [[saul]] in [[1-samuel]] 15). The book frames the Mordecai/Haman conflict as the eschatological re-enactment of the unfinished Saul/Agag confrontation — a typological reading that the Hebrew narrative makes structurally explicit.

His refusal to bow to Haman at the king's gate (Esth 3:2-4) triggers the crisis. The text leaves the refusal's basis ambiguous — the bow may be straightforward courtly obeisance customary in the Persian court (which the Greek Additions explicitly address by having Mordecai pray that he refused only because he would bow only to God), or may be specifically a worship gesture, or may simply be ancestral enmity. Whatever the rationale, Haman generalizes the personal insult into genocidal policy against all the empire's Jews.

Mordecai's role is the narrative engine. He uncovers and reports the assassination plot of Bigthan and Teresh (Esth 2) — a deed forgotten until precisely the right moment. He confronts Esther with the demand to act despite the risk (Esth 4). He is paraded through Susa on the king's horse in royal robes by a humiliated Haman (Esth 6) in the central reversal. He drafts the counter-decree authorizing the Jews to defend themselves (Esth 8). At the book's close he is installed second in the kingdom after Ahasuerus and continues to "seek the good of his people and speak peace to all his offspring" (Esth 10:3).

The Mordecai/Esther partnership is a defining diaspora-political pairing: Mordecai's public Jewish identity outside the court and Esther's hidden Jewish identity inside the court combine to broker the survival of a vulnerable minority through influence rather than military or prophetic means. Rabbinic and later Jewish reception elaborates Mordecai into a Talmudic figure of Sanhedrin leadership and pious learning; the canonical text is more spare.
