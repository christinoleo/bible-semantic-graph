Joseph, eleventh son of [[jacob]], firstborn of [[rachel]]. Subject of the longest sustained narrative in Genesis (Gen 37, 39-50), often treated as a discrete literary unit — the "Joseph novella" — with a clear arc, named scenes, dream-pairing, and a wisdom-tale structure unlike the patriarchal cycles around it.

Favorite son. The `kĕtōnet passîm` ("coat of many colors" / "long-sleeved tunic") provokes his brothers; his dreams of sheaves and stars bowing to him intensify the hatred. Sold to a passing caravan — Ishmaelites or Midianites, the text is uncertain (Gen 37:25-28), one of the seams pointing to source composition. Taken to [[egypt]].

Rises in Potiphar's house; falsely accused by Potiphar's wife after refusing her; imprisoned (Gen 39). Interprets the dreams of the cupbearer and baker, then of Pharaoh — seven fat years, seven lean years. Made vizier (`šallîṭ`) over Egypt. Stockpiles grain. The famine drives his brothers down; he tests them through repeated cycles of accusation, ransom, and reversal (Gen 42-44) — most pointedly the planted cup in [[benjamin]]'s sack and [[judah]]'s redemptive speech offering himself in Benjamin's place. Joseph reveals himself, weeps, brings [[jacob]] and the family to Goshen, settles them through the famine while consolidating Egyptian land and labor under Pharaoh (Gen 47:13-26 — a darker subplot).

Theological signature line: "you intended evil against me, but God intended it for good" (Gen 50:20) — providence working through human malice without absolving it. Dies 110 years old; bones embalmed in Egypt, carried out at the Exodus (Ex 13:19), buried at Shechem (Josh 24:32).

Structural significance. The Joseph cycle reads as a diaspora wisdom-novel — a Jew rising in foreign court — and prefigures [[book-of-daniel]] and [[book-of-esther]]. His two sons Ephraim and Manasseh become tribes (a "double portion" reinforcing his firstborn-by-Rachel status). Patristic typology pairs Joseph with [[jesus]] (betrayed by brothers, descends, exalted, saves the very ones who rejected him). Islamic tradition gives Yūsuf an entire Quranic sura (Q 12) — the most extended narrative in the Qurʾān.
