Nehemiah son of Hacaliah is cupbearer to Artaxerxes I at Susa who secures imperial authorization in 445 BCE to return to [[jerusalem]] and rebuild the city wall ([[book-of-nehemiah]] 1-2). He serves as Persian-appointed governor (*pechah*) of Yehud for two terms, separated by a return to court, and is the principal political-administrative figure of the post-exilic community in the second half of the 5th c. BCE.

The wall rebuilding (Neh 3-7) is the book's narrative spine. He surveys the rubble by night, organizes the community into work crews by family and trade, defends against external opposition from Sanballat (governor of Samaria), Tobiah (an Ammonite official), and Geshem (an Arab leader), arms the workers with one hand and trowel in the other, and completes the wall in 52 days against considerable harassment. The narrative is preserved in a strikingly first-person "Nehemiah Memoir" — the most extended autobiographical material in the Hebrew Bible — with characteristic refrains ("remember to my credit, O my God").

The administrative reforms (Neh 5, 13) target debt-bondage among returnees, restoration of mortgaged land, tithing failures, Sabbath violations, mixed marriages, and abuses involving Tobiah's residence in a Temple storeroom (which Nehemiah personally throws out, along with all the man's belongings). His treatment of mixed marriages is even more confrontational than [[ezra]]'s — he "contends with them, curses them, beats some of them, and pulls out their hair" (Neh 13:25). The relationship between Nehemiah's reforms and [[ezra]]'s — the chronology, who came first, whether they overlapped — is one of the canonical historiographic puzzles, with critical proposals ranging from straightforward sequence to substantial dislocation of Ezra to the reign of Artaxerxes II.

His covenant renewal assembly under Ezra's Torah reading (Neh 8-10) — the people fasting and confessing, the long historical confession of Neh 9, the signed pledge to keep the law — is foundational for the development of Second Temple Judaism as a covenant-renewal religion oriented around Torah text. The administrative pairing with [[ezra]] (priest-scribe / governor) becomes the structural model for diaspora Jewish leadership pairing scholarship with civil authority.
