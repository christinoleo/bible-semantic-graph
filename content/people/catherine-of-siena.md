Catherine of Siena (1347-1380) — Dominican tertiary; mystical theologian, ecclesiastical activist, and one of the most influential lay women of the Latin Middle Ages. Twenty-fourth (or twenty-fifth) child of a Sienese wool-dyer and his wife. Received her first visions at six or seven; resisted family pressure to marry; entered the Dominican Third Order (mantellate — laywomen wearing the Dominican habit while living at home) around 1364 at sixteen or seventeen.

Three years of contemplative seclusion in a tiny room of the family house (1364-1367), culminating in the mystical betrothal to Christ; thereafter, abrupt entry into public ministry — care for the sick and prisoners in Siena, then political mediation across Italy. Gathered a famiglia of disciples — laity, clerics, scholars — who took down her dictated letters; Catherine was probably barely literate, learning to write only late in life. The Dialogue of Divine Providence (Il Dialogo, 1377-1378) — extended exchange between the soul and God the Father on the bridge of Christ, the cell of self-knowledge, the threefold staircase of feet, side, and mouth of Christ. Distinctive imagery, theologically dense, dictated to amanuenses (Raymond of Capua, her confessor, prominent among them).

Pressed Gregory XI through letters and (1376) in person at Avignon to return the papacy to Rome, against the resistance of the French cardinals — accomplished 1377. After Gregory's death and the contested election that opened the Great Western Schism (1378), supported the Roman claimant Urban VI against Clement VII in Avignon; spent her last years in Rome pleading for unity until her death from progressive paralysis at thirty-three (April 1380). Stigmata reportedly received at Pisa in 1375 — invisible to others by her request.

Letters (381 surviving) — addressed to popes, princes, condottieri, prisoners, nuns, family members — the largest extant medieval epistolary corpus by a woman, in vigorous Tuscan vernacular. Canonized 1461; named patron of Italy alongside [[francis-of-assisi]] in 1939; Doctor of the Church 1970 (with [[teresa-of-avila]], the first women so declared by Paul VI); proclaimed co-patron of Europe 1999.
