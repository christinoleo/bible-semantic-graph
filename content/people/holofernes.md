Holofernes is the Assyrian commander-in-chief dispatched by Nebuchadnezzar — in [[book-of-judith]] anachronistically king of Nineveh rather than [[babylon]] — to punish the western nations that refused to join his campaign against [[media]]. He devastates the Levantine coast, sieges the fictive Judahite town of [[bethulia]], and is beheaded by [[judith]] in his own tent on the fourth night of her infiltration (Jdt 13). His severed head, displayed on the Bethulia wall at dawn, triggers the Assyrian rout.

The narrative role is the archetypal hubristic foreign general — wine-drunk, lustful, contemptuous of [[yhwh]] ("who is god except Nebuchadnezzar?", Jdt 6:2) — a composite of every Near Eastern oppressor in the Jewish memory. No historical Assyrian general named Holofernes is attested; the name is Persian (Cappadocian satrap Holophernes served Artaxerxes III against Egypt c. 350 BCE) and is generally read as a deliberate or accidental displacement of a Persian/Hellenistic figure into a pseudo-Assyrian setting. Most scholars locate the book's composition in the Hasmonean period (late 2nd c. BCE) with Holofernes/Nebuchadnezzar as cipher for [[antiochus-iv-epiphanes]] or a Seleucid general.

Patristic exegesis allegorizes him as the diabolus decapitated by the Church (Judith); Renaissance and Baroque iconography fixates on the beheading scene (Caravaggio, Artemisia Gentileschi, Cranach) as the locus where female agency meets political violence. The figure has no independent existence outside [[book-of-judith]] — a literary construct in the strict sense.
