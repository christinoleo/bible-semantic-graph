John Chrysostom (~347-407) — "Golden-mouthed," archbishop of Constantinople from 397; foremost preacher of the patristic age. Antiochene by formation: trained in rhetoric under Libanius, in scripture under Diodore of Tarsus alongside Theodore of Mopsuestia. Twelve years as ascetic and presbyter in Antioch (386-397) produced the bulk of his exegetical homiletic output, including the great verse-by-verse series on [[gospel-of-matthew]], [[gospel-of-john]], [[acts]], and the Pauline corpus.

Antiochene literal-historical hermeneutic: against the high-flown allegoresis of the Alexandrians, John reads the text rhetorically and pastorally, attending to authorial intent (skopos), audience, and moral application. Less speculative than [[origen]] or [[gregory-of-nyssa]], more directly applicable to the social ethics of urban Christian life. Repeatedly attacks luxury, theatre, slavery's abuses, and the indifference of wealthy Christians to the poor — "the rich man is a thief" recurs across his corpus.

Constantinople episcopate (397-404) is a study in collision: a reforming preacher and a corrupt imperial court (Eutropius the eunuch; the empress Eudoxia; bishop Severian of Gabala; Theophilus of Alexandria). Deposed by the Synod of the Oak (403) under trumped charges; recalled briefly; exiled again 404 to Pontic Comana, where he died of forced overland travel and exposure. "Glory to God for all things" reportedly his last words.

Adversus Judaeos (Eight Homilies Against the Jews, Antioch, 386-387) — virulent rhetorical attacks on Christian Judaizers attending the synagogue and on Jews themselves. Historically catastrophic: weaponized in late antique and medieval anti-Jewish polemic, in the Reformation, and in Nazi propaganda; modern scholarship contextualizes the rhetorical genre (psogos) and the local intra-Christian quarrel but does not absolve the texts of their effects. Hagiographic reception ("Chrysostom" affixed by the 6th century) and liturgical influence (the Divine Liturgy of St. John Chrysostom, the standard Byzantine eucharistic anaphora) remain enormous.
