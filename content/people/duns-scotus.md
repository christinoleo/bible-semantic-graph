John Duns Scotus (c. 1266-1308) — Franciscan friar; the *Doctor Subtilis* (Subtle Doctor); the Franciscan school's most original alternative to the Dominican Aristotelianism of [[thomas-aquinas]]. Born at Duns in the Scottish Borders; ordained priest at Northampton in 1291 (terminus a quo for his birth year); studied and lectured at [[oxford]] and [[paris]], with a brief period at Cambridge. Final relocation to the Franciscan studium at Cologne in 1307, where he died the following year; remains the *Doctor Marianus* of Franciscan tradition, beatified in 1993.

Three distinctive theses define his philosophical signature. Univocity of being — *ens* is predicated univocally (not merely analogically) of God and creatures, a precondition for any rational natural theology; contrary to the analogical doctrine of [[thomas-aquinas]] and a position with lasting consequences for modern metaphysics. The formal distinction (*distinctio formalis a parte rei*) — a real distinction weaker than the distinction of separate things, allowing him to articulate divine attributes, the persons of the Trinity, and individuating principles (haecceity, "thisness") without compromising simplicity. Voluntarist priority of the divine will — God's freedom is not constrained by an antecedent rational necessity; the moral law derives from the divine will, not the divine intellect. Defended the [[immaculate-conception]] of [[mary]] against contemporary Dominican (and Thomistic) denial via the *praeservatio* argument — Mary preserved from original sin in view of Christ's future merits — the position eventually adopted by [[pius-ix]] in *Ineffabilis Deus* (1854). His [[scholastic]] inheritance bifurcated: appropriated by [[william-of-ockham]] on certain points while being a chief target of his nominalist critique, then revived in twentieth-century Catholic philosophy and analytic philosophy of religion alike.
