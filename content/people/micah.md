Micah of Moresheth-Gath is an 8th-c. BCE Judahite prophet, contemporary of [[isaiah]] and [[hosea]], prophesying under Jotham, Ahaz, and [[hezekiah]] in the decades around the destruction of [[samaria]] (722 BCE). He comes from a small town in the Shephelah, the agricultural hinterland between the Judean hills and the Philistine coast — and his prophetic register reflects rural and small-town perspective on the depredations of [[jerusalem]] elites.

The book moves between sharp judgment oracles against both Samaria and Jerusalem and restoration oracles. The indictment of social oppression is comparable in intensity to [[amos]]: "they covet fields and seize them; and houses, and take them away; they oppress a man and his house, a man and his inheritance" (Mic 2:2). "The heads of Jacob… abhor justice and pervert all equity, building Zion with blood and Jerusalem with iniquity. Its heads give judgment for a bribe, its priests teach for a price, its prophets practice divination for money" (Mic 3:9-11). His prophecy that Zion shall be plowed as a field and Jerusalem become a heap of ruins (Mic 3:12) is cited a century later in [[book-of-jeremiah]] 26:18 as precedent — and as evidence that [[hezekiah]] feared YHWH and the city was spared.

Two passages are disproportionately consequential in canonical reception. Mic 5:2 ("but you, O Bethlehem Ephrathah… from you shall come forth for me one who is to be ruler in Israel") is cited in [[gospel-of-matthew]] 2:6 as predicting [[jesus]]'s Bethlehem birth. Mic 6:8 ("he has told you, O man, what is good; and what does YHWH require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God") is the iconic prophetic ethical summary, cited centrally in Jewish and Christian moral tradition as the most compact distillation of the prophetic message.

Critical scholarship treats the book as composite — the judgment material of chs. 1-3 attributed broadly to the 8th-c. prophet, with later editorial expansion in chs. 4-7 (especially the Zion oracle 4:1-4, which appears nearly verbatim in [[book-of-isaiah]] 2:2-4 and whose direction of dependence is debated). The book's structural alternation of judgment and salvation is itself often read as a later compilation pattern rather than original arrangement.
