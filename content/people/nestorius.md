Nestorius (~386-450) — patriarch of Constantinople from 428 to 431; Antiochene-trained theologian whose name attaches (more or less fairly) to the dyophysite Christology condemned at [[ephesus]] (431). Born in Germanicia in Syria; monk and presbyter at Antioch under the homiletic style of [[john-chrysostom]] and the exegetical lineage of Diodore of Tarsus and Theodore of Mopsuestia; elevated to the imperial capital under Theodosius II.

The controversy ignited within months of his arrival. Nestorius backed the priest Anastasius, who preached against the Marian title Theotokos ("God-bearer"), arguing that strictly God cannot be born; the title Christotokos ("Christ-bearer") preserves theological precision. Nestorius's own emphasis: the union of divinity and humanity in Christ is a moral and personal conjunction (synapheia, "joining") of two complete natures; the divine Logos cannot literally be the subject of human birth, suffering, death — those are predicated of the humanity, the divine acts of the divinity, the one Christ as the conjoined unity (prosōpon of union).

[[cyril-of-alexandria]] read this as effectively positing two sons — divine and human — only verbally conjoined, dividing Christ. Whether Nestorius actually held the two-persons (or two-sons) Christology subsequently called "Nestorianism" is genuinely debated. His own surviving works — especially the Bazaar of Heracleides (Liber Heraclidis), composed in exile and rediscovered in Syriac in 1889 — present a more nuanced position that affirms one prosōpon of union even while reserving particular grammatical objections to attributions of suffering directly to the divine nature. The Antiochene tradition that followed him (Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret of Cyrrhus) was condemned at [[constantinople-ii]] (553) as part of the Three Chapters controversy.

Condemned and deposed at [[ephesus]] (431) before his Antiochene supporters arrived; exiled to Antioch monastery, then to Upper Egypt; died there in 451 — reportedly having heard of [[chalcedon]]'s formula and considering himself vindicated. The Church of the East (so-called Nestorian Church, though it does not use the label and historically broke with Nestorius's theological successors more than with him) traces its continuity to the Syrian dyophysite tradition; it never accepted Ephesus and remains in communion with the early Antiochene fathers.
