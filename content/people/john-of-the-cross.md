John of the Cross (1542-1591) — Spanish Discalced Carmelite friar; mystical theologian and major lyric poet of the Spanish Golden Age. Born Juan de Yepes y Álvarez at Fontiveros to an impoverished family — his father, a converso silk-weaver, had been disinherited for marrying his mother. Childhood of acute material deprivation; trained in carpentry and tailoring; served as orderly at a hospital for venereal disease in Medina del Campo while studying with the Jesuits 1559-1563. Entered the Carmelite Order 1563, taking the name Juan de San Matía.

Met [[teresa-of-avila]] in Medina in 1567; she was forty years his senior and had recently begun her Discalced reform; recruited him on the spot to extend the reform to the male branch. Took the new name Juan de la Cruz at the inauguration of the first Discalced friary at Duruelo (1568) with two companions. Twenty-three years of relentless labor — confessor to Teresa's convent of the Incarnation in Ávila (1572-1577), foundation work across Castile and Andalusia, prior at El Calvario, Baeza, Granada, Segovia.

Imprisoned in Toledo (December 1577 - August 1578) by the Calced Carmelite leadership in the struggle over the Discalced reform's legitimacy — held in a six-by-ten-foot cell, beaten weekly, allowed neither change of clothes nor reading material. During those nine months he composed in memory the first stanzas of the Spiritual Canticle and likely the Dark Night poem. Escaped through a window with knotted strips of his cell-coverings; the entire literary corpus dates from after this captivity.

Four major works, each combining a brief lyric poem with extensive prose commentary. The Ascent of Mount Carmel and the Dark Night of the Soul together expound the threefold purification (active and passive nights of sense and spirit) by which the soul is detached from creatures and prepared for union with God. The Spiritual Canticle (lyric structurally modeled on the Song of Songs) and the Living Flame of Love treat the higher stages of union. The signature theological move: the via negativa — nada, nada, nada — radical apophatic detachment from images, feelings, particular knowledge, even spiritual consolations themselves; faith as a "dark light" that surpasses sense and intellect. Influences span Augustinian, Dionysian, and Thomist sources, integrated with personal mystical experience and Spanish poetic form. Died at Úbeda from infection following neglect by hostile Carmelite superiors. Canonized 1726; Doctor of the Church 1926.
