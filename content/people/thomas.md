Thomas — one of the Twelve, named in all four Twelve-lists. Aramaic Tʾoma and Greek Didymos both mean "twin"; whose twin is never specified, fueling speculative traditions (twin of [[jesus]] in Syriac apocrypha). Synoptic gospels list him without narrative role.

The Johannine portrait gives him three set pieces, all theologically charged. (1) [[gospel-of-john]] 11:16: when [[jesus]] resolves to go to [[lazarus]] in Bethany despite mortal danger, Thomas says "Let us also go, that we may die with him" — courage shading into resignation. (2) [[gospel-of-john]] 14:5: "Lord, we do not know where you are going; how can we know the way?" — the question that triggers the I-am-the-way discourse. (3) [[gospel-of-john]] 20:24-29: absent from the first resurrection appearance, demands to touch the wounds, then confesses "My Lord and my God" — the highest Christological confession in the Fourth Gospel. The cluster gives him the lasting handle "Doubting Thomas."

Tradition assigns him a mission to India: the Syrian Christians of Kerala (the Mar Thoma church) trace their origin to his 1st-century preaching; archaeological and literary attestation is thin but not impossible given established Roman-Indian trade routes. The Acts of Thomas (3rd-c. Syriac) elaborates the Indian mission with strongly encratite theology.

The Gospel of Thomas — a Coptic collection of 114 sayings discovered at Nag Hammadi (1945), Greek fragments earlier — attributes itself to "Didymos Judas Thomas." Non-canonical, often labeled gnostic though the label fits loosely; some sayings parallel the synoptics and may preserve independent tradition (debated, with the "Crossan side" reading it as early and "Meier side" as late and derivative). Its prominence as a source for the historical Jesus is disproportionate to scholarly consensus.
