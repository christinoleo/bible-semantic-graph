Bishop of Mopsuestia in Cilicia (c. 350 – 428), the most influential Antiochene exegete after his teacher Diodore of Tarsus and the principal doctrinal authority of the [[church-of-the-east]] tradition, which reveres him as "the Interpreter" (*mpašqānā*). His Christology — articulated through a sharp distinction between the assumed humanity and the assuming Logos, joined in a "person of union" without confusion — became the conceptual ancestor of [[nestorianism]], and his pupil Nestorius pressed the same grammar into the formula condemned at [[ephesus]] (431).

Theodore died in communion with the church, but the Three Chapters edict of Justinian (544) and the Second Council of [[constantinople-ii]] (553) condemned his person and writings posthumously — a controversial move (the West resisted; Pope Vigilius initially refused, then capitulated under duress) that succeeded in suppressing the Antiochene exegetical tradition within the Byzantine world but cemented Theodore's authority in the East Syriac church beyond the Roman frontier, where his commentaries on Scripture and his catechetical writings became the dogmatic foundation of an entirely distinct Christian tradition.
