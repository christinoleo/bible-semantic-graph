Cyprian of Carthage (~200-258) — wealthy pagan rhetor; converted around 246 under the priest Caecilianus; elected bishop of Carthage within two years over the heads of senior presbyters, sowing the seeds of a rivalry that would shape his episcopate. Read [[tertullian]] daily — "give me the master" — though stripped of the Montanist excesses.

The Decian persecution (250-251) precipitated the crisis that defined him. Many Carthaginian Christians lapsed (sacrificed or obtained false certificates, libelli) under threat. Cyprian fled into hiding and governed by letter — actions criticized by Roman clergy and rival presbyters. The post-persecution question — under what conditions to reconcile the lapsi to communion — pitted him against rigorist Novatianists (who refused reconciliation) and laxist confessors (who reconciled freely on the merits of confessor-martyrs). His De lapsis stakes out a graduated penitential discipline.

De unitate ecclesiae (251) — the foundational Latin treatise on church unity. Two recensions of chapter 4 survive: the "primacy text" giving Peter pride of place (long used in Roman polemic) and the "received text" emphasizing equal episcopal collegiality. Most modern scholars (Bévenot et alia) hold both are Cyprianic, the primacy text earlier, the received text revised after the Roman dispute on rebaptism. "Habere non potest Deum patrem qui ecclesiam non habet matrem" (you cannot have God for your Father who do not have the Church for your mother). "Salus extra ecclesiam non est" (no salvation outside the church) — Cyprian's formula, often attributed to him in the [[augustine-of-hippo]] form "extra ecclesiam nulla salus".

Rebaptism controversy with Pope Stephen I (255-257): Cyprian held that schismatics' and heretics' baptisms were invalid, must be repeated; Stephen held they were valid, only laying on of hands needed. The dispute did not lead to schism but resurfaces at Augustine's Donatist controversy. Beheaded under Valerian's second edict, 14 September 258 — the Acta proconsularia preserve the trial verbatim, the earliest such Latin Christian record.
