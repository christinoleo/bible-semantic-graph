Jeremiah son of Hilkiah is the prophet whose career spans the catastrophic last four decades of Judah, from the 13th year of [[josiah]] (c. 627 BCE) through the fall of [[jerusalem]] (586 BCE) and into the Egyptian Jewish diaspora afterward. He is the most psychologically exposed prophet in the canon, partly because [[book-of-jeremiah]] preserves an extensive prose biographical layer (the "B" material in Mowinckel's source-critical scheme), partly because the so-called Confessions (Jer 11-20) record interior complaint that approaches accusation against [[yhwh]] ("you have deceived me, and I was deceived").

His message is single-minded: submit to [[babylon]] as [[yhwh]]'s instrument of judgment; the Temple is no guarantee of safety; covenant violation has run too long for reprieve. This puts him in direct conflict with court prophets, pro-Egyptian factions, the [[jerusalem]] establishment, and frequently king [[zedekiah]] — who consults him secretly while publicly tolerating his persecution. He is locked in stocks, lowered into a muddy cistern to die, dragged out by an Ethiopian eunuch, repeatedly imprisoned. The Temple Sermon (Jer 7, 26) — denouncing the "deceptive words, 'this is the Temple of YHWH'" — nearly costs him his life.

After [[jerusalem]] falls he is offered safe passage to Babylon and chooses to remain with the Judahite remnant in the land. After the assassination of Gedaliah the Babylonian-appointed governor, the surviving community panics and flees to [[egypt]], dragging Jeremiah with them against his counsel. He continues prophesying judgment from there. The last we hear of him is in Egypt; legends of his death there or martyrdom by stoning are post-biblical.

His scribe [[baruch]] son of Neriah is the canonical companion figure and likely first compiler/redactor of the Jeremiah material. The book exists in two markedly different ancient forms — the Hebrew Masoretic Text and a Greek Septuagint shorter by about 1/8 and ordered differently — one of the clearest cases of textual pluriformity for any biblical book. Traditional attribution of [[lamentations]] to Jeremiah is not made by the book itself and is not held by critical scholarship.
