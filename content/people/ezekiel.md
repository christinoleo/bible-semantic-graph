Ezekiel son of Buzi the priest is the prophet of the Babylonian exile, taken to [[babylon]] in the 597 BCE deportation alongside king Jehoiachin. His call vision (Ezek 1) — the elaborate chariot-throne with four living creatures, wheels within wheels, the rim full of eyes — opens the book in Babylonian territory by the river Chebar in the fifth year of exile (c. 593 BCE) and inaugurates one of the most visually saturated prophetic corpora in the canon. The merkavah ("chariot") imagery becomes foundational for later Jewish mystical tradition (Maaseh Merkavah, Hekhalot literature).

His ministry is structured as before-and-after the [[fall-of-jerusalem-586]]. The earlier oracles (chs. 1-24) announce inevitable judgment on Jerusalem, often through dramatic symbolic actions: lying on his left side for 390 days and right side for 40 days bearing the iniquities of the two houses, eating defiled bread, shaving his head and dividing the hair by sword, fire, and wind. When his wife dies (Ezek 24) he is forbidden to mourn — sign-act for an exilic community who will lose city and Temple and likewise be unable to mourn properly. The temple-abandonment vision (Ezek 8-11) — where the [[kavod]] (divine glory) departs the [[temple]] eastward — is the theological climax of the pre-fall material.

The later oracles (chs. 25-48) shift to oracles against the nations, then to restoration: the valley of dry bones (Ezek 37), Gog of Magog (chs. 38-39), and the visionary new Temple (chs. 40-48). The new-temple vision is more architecturally detailed than anything else in the canon, including specifications never built and unbuilable — a contested whether-and-how status that has driven exegetical traditions from Second Temple millenarianism through dispensationalist literalism to symbolic-eschatological readings.

The book's literary unity is strong relative to other prophetic books — most critical scholarship accepts a substantial 6th-c. prophetic core with subsequent priestly redaction. The theology is distinctively priestly (holiness, ritual purity, Temple-centered) and individualistic ("the soul who sins shall die," ch. 18) in contrast to the more collective covenant frame of other prophets.
