Bathsheba is the wife of Uriah the Hittite, taken by [[david]] in the most morally pointed episode of [[2-samuel]] (2 Sam 11). David sees her bathing from his palace roof, summons her, sleeps with her, and on learning of her pregnancy arranges Uriah's death at the front. The narrator's restraint is total — Bathsheba's interior is never given — but [[nathan-prophet]]'s parable of the rich man taking the poor man's lamb (2 Sam 12) names David's act unambiguously as theft and murder.

Her later trajectory is political. After the death of the first child, she bears [[solomon]] (2 Sam 12:24). At David's senescence (1 Kings 1), with [[adonijah]] making his own succession bid, she — coached by [[nathan-prophet]] — enters David's chamber to secure the throne for Solomon by reminding him of a prior oath. Whether the oath was actual or fabricated by Nathan and Bathsheba is one of the canon's most-discussed silences. The succession is brokered in that scene.

Solomon installs her as [[gevirah]] (queen mother) — a politically substantive office in Judahite court structure (1 Kings 2:19). She intercedes for [[adonijah]] in a request for [[abishag]] that Solomon reads as renewed dynastic threat and uses as pretext for Adonijah's execution. The episode is the last we see of her.

The [[gospel-of-matthew]] genealogy of [[jesus]] names her obliquely — "the wife of Uriah" (Matt 1:6) — one of four women included alongside Tamar, [[rahab]], and [[ruth]], each marked by irregular sexual circumstance and Gentile or outsider association. Reception history has oscillated between reading her as victim of royal coercion and as complicit seductress; the text itself supports the former far better than the latter.
