Hildegard of Bingen (1098-1179) — Benedictine abbess of Rupertsberg; visionary, theologian, composer, natural philosopher, healer, and correspondent of kings, popes, and emperors. Tenth child of a noble Rhineland family; offered as oblate at eight to the anchoress Jutta of Sponheim at the male Benedictine monastery of Disibodenberg; took the veil around fifteen; succeeded Jutta as magistra of the female community in 1136.

Reports of recurring luminous visions since childhood; in 1141 a commanding command "to write what you see and hear" launched the public phase. Scivias (sci vias Domini, "Know the ways of the Lord," composed 1141-1151) — twenty-six visions on creation, redemption, and last things, with extensive theological exposition and the famous iconography (now widely reproduced) found in the lost Rupertsberg manuscript. Submitted for approval to [[bernard-of-clairvaux]] (1146), who endorsed her, and Pope Eugene III at the Synod of Trier (1147-1148) endorsed her further — exceptionally explicit institutional sanction for a female visionary.

Liber vitae meritorum (1158-1163) — vices and virtues in dialogue; Liber divinorum operum (1163-1173) — ten visions of cosmic order integrating microcosm and macrocosm. Physica and Causae et curae (~1150s) — natural-philosophical and medical handbooks on stones, animals, plants, elements, and human physiology; she compiles and reframes received Salernitan and folk medicine within a viriditas ("greenness," divine vital force) metaphysics distinctively her own. Symphonia armonie celestium revelationum collects seventy-seven monophonic compositions in characteristic soaring melodies — some of the most distinctive surviving medieval liturgical music. Ordo virtutum, a sung morality play, predates the genre's later development by more than a century.

Founded Rupertsberg (1150) over the resistance of Disibodenberg's monks who lost income and prestige; founded Eibingen (1165) as a daughter house. Undertook four preaching tours of the Rhineland — striking activity for a cloistered nun. Voluminous correspondence with Bernard of Clairvaux, Henry II of England, Frederick Barbarossa, four popes. Late conflict over the burial of an excommunicate at Rupertsberg led to interdict over her community, lifted shortly before her death (1179). Canonization remained informal for centuries; equipollently canonized and declared Doctor of the Church by Benedict XVI in 2012 — the fourth woman so honored.
