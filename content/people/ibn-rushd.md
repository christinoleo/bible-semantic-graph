Ibn Rushd (1126-1198) — Andalusian Mālikī jurist, court physician, and philosopher; in the Latin West simply *The Commentator* on account of his line-by-line readings of Aristotle that shaped [[scholastic]] reception of the Greek corpus from the thirteenth century onward. Born into a Córdoban family of Mālikī chief judges; served as *qāḍī* of Seville and then Córdoba, and as personal physician to the Almohad caliphs Abū Yaʿqūb Yūsuf and Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb al-Manṣūr; fell into political disgrace late in life and was briefly exiled to Lucena before partial rehabilitation; died at [[marrakesh]].

*Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* (The Incoherence of the Incoherence) — point-by-point defense of Aristotelian [[falsafa]] against [[al-ghazali]]'s *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*, recovering the eternity of the world, the philosophical reading of providence, and the legitimacy of demonstrative reasoning in matters of revelation. *Faṣl al-Maqāl* (The Decisive Treatise) argues, from within Mālikī jurisprudence, that philosophical inquiry is religiously obligatory for the qualified — the most ambitious medieval Muslim case for the harmony of reason and revelation. Engages [[classical-kalam]] critically: rejects Ashʿarite occasionalism and the dialectical method as inadequate substitutes for demonstrative science. Latin Averroism — read through translations by Michael Scot and others — becomes the polemical foil against which [[thomas-aquinas]] and the Paris condemnations of 1277 define [[scholastic]] orthodoxy.
