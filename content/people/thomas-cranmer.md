Thomas Cranmer (1489-1556) — archbishop of Canterbury from 1533 to 1553; principal architect of the English Reformation in its liturgical and confessional form. Born to minor gentry in Aslockton, Nottinghamshire; educated at Jesus College, Cambridge, where he absorbed humanist scholarship and was exposed to Lutheran ideas from the 1520s; quietly held evangelical views from at least 1531. Came to Henry VIII's attention through the suggestion (1529) of consulting university theologians on the king's divorce — a proposal that opened the path to his rapid elevation.

Appointed archbishop of Canterbury in 1533 by Henry VIII over more senior candidates, on condition of cooperating with the king's nullification of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon. Privately married — illegally for a priest — Margarete, niece of the Lutheran theologian Andreas Osiander, around the same time; transported her concealed across the Channel and kept her hidden for years. Steered the Reformation in cautious stages under Henry — preserving theological evangelical commitments while accepting royal supremacy and a generally conservative liturgical and doctrinal stance during the king's lifetime — and then moved decisively under the boy king Edward VI (1547-1553).

Liturgical legacy is the centerpiece. The first English Book of Common Prayer (1549) translated and selectively reformed medieval Sarum materials into stately vernacular prose. The second (1552) — far more thoroughly Reformed in eucharistic theology and ceremonial — replaced explicit affirmations of bodily presence with reception-emphasizing language, removed prayers for the dead, abolished the elevation of the host. The Defence of the True and Catholic Doctrine of the Sacrament (1550) and his 1551 disputation with Stephen Gardiner articulate a spiritual-presence Reformed eucharist closer to Bucer and the mature Reformed tradition than to Zwinglian memorialism. The 42 Articles (1553) — confessional standard, the basis of the later 39 Articles (1571).

Imprisoned at Mary I's accession (1553); tried for heresy and treason; subjected to lengthy interrogation and pressure that produced five recantations, signed under different degrees of duress — a moral collapse he repented publicly. Burned at the stake in Oxford on 21 March 1556 — at the stake he repudiated all recantations and thrust the hand that had signed them into the flames first, an act recorded by [[john-foxe]] (forward ref) in the Book of Martyrs and indelibly fixed in English Protestant memory. His liturgical English — the cadence of collect, eucharistic prayer, and burial office — remains the deep substrate of Anglican worship and a substantial literary monument of early modern English.
