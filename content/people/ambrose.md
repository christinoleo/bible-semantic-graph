Ambrose of Milan (~340-397) — bishop of Milan from 374; the great Latin bishop of the late fourth century alongside whom imperial Christianity took its institutional shape in the West. Born to a praetorian-prefect family in Trier; trained in Roman law; serving as consular governor of Liguria-Aemilia at Milan when the death of the Homoian Arian bishop Auxentius prompted a contested election. Still a catechumen — neither baptized nor ordained — he was acclaimed by both parties to defuse the impasse and was baptized, ordained, and consecrated within a week (December 374).

Self-taught in theology during a rapid catch-up, leaning heavily on Greek sources ([[origen]], [[basil-the-great]], Didymus the Blind) which he mediated into Latin. The Hexaemeron, On the Holy Spirit, and On the Faith transpose Cappadocian and Alexandrian content into Western idiom. Allegorical exegesis (in continuity with Origen and against the literal Antiochene mode) shaped [[augustine-of-hippo]] decisively — the figural reading of the Old Testament that "opened the scriptures" for the future bishop (Conf. 5.13-14).

Confrontations with imperial power define his episcopate. Resisted the empress Justina's demand for a basilica for the Arian Gothic mercenaries in 385-386 — the basilica siege, the hymn-singing congregations as nonviolent resistance, the introduction of antiphonal Ambrosian hymnody. After the massacre at Thessalonica (390), required public penance from Theodosius I — the first major precedent of a bishop disciplining a reigning emperor. Forced concession from Valentinian II in the Altar of Victory dispute (384) against Symmachus's plea for restoration of the pagan altar in the Senate.

On the Duties of Ministers — Christian appropriation of Cicero's De officiis, the foundational Latin treatise on Christian ethics for clergy. Composed Latin hymns (Aeterne rerum conditor, Veni redemptor gentium) in iambic dimeter that fixed the meter of Latin office hymnody for a thousand years. Baptized [[augustine-of-hippo]] at Easter 387; the Confessions remember Ambrose as the model of episcopal gravitas — and of silent reading (Conf. 6.3).
