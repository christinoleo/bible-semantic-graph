Judas Maccabeus, third son of [[mattathias]], is the military protagonist of the [[maccabean-revolt]]. The epithet "Maccabaeus" — conventionally glossed "the hammer" (Aramaic maqqaba) — becomes the family name in Greek and Latin tradition, though [[1-maccabees]] applies it only to Judas himself. He assumes command on his father's death in 166 BCE and over the next six years defeats successive Seleucid armies under Apollonius, Seron, Gorgias/Nicanor, and Lysias in a series of guerrilla and set-piece engagements (1 Macc 3-4).

In Kislev 164 BCE he reclaims [[jerusalem]], purifies the Temple defiled by [[antiochus-iv-epiphanes]] (the "abomination of desolation" of [[book-of-daniel]] 11:31), and reinstitutes sacrifice — the origin of [[hanukkah]] (1 Macc 4:36-59; [[2-maccabees]] 10). [[2-maccabees]] adds the cruse-of-oil tradition only obliquely; the eight-day miracle is a later rabbinic development (b. Shabbat 21b). Judas continues campaigning against Idumean, Ammonite, and Galilean neighbours, and conducts the first known Jewish diplomatic mission to [[rome]] (1 Macc 8) — a treaty whose actual force has been disputed.

He falls at Elasa (160 BCE) against Bacchides, abandoned by all but eight hundred men (1 Macc 9). [[2-maccabees]] ends earlier, at the victory over Nicanor (161 BCE), and never narrates Judas's death — a deliberate hagiographic choice. The figure is the prototype of the warrior-priest and a recurrent reference point in later Jewish messianism, Christian crusading rhetoric (Handel's Judas Maccabaeus, 1747), and modern Zionist iconography. Distinct from [[judas-iscariot]] and from [[jude]] of the eponymous epistle.
