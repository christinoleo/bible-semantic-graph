Samuel straddles three offices — last judge of [[judges]]'s line, first of the classical prophets, and a Levite raised at the sanctuary of [[shiloh]] under [[eli]]. The opening chapters of [[1-samuel]] establish him as Hannah's miracle child, dedicated to [[yhwh]] before birth (1 Sam 1-2). The night-call vision in the temple (1 Sam 3) is the canonical inauguration of his prophetic office.

His career pivots on the institution of Israelite monarchy. The elders demand a king "like all the nations" (1 Sam 8) — a passage the Deuteronomistic historian treats with deep ambivalence, framing it as rejection of [[yhwh]]'s direct rule. Samuel anoints [[saul]] reluctantly, then rejects him for cultic disobedience at Amalek (1 Sam 15), and finally secretly anoints [[david]] at Bethlehem (1 Sam 16). The pattern fixes a key Deuteronomistic theme: kings serve under prophetic oversight.

Source-critical scholarship has long detected pro-monarchic and anti-monarchic strands woven into the Samuel material (often labeled the "early source" vs. the Deuteronomistic frame). Two accounts of Saul's anointing, two of David's introduction to the court, and contradictory portraits of the monarchy itself sit side by side. The composite character of [[1-samuel]] and [[2-samuel]] is among the textbook cases for the so-called Deuteronomistic History.

His death is reported at 1 Sam 25:1, but the [[witch-of-endor]] episode (1 Sam 28) brings him back from the dead to pronounce final judgment on Saul — one of the canon's most striking necromancy passages and a perennial puzzle for traditions that forbid such consultation.
