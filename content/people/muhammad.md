Muhammad (c. 570-632 CE) — the founder of [[islam]] and, in Muslim belief, the *Khātam al-Nabiyyīn* (Seal of the Prophets), recipient of the [[quran]] as revelation transmitted through the angel Gabriel (Jibrīl). Born in [[mecca]] into the clan of Banū Hāshim of the tribe of Quraysh; orphaned young; raised by his grandfather ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib and his uncle Abū Ṭālib; merchant by trade; married the Meccan businesswoman Khadīja bint Khuwaylid around age 25.

Muslim tradition dates the first revelation to c. 610 CE in the cave of Ḥirāʾ above Mecca; the Meccan preaching period (c. 610-622) gathered a small community under increasing pressure from the Qurayshī establishment over the new strict monotheism and the rejection of the ancestral shrine cult. The Hijra (emigration) to Yathrib — renamed [[medina]] — in 622 inaugurates the Muslim calendar and the constitution of the early *umma* (community) as a political body. The Medinan decade saw the consolidation of Muslim rule, military and diplomatic engagement with Mecca culminating in its peaceful submission in 630, and the integration of most of the Arabian peninsula into the new polity. Died at Medina in 632; succession contests immediately produced the foundational Sunni-Shīʿī division. Muhammad's life, sayings, and practice are preserved in Muslim tradition through the *sīra* (biography — Ibn Isḥāq via Ibn Hishām, al-Ṭabarī) and the [[hadith]] corpora (Bukhārī, Muslim, the four Sunan); Western historical-critical scholarship has debated the reliability and dating of these sources since the nineteenth century, with positions ranging from broadly traditional acceptance to deep skepticism (Wansbrough, the Hagarist hypothesis).
