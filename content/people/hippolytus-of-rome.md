Hippolytus of Rome (c. 170-235) — Greek-writing Roman presbyter; the most learned theologian of the early third-century Roman church and a contested figure who emerges in the modern sources as both saint-martyr and the first antipope. Place of birth unknown (possibly Eastern); a hearer of [[irenaeus-of-lyon]] in tradition (Photius); active at Rome under bishops Victor, Zephyrinus, Callistus, Urban, and Pontian. Broke with Callistus (217) over the bishop's allegedly lax penitential discipline and modalist-leaning Christology, setting himself up as rival bishop — the schism healed in 235 when both he and Pontian were exiled by Maximinus Thrax to the Sardinian mines, where both died.

*Refutation of All Heresies* (*Philosophumena*, after 222) — ten-book heresiology arguing that Christian heresies are derivative of Greek philosophical schools and pagan mystery cults; the most extensive ante-Nicene source for second-century Gnostic systems (Valentinians, Basilideans, Naassenes, Sethians) and for the early modalists (Noetus, Praxeas, Sabellius). The Greek text was rediscovered on Athos in 1842. The *Apostolic Tradition* — once confidently ascribed to him and used to reconstruct early Roman liturgy and ordination prayers (the source of the modern eucharistic prayer II of the *Novus Ordo*) — is now widely regarded as a composite Church Order text whose attribution to Hippolytus is unsafe. The marble statue discovered in 1551, long taken to be Hippolytus, may originally have depicted a female personification. Venerated as martyr in both East and West.
