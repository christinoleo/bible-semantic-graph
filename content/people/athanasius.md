Athanasius of Alexandria (~296-373) — patriarch of Alexandria from 328; the foremost defender of Nicene orthodoxy against the Arians ([[arius]] and successors) for the better part of half a century. Attended [[nicaea-i]] (325) as a young deacon and secretary to bishop Alexander; succeeded Alexander three years later and held the see until death, with five exiles totaling about seventeen years away — Trier, Rome, the Egyptian desert (twice), and Italy again — under Constantine, Constantius II, Julian, and Valens. "Athanasius contra mundum."

On the Incarnation — the early double work (paired with Contra Gentes), composed before the Arian crisis broke; argues the necessity of the incarnation for human deification ("he became man that we might become god," De inc. 54), the cosmic and soteriological logic by which only the divine Logos could re-create what humanity had unmade. The dictum encapsulates the soteriological argument behind his lifelong defense of homoousios: a creature cannot deify creatures.

Against the Arians (three orations, with a fourth probably pseudonymous) is the polemical heavy artillery — exegetical demolition of the Arian proof-texts (Prov 8:22, John 14:28, Heb 1:4, etc.). The Letter to Serapion extends the case to the divinity of the Holy Spirit, against the Pneumatomachoi ("Spirit-fighters") — preparing the ground for [[constantinople-i]].

Life of Antony — hagiography of [[antony-of-egypt]] (forward ref) that decisively popularized monasticism across the Mediterranean; read by [[augustine-of-hippo]] at his conversion (Confessions 8). The 39th Festal Letter (367) lists the 27 books of the New Testament — the earliest extant list matching the later canonical New Testament exactly. Influence on Cappadocians ([[basil-the-great]], [[gregory-of-nazianzus]], [[gregory-of-nyssa]]) and on the trajectory toward [[constantinople-i]] is foundational.
