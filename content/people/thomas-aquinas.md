Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274) — Dominican friar; the central scholastic theologian of the medieval Latin West and the canonical philosophical reference of post-Tridentine Catholicism. Born at Roccasecca to a noble Lombard family in the Kingdom of Sicily; educated at the Benedictine Monte Cassino as an oblate and at Naples where he encountered Aristotle and the Dominicans against his family's wishes (they reportedly imprisoned him for a year to dissuade him). Joined the Dominicans c. 1244; studied at Paris and Cologne under Albert the Great.

Summa Theologiae (1265-1273, unfinished) — the great synthesis in three parts (with a posthumous Supplementum). Prima Pars: God, creation, the angels, humanity in nature; Prima Secundae and Secunda Secundae: moral theology of the human end and the virtues; Tertia Pars: Christ, sacraments. Article structure (objections, sed contra, respondeo, replies) makes it a treatise and a teaching machine simultaneously. The "Five Ways" (ST I q.2 a.3) — five cosmological arguments for God's existence from motion, efficient causality, contingency, gradation, and teleology — repackaging arguments from Aristotle, [[john-of-damascus]], and Maimonides.

Summa contra Gentiles (1259-1265) — earlier and more apologetic in design, articulating natural theology for an imagined Muslim or pagan interlocutor where Scripture cannot be presupposed. The Catena Aurea is a gospel commentary woven entirely from patristic excerpts, exhibiting his vast patristic reading. Disputed Questions (De veritate, De potentia, De anima, De malo, etc.) are the live-classroom debate transcripts behind the polished Summa articles.

Theological method: a self-conscious Aristotelian synthesis — substance and accident, hylomorphism, the four causes, the cardinal virtues — integrated with Augustinian-Dionysian theology and the apophatic reserve that "we do not know what God is, but rather what he is not" (SCG I.30). Distinctive theses: real distinction of essence and existence in creatures; analogy of being; transubstantiation as the metaphysical analysis of the eucharistic change; original sin understood largely in privation terms; predestination grounded in pure divine causality with secondary contingent causes preserved. Set out on the journey to [[lyons-ii]] (1274) but died en route at Fossanova. Canonized 1323; declared Doctor Communis (1567); Leo XIII's Aeterni Patris (1879) mandated his philosophy as the standard of Catholic seminaries.
