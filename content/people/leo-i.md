Leo I the Great (~400-461) — pope from 440 to 461; the architect of mature papal Petrine theology and the doctrinal voice behind [[chalcedon]]. Roman by birth or upbringing, deacon under Celestine I and Sixtus III before election as bishop of Rome while absent on a diplomatic mission to Gaul.

Tome of Leo (Tomus ad Flavianum, 449) — letter to Patriarch Flavian of Constantinople refuting Eutyches's monophysite Christology. Christ is one person in two natures, each preserving its proper operation: "agit utraque forma cum alterius communione quod proprium est." Read aloud at [[chalcedon]] (451), the assembled bishops acclaimed: "Peter has spoken through Leo." Together with [[cyril-of-alexandria]]'s letters, the Tome forms the textual base of Chalcedonian Christology — though the Miaphysite churches read Leo's "two natures" as crypto-Nestorian and rejected the council on that basis.

Sermons (96 surviving, on the liturgical year) and Letters (173) develop a systematic theology of Petrine primacy: the bishop of Rome as legitimate successor to [[peter]], who continues to exercise his apostolic care through Leo as ministerial vicar. The framework would be drawn out further across the medieval papacy. Concrete administrative reach: Leo intervened in episcopal disputes in Gaul, Spain, Illyricum, and Africa; pursued Manichaeans, Priscillianists, and Pelagians within his Italian sphere; received the so-called Petrine privilege from Valentinian III's edict of 445.

Two famous encounters with barbarian leaders. Met Attila the Hun at the river Mincio (452) and reportedly persuaded him to spare Rome — historians read this as theatrical accompaniment to a withdrawal already determined by plague and supply lines, but the legend grew accordingly. Could not similarly avert Genseric's Vandal sack of 451 — 455, securing only protection from massacre and burning. Declared Doctor of the Church by Benedict XIV (1754), one of only two pontiffs called "the Great" by uniform Latin tradition (with [[gregory-the-great]]).
