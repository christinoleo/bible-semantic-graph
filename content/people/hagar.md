Hagar, Egyptian slave (`šipḥâ`) of [[sarah]] (Gen 16:1). Given to [[abraham]] as a surrogate when Sarah remains barren; conceives [[ishmael]]. Tensions escalate — Hagar despises Sarah, Sarah afflicts Hagar — and Hagar flees to the wilderness.

There the `malʾak yhwh` ("angel of YHWH") meets her by a spring on the way to Shur (Gen 16:7-13). She receives the first individualized annunciation in the Bible — promise of innumerable descendants, naming of the unborn [[ishmael]] — and is the first and only person in Scripture to name God: `ʾēl rŏʾî`, "the God who sees me." Returns and bears Ishmael.

Cast out a second time after [[isaac]]'s weaning (Gen 21:8-21), at Sarah's demand and with God's grim approval. Wanders Beersheba; water gone, places the child under a bush and walks away to not watch him die. Again the divine messenger — this time `ʾĕlōhîm` (E source markers throughout Gen 21) — opens her eyes to a well, and reaffirms Ishmael's destiny. She settles in the Wilderness of Paran and finds him an Egyptian wife.

Reception is heavy. [[paul]]'s allegory in [[galatians]] 4:21-31 reads Hagar as Sinai / the present Jerusalem / slavery, opposite Sarah as the free Jerusalem above — an explosive supersessionist move, contested in modern Pauline scholarship. Islamic tradition (extra-Quranic but central) makes Hājar and Ismāʿīl the founders of Mecca; the saʿy between Safa and Marwa during ḥajj re-enacts her search for water. Womanist and feminist biblical theology (notably Phyllis Trible, Delores Williams) has reclaimed Hagar as the paradigmatic enslaved, sexually exploited, foreign-Black mother whom God meets in the wilderness — a counterweight to the Sarah-centric reading of the patriarchal narratives.
