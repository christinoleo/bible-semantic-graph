Esther (Hebrew name Hadassah, "myrtle") is the Jewish orphan raised by her cousin [[mordecai]] in Persian Susa who, through a beauty contest, becomes queen to Ahasuerus (typically identified with Xerxes I, 486-465 BCE) and uses her position to thwart [[haman]]'s genocidal decree against the Jewish population of the empire. The [[book-of-esther]] is the etiology of the festival of Purim and one of two canonical books named for a woman (the other is [[book-of-ruth]]).

The book is famously the only canonical book that does not name God. No prayer, no divine voice, no miracle, no Temple, no Torah reference. Providence operates entirely behind the human action — coincidences (the king's insomnia, the chronicles read at the right moment, Haman entering the court at the wrong moment) accumulate to such density that the absence of explicit divine reference reads as deliberate. Rabbinic tradition reads the book as the canonical demonstration of hester panim — the "hiding of God's face" — in the diaspora.

The narrative is closer to court tale or romance than to historical chronicle, with markers of literary construction (the symmetry of Mordecai displacing Haman point-for-point, the doubled banquets, the precise reversal of the date of doom into the date of deliverance). Critical scholarship treats it as a 4th-3rd c. BCE diaspora novella, with no extra-biblical attestation of an Esther / Hadassah at the Persian court (Xerxes I's queen is known to be Amestris). The historicity question is mostly orthogonal to the book's theological function.

The pivot is Esth 4:14 — Mordecai's challenge to Esther to act despite the risk: "if you keep silent at this time, relief and deliverance will rise for the Jews from another place, but you and your father's house will perish. And who knows whether you have not come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" The Greek versions (LXX, with the so-called Additions to Esther) supplement the Hebrew with prayers and explicit references to God, smoothing out the canonical strangeness; these are accepted as deuterocanonical in Catholic Bibles and apocryphal in Protestant ones.

The festival of Purim — costumes, noise-making at Haman's name, drinking, gift-giving, the Megillah reading — is etiologically established in Esth 9 and remains one of the most distinctively diaspora-flavored Jewish holidays. The book's reception by Christian tradition has been more ambivalent; Luther famously wished it did not exist.
