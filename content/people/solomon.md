Solomon is the second son of [[david]] and [[bathsheba]], the third and last king of the united monarchy (traditionally c. 970-931 BCE). His succession over [[adonijah]] is brokered by [[bathsheba]] and [[nathan-prophet]] at David's deathbed (1 Kings 1) and consolidated by a wave of strategic executions early in his reign (1 Kings 2).

The reign is presented in [[1-kings]] 3-10 in mostly idealizing terms — the dream at Gibeon where he asks for wisdom over wealth (1 Kings 3), the wisdom display in the judgment of the two mothers, the building of the [[temple]] over seven years (1 Kings 6), administrative reorganization into twelve districts cross-cutting tribal boundaries, lavish trade with Hiram of Tyre and the queen of Sheba, and proverbial wisdom (3,000 proverbs, 1,005 songs — 1 Kings 4:32). The [[2-chronicles]] account is even more flattering, dropping the negative material almost entirely.

1 Kings 11 reverses the portrait sharply. Solomon's hundreds of foreign wives lead him into syncretistic worship — Ashtoreth, Milcom, Chemosh, Molech. The prophet Ahijah tears his cloak into twelve pieces, signaling the coming division of the kingdom under [[rehoboam]]. The Deuteronomistic editor reads the schism as direct judicial consequence of Solomon's apostasy.

Traditional authorship attributes three canonical books to him: [[proverbs]], [[ecclesiastes]], and [[song-of-songs]] (plus Wisdom of Solomon in deuterocanonical literature). Critical scholarship treats all of these as later compositions ascribed pseudepigraphically to Solomon's reputation for wisdom and erotic poetry. The Solomonic temple is archaeologically unrecoverable in any clear way; minimalist historians question the scale of the empire described in 1 Kings, while traditional historians defend a more substantial 10th-c. polity centered on [[jerusalem]].
