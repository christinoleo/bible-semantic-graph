Haman son of Hammedatha the Agagite is the Persian vizier of Ahasuerus and the antagonist of the [[book-of-esther]], whose plot to exterminate the Jews of the empire is the book's central threat. His "Agagite" designation links him genealogically to king Agag of the Amalekites, whom [[saul]] failed to destroy in [[1-samuel]] 15 — and thus to Amalek, the archetypal enemy of Israel since [[exodus]] 17 ("YHWH will have war with Amalek from generation to generation"). The Hebrew text builds the book's conflict explicitly into this longest-running enmity in the canon.

[[mordecai]]'s refusal to bow at the king's gate (Esth 3) precipitates the plot. Haman, "filled with fury," does not deign to act against Mordecai alone; he sets out to destroy "all the Jews, the people of Mordecai, throughout the whole kingdom" — a project he frames to the king as profitable revenue-generating policy against a separatist people whose laws differ from those of every other people in the empire (Esth 3:8-9). He casts lots (*pur* — the etymology of Purim) to determine the date.

The narrative structure of the book is the systematic, point-by-point reversal of Haman's expectations. He builds a 50-cubit gallows for [[mordecai]] (Esth 5:14) and is hanged on it himself (Esth 7:10). He plans honors for himself and is forced to lead [[mordecai]] in those honors through Susa (Esth 6). He intends to come to [[esther]]'s banquet to ratify his coup against her people; he comes to be denounced as enemy of the queen herself (Esth 7). His estate is given to Esther; his ten sons are killed by the Jews in self-defense and posthumously hanged (Esth 9). The reversal is so total that the book's structural symmetry is one of its defining features.

Reception in Jewish tradition has been intense and ritualized. The Purim observance includes noise-making at every mention of his name during the Megillah reading — a deliberate effacement of memory matched by Esther's request that his ten sons' names be written in unusual column form in the scroll. The Christian reception is thinner (Esther itself is canonically marginal in much Christian tradition), though Haman has long served as a typological figure for various enemies of God's people.
