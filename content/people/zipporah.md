Zipporah (`Ṣippōrâ`, "bird"), Midianite daughter of [[jethro]], wife of [[moses]]. Given to Moses after he defends her and her sisters from rival shepherds at the well (Ex 2:16-22) — the third betrothal-at-a-well scene in the Torah ([[rebekah]], [[rachel]], Zipporah). Bears Gershom (`gēr šām`, "I have been a sojourner there") and Eliezer ("my God is help") (Ex 18:3-4).

The Bridegroom of Blood (Ex 4:24-26). On the way back to Egypt: `wayĕhî baddērek bammālôn wayyipgĕšēhû YHWH wayĕbaqqēš hămîtô` — "on the way, at a lodging place, YHWH met him and sought to put him to death." The pronouns are ambiguous: him = Moses? Gershom? Zipporah acts immediately: takes a flint, circumcises her son, touches "his feet" (likely euphemism for genitals — Moses' or the son's?), declares `kî ḥătan-dāmîm ʾattâ lî` ("you are a bridegroom of blood to me"). YHWH releases him.

The passage is among the densest text-critical and exegetical cruxes in the Pentateuch. Three verses, multiple ambiguous pronouns, an unexplained divine attack on the prophet just commissioned, a Midianite woman performing an emergency circumcision, an obscure ritual idiom. Proposed readings include: apotropaic rite preserving Moses' life, polemic against uncircumcised Israelite practice, fragment of an older Moses-tradition incompatible with the larger framework, etiology of childhood circumcision, an early-source moment of family-Yahwism. No reading commands consensus.

Sent back to Jethro with the boys ("after he had sent her away," Ex 18:2 — suggesting a separation, possibly tied to the Ex 4 episode). Rejoins Moses at the wilderness camp with her father (Ex 18:5-6). Disappears from the narrative after this; not mentioned at Moses' death.

The "Cushite woman" of Num 12:1 is sometimes identified with Zipporah (Midianites bordering Cushan, Hab 3:7) and sometimes read as a second wife. The Ethiopian-Jewish (Beta Israel) tradition develops the latter reading. Modern feminist scholarship (Ilana Pardes, Pamela Tamarkin Reis) emphasizes Zipporah's quick-decisive action at the lodging place as a rare moment of female ritual authority in the Mosaic narrative.
