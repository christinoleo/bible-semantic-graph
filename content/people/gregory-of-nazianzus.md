Gregory of Nazianzus (~329-390) — Cappadocian Father; sole patristic figure to bear the epithet "the Theologian" alongside [[john-apostle]] and Symeon the New Theologian in Byzantine usage. Friend of [[basil-the-great]] from student days in Athens; son of Gregory the Elder, bishop of Nazianzus. Reluctant ordained presbyter, reluctant bishop of the dust-bowl see of Sasima (a manipulation by Basil that bruised the friendship), reluctant patriarch of Constantinople.

Summoned to Constantinople in 379 to lead the small Nicene community in a city dominated by Arian (Homoian) clergy. The Anastasia chapel — his rented house turned house-church — became the rallying point of Nicene revival. Delivered the Five Theological Orations (Or. 27-31) in 380 — concise dogmatic masterpieces on apophatic restraint, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, defining the Cappadocian Trinitarian synthesis. Or. 29-30 are unsurpassed as Greek patristic exposition of Christology and Trinity in tandem; Or. 31 contains the strongest fourth-century argument for the full divinity of the Spirit alongside [[basil-the-great]]'s.

Presided briefly over [[constantinople-i]] (381) after Meletius died mid-council; resigned after Western and Egyptian delegates contested the legitimacy of his transfer from Sasima. Returned to Nazianzus, then to family estates in Arianzus, where he composed the autobiographical De vita sua (in iambic verse, ~1900 lines) and a substantial poetic corpus including doctrinal hexameters and personal lament.

Condemned the Christology of Apollinaris of Laodicea, who held that the Logos replaced the human rational soul in Christ. Gregory's reply — "what is not assumed is not healed" (Ep. 101 to Cledonius) — becomes a foundational soteriological axiom carried into [[chalcedon]] (451): full humanity must be assumed for full salvation. Liturgical and poetic influence in Byzantium unmatched among the fathers; over half a million words of his survive.
