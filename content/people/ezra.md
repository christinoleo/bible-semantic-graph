Ezra son of Seraiah is a priest-scribe in the Persian court who leads a second wave of returnees from [[babylon]] to [[jerusalem]] (traditionally 458 BCE under Artaxerxes I, though some scholars argue for 398 BCE under Artaxerxes II). He carries a royal mandate authorizing him to investigate Judah and Jerusalem "according to the law of your God which is in your hand" (Ezra 7:14) and is given substantial latitude to teach, judge, and reform the post-exilic Judean community.

His program is the consolidation of Torah-centered identity. He teaches "the law of YHWH" (Ezra 7:10), conducts a public reading of Torah in Jerusalem (Neh 8) — a foundational moment for emerging Judaism as a textual community organized around scripture rather than around the now-rebuilt-but-modest Second [[temple]]. The Levites translate or explain the reading to make it intelligible, often read as the origin point of the Aramaic Targum tradition.

The most controversial action is the mass divorce of foreign wives (Ezra 9-10). Discovering that returned Judeans — including priests and Levites — have intermarried with surrounding peoples, Ezra tears his garments, sits appalled, and orchestrates a public assembly in the rain at which mixed marriages are to be dissolved. The episode sits in deep tension with [[book-of-ruth]]'s celebration of a Moabite ancestress of [[david]] and with prophetic universalist material — and is one of the great moral cruxes of the post-exilic canon. Modern readings span sympathetic (covenant survival under demographic pressure) to deeply critical (ethnic cleansing of marriage relationships, treatment of women and children).

Ezra's later reception in rabbinic tradition is immense. He is credited with the Great Assembly, the canonical fixing of the Torah text, the introduction of the square Aramaic script, the establishment of synagogue Torah reading, and substantially the founding of post-Temple Judaism. The Talmud holds that "if the Torah had not been given through [[moses]], Ezra would have been worthy to receive it." Critical scholarship treats much of this as later projection back onto the figure; the historical kernel is more modest and the textual portrait already heavily literary.

The composite [[book-of-ezra]]-[[book-of-nehemiah]] (a single book in the original Hebrew tradition) is one of the most source-critically debated post-exilic compositions, including substantial Aramaic sections, an "Ezra Memoir" first-person source, and complex chronological relationships between Ezra's mission and [[nehemiah]]'s governorship.
