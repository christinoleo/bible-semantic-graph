Jesus of Nazareth — Galilean Jewish prophet, healer, and itinerant teacher active c. 27-30 CE. Crucified in Jerusalem under [[pontius-pilate]] during the prefecture of Tiberius. Christian confession reads the event through resurrection, incarnation, and divine sonship; the canonical [[gospel-of-matthew]], [[gospel-of-mark]], [[gospel-of-luke]], and [[gospel-of-john]] are its primary witnesses, supplemented by Pauline letters predating them ([[1-corinthians]] 15:3-8 contains the earliest creedal formula).

Genealogies in [[gospel-of-matthew]] 1 and [[gospel-of-luke]] 3 trace descent from [[david]] and [[abraham]] (Matthew) or back to [[adam]] (Luke) — the two lists diverge between [[david]] and [[joseph-of-nazareth]] and have been harmonized variously (Matthew = legal/Joseph's line, Luke = biological/Mary's line; or theological-typological vs historical). Mother [[mary-mother-of-jesus]]; legal father [[joseph-of-nazareth]]; siblings include [[james-brother-of-jesus]] and [[jude-brother-of-jesus]] (Catholic and Orthodox traditions read these as cousins or stepbrothers under the perpetual-virginity doctrine).

Synoptic portrait (Mark/Matthew/Luke) emphasizes parables, kingdom-of-God proclamation, exorcisms, conflict with Pharisees and Sadducees, a Galilean ministry climaxing in a single Passover trip to Jerusalem. Johannine portrait is theologically thicker: pre-existent Logos, multiple Jerusalem visits, long discourses, fewer parables, no exorcisms. The synoptic vs. Johannine divergence anchors classical source-critical work and shapes any reconstruction of the historical figure.

The "historical Jesus" question — what the Galilean teacher said and did, behind the gospels' theological framing — generates successive scholarly quests: Schweitzer's apocalyptic prophet (1906), the Bultmannian skepticism, the second quest of Käsemann, and the third quest (Sanders, Meier, Crossan, N. T. Wright) using criteria of multiple attestation, dissimilarity, embarrassment. Independent attestation: [[john-the-baptist]] event, the crucifixion, brother [[james-brother-of-jesus]]. Tacitus and Josephus corroborate execution under Pilate; the Testimonium Flavianum is partially interpolated but contains an authentic core.

Christological development from Jewish messianic categories to Nicene homoousios runs through Pauline pre-existence language ([[philippians]] 2, [[colossians]] 1), Johannine Logos ([[gospel-of-john]] 1), and patristic controversies. Jewish and Islamic readings hold the figure as prophet without divinity; modern liberal-Protestant, evangelical, Catholic, and Orthodox readings diverge sharply on the historicity of miracles, virgin birth, and bodily resurrection. Reception history spans rabbinic polemic (Toledot Yeshu), Quranic ʿĪsā, and two millennia of Christian art, doctrine, and politics.
