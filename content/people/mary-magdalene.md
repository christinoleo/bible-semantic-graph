Mary of Magdala — fishing-village native on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee. [[gospel-of-luke]] 8:1-3 introduces her among women travelling with [[jesus]] and the Twelve, financing the mission "from their own resources," with seven demons cast out of her — the only NT detail about her background, which says nothing about prostitution.

Present at the [[crucifixion]] when most of the male disciples have fled (all four gospels). Witness to the burial. First or among the first witnesses of the resurrection in every gospel — the only individual named in all four resurrection narratives. In [[gospel-of-john]] 20:1-18 she encounters the risen [[jesus]] alone in the garden, is the first to bear the message to the disciples ("I have seen the Lord"), earning the patristic title Apostola Apostolorum, "apostle to the apostles" (Hippolytus, Augustine).

The medieval conflation that fuses her with the sinful woman of [[gospel-of-luke]] 7:36-50 and with [[mary-of-bethany]] (cementing the "repentant prostitute" image) is a Western artifact, traceable to Gregory the Great's homily of 591 CE. Eastern Orthodox tradition never made the conflation; modern Catholic scholarship has formally undone it (1969 liturgical reform). The "prostitute" Mary Magdalene of Renaissance painting and Hollywood has no scriptural footing.

Resurgent feminist and historical-critical interest reads her as a major apostolic figure whose authority was suppressed by patriarchal redaction. The non-canonical Gospel of Mary (Coptic, 2nd c.) develops a tradition of her as recipient of secret teaching from [[jesus]], in tension with [[peter]] — possibly reflecting actual conflicts in early Christian communities over female teaching authority. The Da Vinci Code's "wife of Jesus" claim is fiction, not history.
