Priscilla (Prisca in Paul's letters) — wife of [[aquila]], usually named before her husband (four of six NT occurrences), unusual for the period and generally read as marking her as the more prominent figure. With [[aquila]] expelled from Rome by Claudius (c. 49 CE) under the edict reported by Suetonius as targeting Jewish disturbances "at the instigation of Chrestus" — possibly Christ-related unrest in the synagogues. They settle in Corinth where [[paul]] meets them ([[acts]] 18:2-3); shared trade of tentmaking grounds the partnership.

Travels with [[paul]] from Corinth to Ephesus ([[acts]] 18:18-19), where they instruct Apollos — the eloquent Alexandrian who knew only [[john-the-baptist]]'s baptism — in "the way of God more accurately" ([[acts]] 18:26). The episode is a clear NT case of a woman teaching a man in matters of doctrine, in tension with later prohibitions ([[1-timothy]] 2:12); often invoked in debates over women's ordination.

Hosts house churches at Ephesus ([[1-corinthians]] 16:19) and then again at Rome ([[romans]] 16:3-5), where [[paul]] sends greetings to "Prisca and Aquila, my fellow workers in Christ Jesus, who risked their necks for my life." [[2-timothy]] 4:19 has her back in Ephesus. Final NT mention. Adolf von Harnack famously proposed her as the author of [[hebrews]] — a hypothesis defended by some modern feminist scholarship, undecidable in practice, though it elegantly explains why the letter is anonymous despite an authoritative apostolic voice.
