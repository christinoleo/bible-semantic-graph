Joel son of Pethuel is the prophet of the short [[book-of-joel]] (three chapters in Hebrew, four in Septuagint/English versification). The book gives no datable royal synchronism — almost uniquely among prophetic books — and dating proposals range from the 9th c. (early pre-exilic, on grounds of placement among the Twelve and lack of mention of king) to the post-exilic 5th-4th c. (on grounds of references to the dispersion, lack of northern kingdom material, and developed eschatology). Most critical scholarship now favors a post-exilic date.

The book's frame is a devastating locust plague (Joel 1-2) — described in escalating waves of insect destruction — that the prophet reads as harbinger of the [[day-of-yhwh]]. The call is to communal repentance through fasting and lamentation. "Rend your hearts and not your garments" (Joel 2:13) is the iconic call. Locust comparative literature has long debated whether the plague is literal, metaphorical for invading armies, or apocalyptically blurred — the imagery moves fluidly between the registers.

The second half (Joel 2:28-3:21 in English / 3-4 in Hebrew) is wholly eschatological. After the disaster comes the great outpouring: "I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh; your sons and your daughters shall prophesy" (Joel 2:28). This passage is cited at length by [[peter]] in [[acts]] 2 as the interpretive framework for Pentecost — the gift of the Spirit at the birth of the church is read as inaugural fulfillment of Joel's promise. The "valley of Jehoshaphat" judgment scene and the imagery of plowshares-into-swords (deliberate inversion of [[book-of-isaiah]] 2 / [[book-of-micah]] 4) close the book.

Joel's pluralism of citation — short book, but disproportionately quoted in NT and later Jewish liturgical traditions — is partly a function of the eschatological-pneumatological material concentrated in three chapters of unusually charged imagery.
