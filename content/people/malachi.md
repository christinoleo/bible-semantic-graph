Malachi is the last of the Twelve Minor Prophets and traditionally the last canonical prophet of the Hebrew Bible in the Christian ordering (where Malachi closes the OT). The name *malʾakhi* means "my messenger" — and a substantial minority of critical scholarship has read it as title rather than personal name (compare Mal 3:1 "behold, I send my messenger"), with the actual prophet pseudonymous or unnamed. Most scholarship treats it as a personal name; the question is unresolved.

The book is post-exilic and post-Temple-rebuild, datable to the mid-5th c. BCE on the basis of references to the rebuilt Temple in operation, a Persian-appointed governor (*pechah*), and concerns paralleling [[ezra]] and [[nehemiah]] (mixed marriages, tithing failures, cynicism about cult). The setting is the spiritual exhaustion of the second-generation returnees: the Temple is rebuilt but the promises of [[isaiah]] 40-55 about glorious restoration have not materialized.

The literary form is distinctive — six disputation oracles structured as YHWH's assertion, the people's skeptical question, and YHWH's expanded answer. "I have loved you, says YHWH. But you say, How have you loved us?" (Mal 1:2). "You have wearied YHWH with your words. But you say, How have we wearied him?" (Mal 2:17). The form captures the texture of a worn community arguing back at its God.

The indictments are concrete: priests offering blemished animals, men divorcing the wives of their youth and marrying foreign women, withholding tithes, calling the proud blessed and the evildoers prosperous. The promises that frame the disputations include "from the rising of the sun to its setting my name will be great among the nations" (Mal 1:11) — sometimes read as a pre-Christian universalist text — and the messenger of the covenant who suddenly comes to the Temple (Mal 3:1) and refines the sons of Levi like silver.

The book closes (Mal 4:5-6) with the announcement that YHWH will send [[elijah]] before the great and terrible Day of YHWH, to turn the hearts of fathers to children and children to fathers. This becomes the canonical hinge between the testaments in Christian Bibles: [[gospel-of-matthew]] 11:14, [[gospel-of-mark]] 9:11-13, and [[gospel-of-luke]] 1:17 all identify [[john-the-baptist]] as Elijah-redivivus, fulfilling Mal 4 to inaugurate the messianic age. In Jewish ordering Malachi closes the Prophets but not the Hebrew Bible (which ends with [[2-chronicles]]), and Elijah's eschatological return remains future.
