Marcion of Sinope (~85-160) — Pontic shipowner turned theological reformer; founder of a Christian movement that for over a century rivaled the proto-orthodox church across the empire. Native of Sinope on the Black Sea; arrived in Rome around 140, made a substantial donation to the Roman church, taught for several years, was excommunicated and expelled around 144 — his money was returned. Established his own ecclesial network with its own bishops, clergy, sacraments, and martyrs, which spread rapidly from Italy to Persia.

Theological architecture: a sharp metaphysical dualism between the just but harsh creator-God of the Hebrew Bible (the Demiurge — capricious, vengeful, materially embodied in flesh and law) and the wholly other Father revealed by Jesus — utterly good, previously unknown, alien to creation. Christ is the messenger of the alien God; he only seemed to take flesh (a docetic element); his mission rescues a portion of humanity from the Demiurge's law and judgment. The Hebrew Scriptures are real prophecy of the Demiurge's own messiah, not Christ — they should be read literally and rejected as scripture for Christians.

Canon: the first known Christian biblical canon, structured for purpose. The "Gospel" — a redaction of [[gospel-of-luke]] purged of perceived Judaizing material (no birth narrative, no Hebrew-Bible citations) — and the "Apostolikon" — ten Pauline letters in the same purged form (excluding the Pastorals, with [[ephesians]] under the title "to the Laodiceans"). The relationship to canonical Luke is contested: most scholars hold Marcion abbreviated Luke, but Markus Vinzent and others have argued the canonical Luke instead expanded Marcion. Either way, Marcion's canon is the catalyst — the proto-orthodox response of a fourfold gospel ([[irenaeus]]) and broader Pauline corpus is partly a polemical counter-canon.

The Antitheses — a now-lost prefatory text setting passages of the Hebrew Bible against Christ's teaching to demonstrate the incompatibility of the two deities — became the template for later contrast theologies and survives in extensive citation in [[tertullian]]'s Against Marcion. Marcionite communities survived into the fourth and fifth centuries in Syria and Mesopotamia, absorbed mostly by Manichaeism (cf. [[mani]]). Catalyst — not member — of gnostic literature: Marcion's strict bibliographic literalism and rejection of allegoresis sets him apart from Valentinian gnosticism, though [[irenaeus]] and [[justin-martyr]] grouped them together.
