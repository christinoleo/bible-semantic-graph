Manasseh is the fourteenth king of Judah (traditional dating c. 697-642 BCE), son and successor of [[hezekiah]], and at 55 years the longest reign in the divided monarchy. The [[2-kings]] account (2 Kings 21:1-18) is uniformly hostile: he rebuilds the high places [[hezekiah]] tore down, sets up altars to Baal in the Temple itself, installs an Asherah pole in the Temple, practices child sacrifice in the Valley of Hinnom, consults mediums and necromancers, and "sheds very much innocent blood" until Jerusalem is full of it. The narrator gives no positive material.

The Deuteronomistic theology of Kings treats Manasseh's sins as the proximate cause of the eventual Babylonian destruction. Even [[josiah]]'s thoroughgoing reform two reigns later cannot defer the judgment: "still YHWH did not turn from the fierceness of his great wrath, by which his anger was kindled against Judah, because of all the provocations with which Manasseh had provoked him" (2 Kings 23:26). The fall of [[jerusalem]] in 586 BCE is read as the cashing-out of Manasseh's account.

The [[2-chronicles]] account (2 Chr 33:1-20) is strikingly different. The same opening catalogue of sins is followed by an unrecorded-in-Kings episode where Manasseh is taken in chains to Babylon by the Assyrians, repents abjectly, is restored to Jerusalem, and undertakes a partial reform. This narrative addition has long been read either as the Chronicler's reconciliation of the long reign (theologically anomalous for so wicked a king) with the doctrine of retribution, or as preservation of a genuine tradition not transmitted in Kings. The deuterocanonical *Prayer of Manasseh* is a later composition modeled on this Chronicler material.

Extra-biblical evidence places him as a loyal Assyrian vassal under Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal, listed among the kings contributing materials to royal building projects. The syncretism the biblical narrators denounce was likely partly imperial accommodation to Assyrian astral cult.
