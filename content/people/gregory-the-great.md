Gregory the Great (~540-604) — pope from 590 to 604; the last of the four traditional Latin Doctors (with [[ambrose]], [[jerome]], [[augustine-of-hippo]]) and a hinge figure between patristic and medieval Christianity. Son of a senatorial family; urban prefect of Rome around 573 before retiring to monastic life in a household monastery on the Caelian Hill; sent as apocrisiarius (papal envoy) to Constantinople 579-585. Reluctant pope after election by acclamation amid the Lombard wars, plague, and the collapse of imperial logistics in Italy.

Pastoral Rule (Regula pastoralis) — the manual of episcopal practice that became the standard handbook of medieval ministry; translated into Anglo-Saxon under Alfred the Great. Moralia on Job (35 books) — vast allegorical commentary expounding the literal, moral, and mystical senses; foundational text for medieval mystical and contemplative literature; the seven deadly sins as a recognized scheme emerge in identifiable form here.

Dispatched the Gregorian mission under [[augustine-of-canterbury]] (596) to evangelize the Anglo-Saxons — durable success despite later setbacks. The famous anecdote about Anglo slave-children in the Roman market ("non Angli sed angeli") is Bede's; Gregory himself records the missionary policy in letters that strikingly advise [[augustine-of-canterbury]] to repurpose pagan shrines rather than destroy them. Negotiated independently with the Lombard king Agilulf for the safety of Rome when the Byzantine exarch failed to act — practical precursor to the medieval Papal States.

Liturgical and musical influence is partly real, partly retrojected: the Gregorian Sacramentary is a later compilation drawing on his materials; "Gregorian chant" was systematized in Carolingian times and named for him post hoc. Dialogues — four books on the lives and miracles of Italian saints including the only ancient life of [[benedict-of-nursia]] (forward ref) — has had its authenticity questioned by some scholars (Francis Clark) though most accept it as Gregorian. Styled himself servus servorum Dei, the title every subsequent pope has borne.
