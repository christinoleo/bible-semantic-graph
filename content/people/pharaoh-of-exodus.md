The Pharaoh of the Exodus, the unnamed king of [[egypt]] who opposes [[moses]] and refuses to release Israel. The Hebrew Bible never names him — `parʿōh` is a title (Egyptian `prʿ ʾȝ`, "great house"), not a personal name, and the omission is deliberate: the canon's preferred protagonist is YHWH, and Pharaoh is reduced to the function of opposing power.

Historical identification is contested and uncertain. The two leading candidates correspond to two competing chronologies:

- **Early date / "early Exodus" (c. 1446 BCE):** based on 1 Kgs 6:1 (480 years before Solomon's fourth year). Pharaoh = Thutmose III or Amenhotep II (18th Dynasty).
- **Late date / "late Exodus" (c. 1260-1230 BCE):** based on Ex 1:11 mentioning the store-cities Pithom and Raamses, the Merneptah Stele (c. 1207 BCE) naming "Israel" already in Canaan, and broader archaeological dating. Pharaoh = Ramesses II (19th Dynasty), with Merneptah as the pharaoh of the early conquest period.

The late date is the majority scholarly position; the early date persists in conservative biblical chronology. A third minority view denies any historical Exodus of the scale Exodus describes, treating it as foundational mythography. The patron deity, religious context, and political environment of Ramesses II's New Kingdom give the late date strong contextual fit but no direct extrabiblical attestation of the Exodus event itself.

The "hardened heart" motif. Ex 4-14 distributes the action across three formulae: YHWH hardens (`ḥāzaq`/`qāšâ`) Pharaoh's heart; Pharaoh hardens his own heart; Pharaoh's heart "was hardened" (passive/middle). The distribution is studied — Pharaoh hardens his own through the first five plagues, then YHWH takes over. The literary and theological effect is to hold human responsibility and divine sovereignty in deliberate tension without resolving them. [[paul]] in [[romans]] 9:17-18 quotes Ex 9:16 — "for this very purpose I have raised you up, to show my power in you" — as Exhibit C (with [[rebekah]] and [[esau]]) for unconditional divine election: "he has mercy on whomever he wills, and he hardens whomever he wills."

Drowns in the Sea (Ex 14:28 — "not one of them remained"). Whether Pharaoh personally is intended as drowned is a long-standing debate; Ex 15:4 ("Pharaoh's chariots and his host he cast into the sea") is ambiguous, and the narrative neither names a successor nor reports Egyptian aftermath.
