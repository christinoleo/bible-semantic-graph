C. S. Lewis (1898-1963) — Northern Irish-born medievalist and literary critic at Oxford (Magdalen) and Cambridge (Magdalene); Anglican lay apologist whose popular writings carried [[christian-apologetic]] into mid-twentieth-century mass culture more widely than any contemporary academic theology. Convert from adolescent atheism by stages through his thirties under the prodding of [[j-r-r-tolkien]] and Hugo Dyson; member of the Inklings.

Mere Christianity (1952) — assembled from BBC radio broadcasts delivered 1941-1944 during the Blitz — argues from a universal moral law to a moral lawgiver and proceeds through a layperson's defense of credal Christianity; the so-called trilemma (Jesus as liar, lunatic, or Lord) became its most-cited move. The Problem of Pain (1940) and the late, raw A Grief Observed (1961) treat theodicy as a Christian on both sides of the bereavement. Miracles (1947) defends the possibility of supernatural intervention against naturalist metaphysics, including the argument from reason that Elizabeth Anscombe famously contested in 1948. Lewis's apologetic blends literary, moral, and philosophical appeals rather than evidentialist or presuppositional rigor in the strict sense; his influence runs through evangelicalism, mainline Anglicanism, and Catholic readership alike, and through the Narnia and Space Trilogy fictions as much as through the explicit apologetics.
