Elisha son of Shaphat is the disciple and successor of [[elijah]], operating across the reigns of four kings of Israel (Joram, [[jehu]], Jehoahaz, Jehoash) — c. 850s through early 8th c. BCE. He is called from plowing twelve yoke of oxen in 1 Kings 19, and he formally assumes Elijah's mantle (literally) at the Jordan in 2 Kings 2, having requested and received a "double portion" of his master's spirit.

The Elisha cycle (2 Kings 2-13) is denser in miracles than the Elijah cycle: purifying the spring at Jericho, the multiplication of the widow's oil, raising the Shunammite woman's son, healing [[naaman]]'s [[tzaraat]], making an axe head float, blinding the Aramean army and leading them captive into Samaria, multiplying twenty loaves to feed a hundred men, predicting the lifting of Samaria's siege through a vision of Aramean panic. The pattern is concrete, often local, often involving women and outsiders — closer to folklore in register than the elevated theophany of his master.

The political role is real but episodic. He sponsors [[jehu]]'s anointing through a young prophet at Ramoth-Gilead (2 Kings 9), initiating the bloody Omride purge. He warns the king of Israel repeatedly of Aramean troop movements. At his deathbed Joash king of Israel weeps over him with Elijah's own departing line — "my father, my father, the chariots of Israel and its horsemen" (2 Kings 13:14) — and Elisha enacts a final prophetic sign with arrows that he tepidly fulfills.

The two most awkward passages are the cursing of forty-two children for mocking his baldness (eaten by two she-bears — 2 Kings 2:23-25), a perennial homiletic embarrassment, and the post-mortem resurrection where a corpse touching Elisha's bones revives (2 Kings 13:21). The miracle-worker register persists past his death. Comparative scholarship has noted significant overlap between the Elijah-Elisha miracle stories and the gospel miracle traditions, particularly in [[gospel-of-luke]].
