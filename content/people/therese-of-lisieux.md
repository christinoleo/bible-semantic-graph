Thérèse of Lisieux (1873-1897) — French Carmelite nun whose brief and apparently obscure life produced one of the most widely circulated spiritual texts of the modern era. Born Marie Françoise-Thérèse Martin at Alençon to a watchmaker father and lacemaker mother — both since canonized themselves (Louis and Zélie Martin, 2015) — the youngest of nine, four of whom died in infancy. Mother died of breast cancer when Thérèse was four; the family moved to Lisieux; raised by elder sisters and aunt. Years of psychosomatic illness and intense scrupulosity through childhood resolved at what she called the "Christmas conversion" of 1886 (age thirteen).

Entered the Carmelite convent of Lisieux at fifteen by special dispensation — three of her sisters were already there or would join — after directly petitioning Pope Leo XIII during a pilgrimage to Rome in 1887. Eight and a half years of cloistered life characterized externally by ordinariness: small obediences, monastic routine, no visions, no extraordinary phenomena. The hidden interior development crystallized in her doctrine of the "Little Way" (la petite voie): sanctification through the smallest acts done with great love, spiritual childhood as utter trust in divine mercy rather than ascetic athleticism, the offering of self to merciful love rather than to divine justice (her Act of Offering, 9 June 1895).

Story of a Soul (Histoire d'une âme) — three autobiographical manuscripts (A, B, C) written at the request of her sister-prioress in 1895-1897 and published posthumously in 1898 in a heavily edited version. The critical edition restoring her original text appeared only in 1956; widely reread in light of it. Diagnosed with pulmonary tuberculosis in 1896; her final eighteen months are documented in the Derniers Entretiens, the Yellow Notebook of conversations recorded by her sister Pauline. Underwent during this period a profound "trial of faith" — described as a darkness in which religious certainty entirely receded — that has been read alongside [[john-of-the-cross]]'s dark night of the spirit and as anticipating modern Christian engagement with doubt (Mother Teresa, Karl Rahner).

Died at twenty-four on 30 September 1897. Canonized 1925 — exceptionally rapid; named patroness of the missions in 1927 alongside [[francis-xavier]] without ever having left the cloister; co-patroness of France 1944 alongside [[joan-of-arc]] (forward ref); declared Doctor of the Church 1997 by John Paul II — the third woman and the youngest figure to be so honored. Her doctrinal contribution: the demotic accessibility of holiness through trust, the relativization of "great works" before the inner posture of love, the foregrounding of mercy in modern Catholic spirituality.
