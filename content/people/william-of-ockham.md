William of Ockham (c. 1287-1347) — Franciscan friar; the *Venerabilis Inceptor* (Venerable Beginner); the most consequential nominalist of the late medieval schools and a watershed figure marking the breakdown of the high-scholastic synthesis. Born at Ockham in Surrey; entered the Franciscans young; studied at [[oxford]] but never completed the regency (whence "Inceptor"), his Sentences commentary having been delated to the papal curia at Avignon in 1324 on suspicion of heresy. Four years of investigation under [[john-xxii]] ended in flight (1328) with Michael of Cesena and other Spiritual Franciscans to the protection of [[louis-iv]] of Bavaria at Munich, where he died in the 1347-1348 plague.

The principle of parsimony — *pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate*, "Ockham's razor" — is not exclusive to him but is most rigorously applied by him to scholastic ontology. The targets: universals as real entities (Ockham reduces them to mental signs — *terminism*), distinct formalities in God and creatures (he rejects [[duns-scotus]]'s formal distinction as multiplying without need), and metaphysical apparatus invoked to ground supposedly necessary truths about contingent beings. *Summa Logicae* is the major systematic treatment of his logic and theory of signs; the *Sentences* commentary (Ordinatio) the major theological statement. Voluntarist on the divine will (following [[duns-scotus]] but radicalizing) — God could in principle have ordained any moral order; the actual moral order rests on the *potentia ordinata* but not on intrinsic necessity. The political writings of the Munich years (*Opus nonaginta dierum*, *Dialogus*) defend Franciscan apostolic poverty against [[john-xxii]] and develop an early theory of papal fallibility and limited temporal authority. His [[scholastic]] inheritance — *via moderna* — dominated late-medieval university theology; [[martin-luther]] was trained in it before turning against it, and the dependency runs deep in early Reformation soteriology.
