Al-Ashari (874-936) — Arab theologian; founder of the [[ashariyya]], the dominant school of Sunni [[classical-kalam]] across the medieval Shāfiʿī and Mālikī worlds and, through [[al-ghazali]] and [[fakhr-al-din-al-razi]], the central transmitter of philosophical theology in mainstream Sunnism. Born and trained at [[basra]] under the Muʿtazilī master al-Jubbāʾī, whose stepson he was; by his own later account he experienced a forty-day crisis in his fortieth year and renounced [[mutazila]] doctrine publicly from the pulpit of the Basra congregational mosque; spent the remainder of his career at [[baghdad]] defending traditionalist positions with the dialectical tools he had inherited from his former teachers.

*Kitāb al-Ibāna ʿan Uṣūl al-Diyāna* (The Clarification of the Foundations of Religion) and *Kitāb al-Lumaʿ* (The Sparks) — programmatic statements of the school's distinctive positions: the divine attributes are real and distinct from the essence yet not separable from it (against Muʿtazilī negation), the Qurʾān is uncreated speech (against the Muʿtazilī doctrine of *khalq al-Qurʾān* and the *miḥna* it produced), human acts are created by God and *acquired* (*kasb*) by the agent, and the moral value of acts is fixed by revelation rather than discovered by reason. *Maqālāt al-Islāmiyyīn* — heresiographical survey of early Islamic sects, the principal source for Muʿtazilī and Khārijī doctrine. [[ibn-taymiyya]] argues that the school departed from genuine *salaf* commitments by retaining the dialectical apparatus of the Muʿtazila even while reversing their conclusions.
