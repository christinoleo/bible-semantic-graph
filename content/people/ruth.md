Ruth is a Moabitess widow whose pledge of loyalty to her mother-in-law [[naomi]] — "where you go, I will go; your people shall be my people, and your God my God" (Ruth 1:16) — has become a paradigmatic conversion text. The [[book-of-ruth]] traces her gleaning in the fields of [[boaz]], her midnight encounter at the threshing floor, and her marriage to him under levirate-adjacent custom.

Her son Obed becomes grandfather of [[david]], placing a Moabite woman in Israel's royal line. The genealogy is the structural payoff of the book (Ruth 4:18-22) and is picked up explicitly in the [[gospel-of-matthew]] genealogy of [[jesus]] (Matt 1:5), one of four women named there alongside Tamar, [[rahab]], and [[bathsheba]].

Critical dating is contested. Traditional placement is in the period of the [[judges]] ("in the days when the judges judged" — Ruth 1:1). Source-critical scholarship has often dated the book late (Persian or even Hellenistic period) and read it as a polemic against [[ezra]] and [[nehemiah]]'s mixed-marriage reforms — a foreign woman lawfully grafted into Israel and producing its greatest king. Linguistic arguments cut both ways; recent studies have pushed back toward earlier dates.

The book is one of two in the Hebrew canon named for a woman ([[book-of-esther]] is the other) and is read liturgically at Shavuot in Jewish practice. Theological reception emphasizes [[chesed]] (covenant loyalty) as the book's defining virtue, embodied serially by Ruth, [[naomi]], and [[boaz]].
