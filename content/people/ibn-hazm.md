Ibn Hazm (994-1064) — Andalusian polymath, Ẓāhirī jurist, theologian, philosopher, and poet; the most systematic early Muslim critic of [[judaism]] and [[christianity]] as religions of revealed scripture. Born into a high administrative family of Umayyad Córdoba; lived through the collapse of the caliphate and the *fitna* of the Taifa kingdoms; held brief ministerial office before retiring to scholarship under increasing political marginalization. Adopted Ẓāhirism (strict literalism in jurisprudence, rejecting analogical reasoning and juristic preference) and made the school his polemical instrument.

*Kitāb al-Fiṣal fī al-Milal wa-l-Ahwāʾ wa-l-Niḥal* (The Decisive Word on Religions, Sects, and Schools) — five-volume comparative critique of religions, including the earliest extant detailed Muslim catalogue of internal contradictions, chronological impossibilities, and theological problems in the Hebrew Bible and the [[new-testament]]. Establishes the textual-critique register that becomes the durable signature of [[islamic-apologetic]] — *taḥrīf* (corruption / alteration of the prior scriptures) argued not as bare assertion but as the conclusion of philological and narrative analysis. Foundational reference for [[ibn-taymiyya]] and, mediated through Ottoman and modern revivalist channels, for [[rahmatullah-kairanawi]] and twentieth-century debaters like [[ahmed-deedat]]. Also authored *Ṭawq al-Ḥamāma* (The Ring of the Dove), a treatise on love that has become the most-read Arabic prose work in modern Western anthologies — an unexpected counterweight to the polemicist's reputation.
