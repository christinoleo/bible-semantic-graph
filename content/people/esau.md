Esau, twin of [[jacob]], firstborn of [[isaac]] and [[rebekah]] (Gen 25:24-26). Born red and hairy (`ʾadmônî ... śēʿār`) — the text puns on his other name Edom (`ʾĕdôm`, "red") and on Seir, the Edomite homeland (`śēʿîr`, "hairy"). Eponymous ancestor of Edom.

Sells his birthright (`bĕkōrâ`) to Jacob for a bowl of red lentil stew when starving from the hunt (Gen 25:29-34) — the narrator's "thus Esau despised his birthright" condemns him out loud. Marries Hittite women, "a bitterness of spirit" to his parents (Gen 26:34-35). Cheated of the patriarchal blessing by Jacob and Rebekah's deception (Gen 27); receives a lesser counter-blessing of sword and desert. Vows to kill Jacob.

The Peniel reconciliation (Gen 33) is among the canon's most striking scenes: Jacob, limping and terrified, sends gifts ahead; Esau runs to him, falls on his neck, kisses him, weeps. Esau refuses the gifts (Jacob insists), declines escort, and the brothers part peaceably — Esau to Seir, Jacob to Canaan. The text refuses to caricature him: he is wronged, and he forgives.

Gen 36 details his descendants — the dukes of Edom, kings reigning "before any king reigned over Israel." Edom and Israel are brother-nations across the OT, sometimes allied, more often hostile (esp. Edom's celebration of Jerusalem's fall — [[book-of-obadiah]], Ps 137:7).

Reception is harsh. Mal 1:2-3 ("Jacob I loved, Esau I hated") becomes [[paul]]'s prooftext in [[romans]] 9:13 for sovereign election. Heb 12:16-17 makes him a paradigm of the "profane" person who trades the eternal for a meal, finding "no place for repentance though he sought it with tears." Rabbinic tradition (esp. against the backdrop of Roman power, frequently identified midrashically with Edom) reads Esau-Edom as archetypal violent gentile empire. The narrative Esau — the one who actually appears in Genesis — is more sympathetic than his reception allows.
