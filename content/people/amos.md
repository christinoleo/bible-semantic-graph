Amos of Tekoa is a Judahite shepherd and dresser of sycamore figs sent north to prophesy at the royal sanctuary of Bethel during the reign of Jeroboam II of Israel (mid-8th c. BCE, traditional dating c. 760-750 BCE). He is the earliest of the literary prophets — his book is widely held to preserve the oldest substantial prophetic preaching collection in the canon — and his biographical occupation matters: he is an outsider in two registers (Judahite at northern Bethel, lay person against the priestly establishment).

The opening (Amos 1-2) is the textbook example of prophetic indictment-by-stages. Oracles "for three transgressions of X, and for four" sweep across surrounding nations (Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, Moab) — winning his audience's agreement — before pivoting unexpectedly to Judah, and then most extensively to Israel itself, whose sins are listed in greater specificity and at greater length than any of the rhetorical foils.

The economic and social-justice material is the most pointed in the canon. "They sell the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals — those who trample the head of the poor into the dust of the earth" (Amos 2:6-7). "Hear this word, you cows of Bashan who are on the mountain of Samaria, who oppress the poor, who crush the needy" (Amos 4:1). The condemnation of luxury — ivory beds, festivals, fatted calves consumed by elites while justice is denied — gives Amos his enduring status in liberation theology and prophetic ethics. "Let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24) is the iconic refrain, cited centrally by Martin Luther King Jr.

The confrontation with Amaziah the priest at Bethel (Amos 7:10-17), who orders him to flee back to Judah and earn his bread there, includes Amos's famous denial of professional-prophet status ("I am no prophet, nor a prophet's son… I am a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore figs"). The book closes (Amos 9:11-15) with restoration material — the raising up of "the fallen booth of David" — widely considered a later editorial addition by source-critical scholarship, since it sits oddly with the relentless judgment of the preceding eight and a half chapters. James cites this passage at the Jerusalem Council in [[acts]] 15:16-17.
