Jerome (~347-420) — premier biblical scholar of the Latin patristic era; translator and reviser of the Latin Bible that became the [[vulgate]] and the standard of Western Christianity until the Reformation. Born in Stridon (Dalmatia/Pannonia), educated in Rome under Donatus, baptized as a young man, ascetic in the Syrian desert (375-378) where he learned Hebrew from a converted Jew — virtually unique among patristic Christians in his Hebrew competence.

Returns to Rome 382 as secretary to Pope Damasus I, who commissions him to revise the Old Latin gospels against the Greek. The full Bible translation expanded from there, undertaken in Bethlehem after Damasus's death and a Roman scandal forced his retreat east (385). He insisted on translating the Old Testament from the Hebrew (hebraica veritas) against the dominant Christian practice of [[septuagint]]-based translation — a conviction [[augustine-of-hippo]] worried about by letter, fearing congregational disruption over deuterocanonical materials. Jerome held the Hebrew canon books primary and labeled the Greek additions "ecclesiastical" (read for edification, not for doctrine) — a distinction proto-Protestant Reformers later cited.

Discussed authorship of [[hebrews]] candidly: cited in Greek and Latin sources, Jerome remained openly skeptical that Paul wrote it, attributing it tentatively to [[barnabas]], [[clement-of-rome]], or [[luke]]; nonetheless accepted it as canonical. Vir scriptural-critical to the bone — but also a savagely polemical correspondent. Against Helvidius defended the perpetual virginity of [[mary]]; Against Jovinian, a treatise so extreme in praising celibacy that even sympathetic Romans found it offensive; Against Vigilantius defended relics and martyr cult.

Personal style waspish, classical (cf. the famous dream — "Ciceronianus es, non Christianus"), and pedagogically demanding. Mentored the Roman aristocratic ascetic circle around Marcella and Paula, founded a monastic community in Bethlehem with Paula and her daughter Eustochium, and produced the Letters — a literary corpus second only to [[augustine-of-hippo]]'s in influence on medieval Latin Christianity. Doctor of the Church; iconographically the lion-with-cardinal-hat figure in renaissance painting, both attributes anachronistic.
