Pontius Pilate — Roman prefect of Judaea c. 26-36 CE, under Tiberius. Title corrected from "procurator" (used by Tacitus and the gospels' later mistranslation) to "prefect" by the 1961 discovery of the Pilate Stone at Caesarea Maritima, which preserves the inscription PRAEFECTVS IVDA[EA]E. Independent attestation: Josephus (Antiquities and Jewish War), Philo (Embassy to Gaius), Tacitus (Annals 15.44 — "Christus, the founder of the name, had undergone the death penalty in the reign of Tiberius, by sentence of the procurator Pontius Pilatus").

Josephus and Philo paint a hostile portrait: a stubborn, insensitive governor who provokes Jewish protest by introducing imperial standards into Jerusalem, plundering temple funds for an aqueduct, hanging votive shields in Herod's palace. Philo calls him "naturally inflexible, a blend of self-will and relentlessness." Recalled to Rome c. 36 CE after massacring a group of Samaritans gathered on Mount Gerizim — Tiberius dies before the inquiry; Pilate disappears from the record. Later tradition (mostly legendary) reports suicide or banishment.

Gospel portraits soften progressively toward a Pilate reluctant to condemn [[jesus]], pushed by Jewish leadership: [[gospel-of-mark]] 15 is the simplest, [[gospel-of-matthew]] 27 adds the wife's dream and the hand-washing, [[gospel-of-luke]] 23 sends [[jesus]] to [[herod-antipas]] in a turf evasion, [[gospel-of-john]] 18-19 stages an extended theological dialogue ("What is truth?"). The trajectory is widely read as apologetic — distancing Rome from blame and concentrating responsibility on Jewish authorities, a move with catastrophic consequences for medieval and modern Jewish-Christian relations.

The condemnation of [[jesus]] is among the most historically secure data of the gospels — corroborated by Tacitus and Josephus, attested in the early creedal formula of [[1-timothy]] 6:13 ("Christ Jesus, who in his testimony before Pontius Pilate made the good confession") and the Apostles' Creed ("crucified under Pontius Pilate"). Ethiopian and Coptic traditions canonize Pilate as a saint, holding to a legend of his conversion; mainstream Christian tradition does not.
