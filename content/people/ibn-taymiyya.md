Ibn Taymiyya (1263-1328) — Hanbali jurist-theologian of the Mamluk period; the most consequential medieval Muslim polemicist against Christianity and the foundational reference for later Salafi and reformist movements. Born at [[harran]] in upper Mesopotamia; family fled the Mongol advance to [[damascus]], where he was trained in the Hanbali madhhab and where he taught for most of his life. Imprisoned several times by Mamluk authorities for his combative positions on visitation of tombs, divorce formulas, and the divine attributes; died in the citadel of Damascus.

*al-Jawāb al-Ṣaḥīḥ li-man baddala dīn al-Masīḥ* (The Sound Reply to One Who Altered the Religion of the Messiah) — a vast multi-volume refutation of Christianity, structured as a point-by-point response to the Letter from the People of Cyprus (itself a reworking of [[paul-of-antioch]]'s twelfth-century apology). Marshals textual-critique, biblical-prophecy, and rational arguments against Trinity, incarnation, and the integrity of received Christian Scripture; canonical reference for the classical Muslim case against [[christianity]]. Engages [[classical-kalam]] critically — rejects its Greek-derived metaphysical apparatus while deploying its dialectical methods against Christian theology and Avicennian philosophy alike. *Darʾ Taʿāruḍ al-ʿAql wa-l-Naql* (Averting the Conflict between Reason and Revelation) is the systematic counter-statement against Ashʿarite and philosophical kalam. His influence on modern [[islamic-apologetic]] is mediated through nineteenth- and twentieth-century revivalists (Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhāb, Rashīd Riḍā) who made him a school in his own right.
