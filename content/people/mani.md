Mani (~216-276) — founder of Manichaeism, a syncretic dualist religion that absorbed Christian, Zoroastrian, Buddhist, and gnostic elements and claimed universal scope. Born near Ctesiphon in Sasanian Mesopotamia to a Parthian father who had joined the Elchasaite Jewish-Christian baptist sect; raised in the sect until two revelations (at twelve and twenty-four) commissioned him to a new mission. The Cologne Mani Codex — a Greek miniature codex containing autobiographical and disciple material, discovered in 1969 — has revolutionized study of his early life.

Theological architecture: an absolute dualism of two unoriginate eternal principles, Light and Darkness, locked in cosmic conflict. The present world is the temporary mixture of light particles trapped in dark matter, the soul a fragment of light entombed in body. Salvation is the cosmic separation: light is liberated from matter through ascetic abstention (the Elect/Electi observe strict celibacy, vegetarianism, no farming or violence to plants); the Hearers (Auditores) support the Elect and progress toward Election in future lives. Christ, the Buddha, and Zoroaster are previous messengers of light; Mani himself the Seal of the Prophets and final messenger. Significant influence on Islamic and later European usages of that title.

Wrote seven canonical scriptures in Syriac (Living Gospel, Treasure of Life, Pragmateia, Book of Mysteries, Book of Giants, Letters, Psalms and Prayers) plus one in Middle Persian (Shabuhragan) for Shapur I, who granted protection. Mani traveled to India and likely Central Asia. The Manichaean church organized into a hierarchy (leader, twelve apostles, seventy-two bishops, three hundred sixty elders) modeled on Christian and apostolic structures, with its own canon, hymnal, and missionary infrastructure that spread from North Africa to China.

Lost royal protection under Bahram I, was imprisoned at Gundeshapur, and died in captivity (276) — Manichaean tradition treats the death as crucifixion-analogue martyrdom. The religion spread west into the Roman Empire, where [[augustine-of-hippo]] was a Hearer for nine years (Confessions 3-7) before conversion to Catholic Christianity and a lifetime of anti-Manichaean polemic (Contra Faustum, Contra Felicem, Acta contra Fortunatum). Manichaeism survived in Central Asia among the Uyghurs into the second millennium and in southeast China (Fujian) into the seventeenth century; Manichaean Bogomil and Cathar influence on medieval European dualisms is debated but plausible.
