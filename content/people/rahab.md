Rahab (`Rāḥāb`, "wide / broad"), `ʾiššâ zônâ` ("prostitute," Josh 2:1) of Jericho. Hides the two Israelite spies sent by [[joshua]] before the assault on the city; lies to the king's officers; lowers the spies down through her window in the city wall by a scarlet cord (`tiqwat ḥûṭ haššānî`).

Her speech to the spies (Josh 2:9-13) is the longest theological confession from a foreigner in the OT. She has heard what YHWH did at the Sea and to Sihon and Og; "your terror has fallen upon us"; `YHWH ʾĕlōhêkem hûʾ ʾĕlōhîm baššāmayim mimmaʿal wĕʿal-hāʾāreṣ mittāḥat` ("YHWH your God, he is God in the heavens above and on the earth below"). Bargains for the rescue of her father, mother, brothers, sisters, and all who belong to them — extends covenantal protection to her household. Marks the house with the same scarlet cord (a Passover-blood echo).

Survives the `ḥērem` of Jericho with all her family (Josh 6:17, 22-25): "she has lived in Israel to this day" — a notice acknowledging an absorbed clan within Israel. The exception she carves out is significant against the totalizing language of the conquest narrative — Canaanites who confess YHWH can be incorporated.

Genealogy ([[gospel-of-matthew]] 1:5). Matthew lists Rahab as mother of Boaz (and so great-grandmother of [[david]]), placing her in the messianic line of [[jesus]] alongside three other foreign/irregular women (Tamar, Ruth, Bathsheba). The genealogy is genealogically problematic (the timeline from Rahab to David is too compressed) but theologically pointed — Matthew foregrounds Gentile-and-scandal as constitutive of the Davidic-messianic story.

Reception. Heb 11:31 lists her among the heroes of faith ("by faith Rahab the prostitute did not perish with those who were disobedient"); James 2:25 deploys her as Exhibit B (after [[abraham]]) for justification by works — "was not Rahab the prostitute justified by works when she received the messengers and sent them out by another way?" — a famous proof-text in the faith/works debates of the Reformation. Patristic typology reads the scarlet cord as a Passover/crucifixion type. Modern scholarship has examined the trope of the helpful-foreign-prostitute (a Canaanite figure brought into Israel's salvation) as a deliberate inclusionary counterweight to the conquest narrative's exclusionary edge.
