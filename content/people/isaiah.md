Isaiah son of Amoz is the 8th-c. BCE Jerusalem prophet whose name attaches to the longest book in the prophetic canon, the [[book-of-isaiah]]. His call vision (Isa 6) is one of the canon's most quoted theophanies — the seraphim, the burning coal, "Holy, holy, holy is YHWH of hosts," and the impossible commission to harden Israel's ears against repentance. He prophesies under Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and [[hezekiah]], with court access apparently throughout.

The historical Isaiah's career intersects the great geopolitical crisis of his era — the Syro-Ephraimite war (734-732 BCE), the destruction of the northern kingdom by [[assyria]] (722 BCE), and the siege of Jerusalem by Sennacherib (701 BCE). The Immanuel oracle of Isa 7 is delivered to king Ahaz under Aramean-Israelite pressure; the deliverance from Sennacherib is presented in parallel narratives at Isa 36-39 and [[2-kings]] 18-20.

Critical scholarship distinguishes three major compositional layers within the canonical book: First Isaiah (chs. 1-39, attributed to the 8th-c. prophet and his school), Deutero-Isaiah (chs. 40-55, set during the Babylonian exile, c. 540s BCE, by an anonymous prophet whose audience is to be comforted with imminent return), and Trito-Isaiah (chs. 56-66, post-exilic, addressing the disappointments of return). The unity-vs-multiplicity debate has been a centerpiece of modern critical study since Duhm (1892); conservative scholarship variously defends a unitary 8th-c. prophet or compositional unity by later disciples.

The Servant Songs (Isa 42, 49, 50, 52:13-53:12) within Deutero-Isaiah present a suffering figure who bears the iniquities of many — the most extensively quoted OT material in the NT for [[jesus]]'s passion (especially Isa 53 in Mark 15, Acts 8, 1 Peter 2). Whether the Servant is the prophet himself, the nation Israel, an idealized remnant, or a future individual messianic figure is one of biblical interpretation's most contested questions, with substantial Jewish and Christian readings of each.
