Greek pandect, 5th c. CE, almost certainly produced in Egypt (a 17th-c. Arabic note inside identifies Thekla as the scribe, plausibly fictitious; paleography supports Alexandrian origin). Sigla: **A**, **02**. Four volumes, ~773 surviving leaves of vellum, two columns per page.

OT in [[septuagint]] form, near-complete (lacks portions of 1 Sam, 1 Maccabees, and a few Psalms). Includes all six standard deuterocanonical books and the additional [[3-maccabees]] and [[4-maccabees]]. NT substantially complete with significant lacunae in [[gospel-of-matthew]] (lacks Matt 1:1-25:6), the Pericope Adulterae area of [[gospel-of-john]], and [[2-corinthians]] 4:13-12:6. Most notably, includes [[1-clement]] and a fragment of [[2-clement]] (breaking off mid-12:5) — direct witnesses to a 5th-c. Alexandrian canonical horizon that still treated 1 Clement as quasi-scriptural.

Provenance: held in the Patriarchal Library of Alexandria by the 11th c. (a note records ownership by patriarch Athanasius II in 1098); Patriarch Cyril Lucaris of Constantinople (formerly of Alexandria) presented it to King Charles I of England in 1627 through ambassador Sir Thomas Roe. Held at the British Museum from 1753, now at the British Library (Royal MS 1.D.V-VIII). Its arrival in England came just a decade too late to influence the [[king-james-bible]] (1611), one of the great near-misses in textual history.

Textually mixed. In Gospels, generally Byzantine — a major early witness to that text-type. In Acts and Epistles, Alexandrian. In [[revelation]], with [[codex-ephraemi]], the chief witness (Sinaiticus is the only earlier major Revelation witness). Foundational; the codex from which the Greek NT was first widely studied in Western Europe.
