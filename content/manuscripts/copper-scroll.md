Unique among the [[dead-sea-scrolls]]: engraved on two rolled sheets of nearly pure copper, not written on leather or papyrus. Recovered from Cave 3 at [[qumran]] in 1952. Mishnaic-style Hebrew, ~50-100 CE on paleographic and metallurgical grounds. Twelve columns listing 64 caches of buried treasure — gold, silver, vessels, scrolls — with topographical clues for each location ("under the eastern corner of the cistern at...").

Opened only by sawing into 23 strips (Manchester, 1955-56), after which it became fragile and brittle; modern conservation has stabilized it. Currently in the Jordan Museum, Amman.

Genre and historicity disputed. Three main hypotheses: (a) folkloric — a treasure legend, comparable to Greek inscriptional treasure-lists, no historical referent; (b) Temple treasure — caches of the Second Temple's wealth hidden before the [[destruction-of-the-second-temple]] in 70 CE (Allegro, Wolters); (c) Qumran community wealth, perhaps tithes. Total quoted value (~65 tons of precious metal) strains hypothesis (c); (b) is currently the leading reading. None of the caches has been confidently located.

Not a textual witness to scripture. Important for: (i) the broader question of who deposited material in the Qumran caves and why (the Copper Scroll suggests the caves served as a generalized Judean refugee depository, not only as a sectarian library, weakening strict Qumran-community-only theories); (ii) the linguistic history of post-biblical Hebrew (Mishnaic features predominate). Editio princeps: Milik, DJD 3 (1962).
