English university town whose schools coalesced into the University of Oxford in the late 11th and 12th centuries, becoming, alongside [[paris]], one of the two great centers of medieval [[scholastic]] theology and the principal one in the English-speaking world. Distinctive for its early emphasis on mathematics, natural philosophy, and logic (the "Oxford Calculators" of Merton College).

[[duns-scotus]] taught here as a Franciscan regent master; [[william-of-ockham]] studied and lectured at Greyfriars before his Avignon trial; John Wyclif, whose vernacular-scripture program anticipated later reform movements, was an Oxford master in the 14th century. In the modern era, the university remained a center of Christian intellectual culture, including the literary-apologetic work of [[c-s-lewis]] and J.R.R. Tolkien.
