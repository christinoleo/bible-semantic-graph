Twelve chapters in the Protestant canon, fourteen in the Catholic/Orthodox (with Greek additions: Prayer of Azariah and Song of the Three Young Men inserted at 3:23; Susanna as ch. 13; Bel and the Dragon as ch. 14). Hebrew canon places Daniel among the [[ketuvim|Writings]], not the Prophets — already signaling its anomalous status. LXX-based Christian canons place it with the major prophets.

Bilingual: 1:1-2:4a Hebrew; 2:4b-7:28 Aramaic; 8:1-12:13 Hebrew. The Aramaic section (chiastically arranged: chs. 2-7 with parallel themes A-B-C-C'-B'-A') is widely held to be the earliest core. The bilingualism is itself a major source-critical datum.

Two-part structure: court tales (1-6) — the four young men in Babylon, the fiery furnace, Nebuchadnezzar's dreams, Belshazzar's feast, Daniel in the lions' den — featuring [[daniel]] and his three companions as Diaspora exemplars under [[nebuchadnezzar]], Belshazzar, and "Darius the Mede." Apocalyptic visions (7-12) — four beasts and the [[son-of-man]]; ram and goat; the seventy weeks; the final vision and resurrection (12:2, the OT's clearest individual-resurrection text).

Dating is the most contested datum in OT scholarship. Traditional view (preserved by conservative Protestants and most pre-modern Jewish and Christian commentators): 6th-c. composition by the historical Daniel during the Babylonian and early Persian periods. Critical consensus since Porphyry (3rd c. CE, preserved in Jerome) and definitively from the 19th c.: pseudonymous composition ca. 167-164 BCE during the Antiochene crisis, with the court tales possibly drawing on older Diaspora material. Evidence: detailed and accurate "predictions" of Hellenistic events through Antiochus IV's profanation of the Temple (Dan 11), then vaguer and historically incorrect after that point; late-biblical Hebrew and Aramaic linguistic features; Persian and Greek loanwords; the book's absence from Sirach's catalogue of worthies (ca. 180 BCE); historical inaccuracies about the Babylonian period (Belshazzar as Nebuchadnezzar's son; "Darius the Mede" as an intermediate ruler before Cyrus).

Themes: divine sovereignty over empires; the "fourth kingdom" schema (Babylon-Media-Persia-Greece in critical reading; Babylon-Persia-Greece-Rome in traditional Christian reading); the [[son-of-man]] figure (7:13-14) — receives an everlasting kingdom from the Ancient of Days, central to NT Christology; the seventy weeks (9:24-27), arguably the most-disputed chronological calculation in biblical exegesis; resurrection and angelic mediation (Michael, Gabriel — Daniel introduces named angels to the OT).

Reception: foundational for [[apocalyptic|apocalyptic literature]] (1 Enoch, 4 Ezra, [[revelation]]). [[jesus]]'s self-designation as son-of-man (most explicit at the trial, [[gospel-of-mark]] 14:62) cites Dan 7:13. The "abomination of desolation" (Dan 9:27; 11:31; 12:11) is reapplied in the [[gospel-of-matthew|Synoptic]] apocalypses (Mark 13 / Matt 24). Rabbinic, patristic, Reformation, and dispensationalist traditions each developed elaborate Danielic chronologies.
