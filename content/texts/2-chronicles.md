Second half of the Chronicler's work (originally one scroll with [[1-chronicles]]). Reign of [[solomon]] through the destruction of the Jerusalem temple and the decree of Cyrus permitting return — ending the Hebrew Bible itself in the Tanakh ordering on a note of restoration.

The Chronicler's strategy of selection and emphasis remains sharp: only the kings of Judah are treated as legitimate, the northern kingdom virtually disappears, immediate retribution governs the narrative (good kings prosper, wicked kings suffer in their own lifetimes — most strikingly, [[manasseh]] is given an exilic repentance unattested in [[2-kings]] to explain his unaccountably long reign). The temple and its cult dominate; Levitical singers and gatekeepers receive prominence absent in the Deuteronomistic source.

Structure: Solomon's reign focused on temple construction and dedication (2 Chr 1-9), with the Bathsheba affair and Adonijah revolt absent and the queen of Sheba pericope expanded; schism under [[rehoboam]] (2 Chr 10-12); reigns of Judahite kings down to the exile (2 Chr 13-36), with major expansions on the reforms of Asa, Jehoshaphat, [[hezekiah]] (esp. the Passover celebration, 2 Chr 30), Manasseh (his repentance, 2 Chr 33), and [[josiah]] (esp. his Passover, 2 Chr 35); the fall and exile in compressed form (2 Chr 36); concluding with the Cyrus decree, which is then picked up as the opening of [[book-of-ezra]].

Themes: temple-centered piety as the criterion of legitimate kingship, immediate retribution, Levitical orthodoxy, the possibility of repentance even for the worst king, Davidic-Solomonic continuity as the spine of true Israel, the exile as interruption rather than termination.

Reception: prooftext repertoire for post-exilic Jewish identity centered on Jerusalem and the temple; Christian reception has often used the Manasseh narrative as paradigm of repentance (the apocryphal Prayer of Manasseh expands this thread). The Cyrus decree (Persian-period authorization for temple rebuilding) is a key historiographic anchor.
