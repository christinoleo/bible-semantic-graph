Fourteen chapters. Critical scholarship near-unanimously partitions the book into First Zechariah (1-8) and Second Zechariah (9-14, sometimes further subdivided into Zech 9-11 and 12-14, called Deutero- and Trito-Zechariah). The case is unusually strong: First Zechariah is dated 520-518 BCE under Darius I, contemporaneous with [[book-of-haggai]] and concerned with Temple rebuilding; Second Zechariah is undated, anonymous (no superscription introducing the new section), and reflects a markedly different — Hellenistic-period, possibly 4th-3rd c. BCE — historical and theological horizon.

First Zechariah (1-8): eight night-visions ([[zechariah|four horsemen]], horns and craftsmen, measuring line, vision of [[zechariah|Joshua the high priest]] and the Satan, the lampstand and two olive trees, the flying scroll, the woman in the ephah, four chariots) interspersed with oracular material. Themes: Temple rebuilding (paired with [[book-of-haggai]]), purification of the priesthood, the dual leadership of Zerubbabel (Davidic) and Joshua (priestly), the "two anointed ones" (4:14) — the source of later "two-messiahs" speculation in Qumran and rabbinic tradition. Highly developed angelology — interpreting angels mediate the visions to the prophet, a feature pointing toward fully apocalyptic literature.

Second Zechariah (9-14): three undated "oracle" (maśśāʾ) units. Apocalyptic in idiom: the coming king on a donkey (9:9), the worthless shepherd and the thirty pieces of silver (11:4-17), the pierced one whom they look upon (12:10), the eschatological battle for Jerusalem, the splitting of the Mount of Olives (14), living waters from Jerusalem, holiness extended to everyday objects ("on that day there shall be inscribed on the bells of the horses, 'Holy to YHWH'"). Theologically distinct from chs. 1-8 — no Temple-rebuilding focus, sharper apocalyptic dualism, eschatological universalism.

Themes (whole book): purification and restoration of Jerusalem; coming peaceable king; the suffering and pierced figure (12:10) — a major OT crux read messianically by Christian tradition and as a national-Israel figure by Jewish tradition; YHWH's universal kingship (14:9); eschatological reversal of the curse on Canaanite-in-the-house (14:21). The book's apocalyptic imagery makes it the closest OT analogue to [[book-of-daniel]] and [[revelation]].

Reception: Zechariah is the most heavily mined OT prophetic book by the [[gospel-of-matthew|gospel]] passion narratives. Zech 9:9 → triumphal entry (Matt 21:5; [[gospel-of-john]] 12:15); Zech 11:12-13 → the thirty pieces of silver thrown into the temple (Matt 27:9-10, attributed by Matthew to Jeremiah — a famous citation puzzle); Zech 12:10 → the piercing (John 19:37; [[revelation]] 1:7); Zech 13:7 → the shepherd struck and the sheep scattered (Matt 26:31). Patristic and Reformation theology consequently develop heavy Zechariah-Christology. The split between First and Second Zechariah is itself an important test case in OT source criticism since [[gospel-of-matthew]] 27:9 already cites Zech 11 as "spoken through the prophet Jeremiah" — an attribution puzzle that exercised the early Church.
