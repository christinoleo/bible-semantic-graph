Diaspora-set deliverance narrative under the Persian court of Ahasuerus (commonly identified with Xerxes I, r. 486-465 BCE). [[esther]], a Jewish queen, and her cousin [[mordecai]] avert the genocide planned by the vizier [[haman]]. Etiological narrative for the festival of Purim.

In the Tanakh, one of the five Megillot in the Ketuvim, read at Purim. The Masoretic Text is famously absent of the divine name — God is never mentioned, miracles are absent, and deliverance unfolds through a chain of "coincidences" that the narrative leaves the reader to construe theologically. The Greek (Septuagint) version adds substantial deuterocanonical "Additions to Esther" that supply explicit prayer, divine intervention, and pious dialogue, accepted in Catholic and Orthodox canons.

Critical scholarship dates composition to the late Persian or Hellenistic period; historicity is contested (no Persian queen Esther is attested in extant sources; the king's character is largely a literary construct). The book reads as a court tale and carnivalesque diaspora novella, with structural reversals (peripeteia) governing every act.

Structure: deposition of Vashti and Esther's accession (Esth 1-2); Mordecai's refusal to bow and Haman's decree (Esth 3); Esther's risk and Mordecai's appeal — "perhaps for such a time as this" (Esth 4); banquets, Haman's gallows, the sleepless king (Esth 5-6); reversal — Haman impaled on his own gallows, Mordecai elevated (Esth 7); counter-decree, Jewish self-defense (Esth 8-9:19); institution of Purim (Esth 9:20-32); brief epilogue (Esth 10).

Themes: hidden providence, diaspora identity and loyalty under foreign rule, reversal of fortunes, the precariousness of Jewish existence in exile, female agency and risk. The absence of the divine name has been read variously as deliberate diaspora theology, as scribal accident, and as theological provocation.

Reception: central to the festival of Purim, with its raucous reading, costuming, and erasure of Haman's name. Luther infamously wished the book did not exist; critical Christian reception has long been ambivalent, sometimes shading into antisemitic readings of Jewish "vengeance" in Esth 9. Post-Holocaust Jewish reception has reclaimed Esther as theology of survival.
