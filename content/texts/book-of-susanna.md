Sixty-four-verse novella featuring the chaste Babylonian-Jewish wife [[susanna]], two lecherous Jewish elders who, after spying on her bath, attempt to coerce her into adultery and, when refused, accuse her of it in court, and the young [[daniel]] who exposes their perjury by cross-examining them separately on the species of the tree under which they claim to have witnessed her crime — one says a mastic, the other a holm-oak — leading to their execution under the talionic provision of Deut 19:18-21 (the false witness suffers the penalty they sought to inflict). Susanna is vindicated; Daniel's reputation as wise judge is established.

Textual situation. Susanna is preserved in two distinct Greek versions — the Old Greek (OG, surviving in only a handful of witnesses, notably Papyrus 967 and the Syro-Hexapla) and the Theodotionic version (Th, the form preserved in most LXX manuscripts and the basis of all Western translation). Th, despite its conventional attribution to the 2nd-c. CE Jewish reviser Theodotion, is now generally held to be a pre-Christian text that became the standard Greek Daniel by patristic times; OG is shorter and rhetorically rougher, Th expanded and narratively polished. No Hebrew or Aramaic original is extant; debate over a Semitic Vorlage has hinged largely on the wordplay between the trees and the punishments — "mastic / saw asunder" (schinon / schisei) and "holm-oak / cut in two" (prinon / prisai) — which works as paronomasia in Greek, suggesting either an original Greek composition or extraordinarily lucky translation choices. Most current scholars favor a Greek original.

Position in the canon. In LXX manuscripts Susanna is placed either before Daniel 1 (as an introductory exemplum) or as Daniel 13 (the position adopted by the Vulgate and the standard Catholic versification). In the Old Greek, Susanna stands separately; Theodotion's placement varied by codex. Hebrew Daniel and the Tanakh do not contain it.

Date and origin. Probably composed in the 2nd or early 1st c. BCE, in the same Jewish Hellenistic milieu that produced [[bel-and-the-dragon]] and [[prayer-of-azariah]]. Connections to internal Jewish legal-reform polemic have been argued — Susanna's exoneration by the cross-examination procedure (questioning witnesses separately) prefigures or reflects Pharisaic / proto-rabbinic judicial reform against an older Sadducean reliance on conjoined testimony.

Themes. Female sexual virtue under threat; the abuse of judicial authority by elders; the duty of separate examination of witnesses; the vindication of the righteous through wise judgment; the origin of Daniel as legal hero distinct from his role as apocalyptic seer in [[book-of-daniel]] proper. The story is the OT-and-Apocrypha's earliest sustained legal-procedural narrative.

Reception. Powerful afterlife in Christian art and ethics: a frequent subject in early Christian funerary art (Susanna as the soul rescued by Christ-as-Daniel, Roman catacombs, 3rd c.); a stock subject of Renaissance and Baroque painting (Tintoretto, Veronese, Rubens, Rembrandt, Gentileschi — whose treatment recast the elders' aggression in starkly feminist key); cited by [[hippolytus]] in his Daniel commentary as the first sustained Christian exegesis of the book; legal-historical scholarship since Sussmann and Daube reads Susanna as a parable of Pharisaic judicial procedure. Excluded from Protestant canons; the King James Apocrypha printed it as "The History of Susanna."
