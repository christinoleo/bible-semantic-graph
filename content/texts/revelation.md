The only sustained apocalypse in the NT, formally trifold: apocalypse, prophecy, and letter (1:1-3; 1:4-5; 22:7-21). Self-presented as the revelation Jesus Christ gave to "his servant John" while in exile "on the island called Patmos, on account of the word of God and the testimony of Jesus" (1:9). Identity of this "John" is among the longest-running NT disputes:
- traditional ascription to [[john-apostle]] son of Zebedee (Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian);
- distinction from the apostle, in favor of "John the Elder" (Dionysius of Alexandria, 3rd c., on linguistic grounds — Revelation's Greek is notoriously Semitic-inflected and uneven, contrasting sharply with the polished Gospel and Epistles);
- a separate Christian prophet "John the Seer", otherwise unknown — the standard modern critical position.

Dating: two main options. (1) Late-Domitianic (ca. 95-96 CE) — the mainstream view, on Irenaeus's testimony ("seen no very long time ago, almost in our own day, toward the end of Domitian's reign") and on internal indications of an imperial cult crisis. (2) Neronian (ca. 65-68 CE) — defended by preterist interpreters and a minority of critics, reading the beast's number 666 as Nero (gematria of Neron Caesar in Hebrew letters = 666; textual variant 616 = Nero Caesar) and the "five fallen, one is, one to come" sequence of kings (17:9-11) as pointing to a Neronian-Vespasianic moment.

Setting: the seven churches of Asia Minor addressed in chs. 2-3 (Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia, Laodicea) suggest the work circulated in the same network as [[1-peter]] and the Pauline mission. The pressures named — accommodation with idol-meat (Nicolaitans), conflict with "those who say they are Jews and are not" (synagogue-church polemic), incipient persecution, the call to "give heed" against the imperial cult — fit late-1st-c. Asia Minor under Domitian's intensified cult of the divinized emperor.

Structure: prologue (1); letters to the seven churches (2-3); throne vision and the Lamb (4-5); seven seals (6-8:1); seven trumpets (8-11); the woman, dragon, two beasts, and the Lamb on Zion (12-14); seven bowls (15-16); fall of Babylon (17-19:10); the parousia, millennium, last judgment (19:11-20:15); new heavens, new earth, new Jerusalem (21-22:5); epilogue (22:6-21). Sevens are structuring throughout (churches, seals, trumpets, bowls, hymns); the structure is variably read as strictly sequential (futurist), as recapitulated cycles (each cycle covering the church age from a different angle — the classic idealist and amillennialist reading), or as a non-linear visionary tapestry.

Hermeneutical schools (the four classic options):
- Preterist: the prophecies refer primarily to events of the 1st century (fall of Jerusalem 70 CE, Domitianic persecution, fall of Rome later) — favored in much critical scholarship.
- Historicist: an unfolding map of church history from John to the end — dominant from medieval period through the Reformation (the papacy as antichrist, the Reformation as one of the seals/trumpets); now mostly defunct in scholarship, preserved in some Adventist traditions.
- Futurist: the bulk of chs. 4-22 await the eschatological future — dominant in modern dispensationalism (Darby, Scofield, Lindsey, LaHaye); also the Patristic-chiliast view.
- Idealist: the visions symbolize timeless patterns of divine sovereignty and conflict with evil, not specific historical events — common in Reformed amillennialism (Hendriksen, Beale to a degree).

Most current critical commentaries combine preterist primary reference with idealist applicability.

Heavy OT allusion: Revelation has been described as a "tissue of Old Testament allusions" — ~278 of the book's 404 verses contain OT echoes, especially from [[book-of-daniel]] (the Son of Man, the four beasts, the time-times-half-time, the cosmic king), [[book-of-ezekiel]] (throne vision, scroll-eating, Gog-Magog, the river from the temple, measurement of the city), [[book-of-zechariah]] (the horsemen, two witnesses, lampstands), [[book-of-isaiah]] (new heavens and earth, eschatological feast), [[exodus]] (the Exodus plagues mapped onto the trumpets/bowls — making the Lamb's victory a cosmic Passover). The book never quotes the OT directly; instead it weaves its imagery into a new apocalyptic register.

Major figures and images: [[jesus]] as Lamb (ἀρνίον, occurring 28x — the slain-yet-standing victor), the Son of Man (1:13-16), the woman clothed with the sun (12 — variously read as Israel, Mary, the church, all three), the dragon (Satan), the beast from the sea (the imperial power, ch. 13), the beast from the land / false prophet (the imperial cult priesthood), Babylon the great whore (Rome, 17-18), the rider on the white horse (19:11-16), the two witnesses (11), the 144,000 (7, 14), the seven spirits before the throne, the four living creatures.

Imperial critique: Babylon's lament (ch. 18) is the most sustained anti-imperial text in the canon — a satirical dirge over a Rome stripped of its luxury and trade networks, "drunk with the blood of the saints" (17:6). The economic catalog of Rome's imports (18:11-13) culminates in "human bodies, that is, human souls" — naming the slave trade as the moral nadir of imperial commerce.

Reception: textually unstable in early canonical history (Eastern church slow to receive it; Syrian church largely omits it); thereafter the most controversially read book in Christian history. Generates the Antichrist tradition (synthesized with [[2-thessalonians]], [[book-of-daniel]], [[1-john]]); Joachim of Fiore's tripartite history; Reformation papacy-as-Antichrist polemic; modern dispensationalism (Darby's Plymouth Brethren, Scofield's Reference Bible, Hal Lindsey, the Left Behind franchise) with its rapture-tribulation-millennium schema; liberation theology (Allan Boesak, Pablo Richard) reads Revelation as a manual for resistance against empire. The new Jerusalem (21-22) anchors Christian hope and shapes the entire imagery of Christian afterlife and consummation.
