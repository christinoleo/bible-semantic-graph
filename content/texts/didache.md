Brief church manual of the late 1st or very early 2nd c. CE, the earliest surviving Christian non-canonical document of its kind. Sixteen short chapters cover four sequential areas: a "Two Ways" ethical catechism for baptismal candidates (chs. 1-6), liturgical instruction on baptism, fasting, and Eucharist (chs. 7-10), regulations on itinerant prophets and apostles and on settled ministry (chs. 11-15), and a brief eschatological exhortation (ch. 16). Rediscovered by Philotheos Bryennios in a Jerusalem manuscript (Codex Hierosolymitanus) in 1873 and published 1883, the work transformed scholarly understanding of early Christianity. Not in any canon.

Composition: a strong case can be made for a date as early as the 50s-70s CE for the underlying material, with the canonical-Didache form crystallizing ~80-110 CE. The work knows oral or written Jesus-tradition (the Lord's Prayer of ch. 8 is closer to [[gospel-of-matthew]] 6 than to [[gospel-of-luke]] 11, with a doxology that has come to be appended to the Matthean prayer in liturgical use), but the question of whether the Didache cites a written [[gospel-of-matthew]] or draws on a common pre-Matthean tradition remains contested. Likely provenance is Syria or western Palestine.

The "Two Ways" section (chs. 1-6) is a Christian adaptation of a Jewish ethical catechism (independently attested in the Epistle of Barnabas chs. 18-20, in [[testament-of-the-twelve-patriarchs]] passages, in 1QS, and in the Latin *Doctrina Apostolorum*). The Two Ways pattern — life vs death, light vs darkness — frames ethics as covenantal choice. The Didachist's version adds dominical sayings (chs. 1.3-2.1, a Christianized expansion echoing the Sermon on the Mount: love enemies, turn the other cheek, give to all who ask) that may be the earliest extant testimonia of Jesus-traditions independent of the canonical Gospels.

Liturgical content is remarkably specific. Baptism: by triple immersion in living (running) water, in the name of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (the earliest non-NT attestation of the threefold name) — pour water on the head three times if no immersion is possible (ch. 7). Fasting: Wednesdays and Fridays (not Mondays and Thursdays like "the hypocrites"). Eucharist: prefaced by short prayers thanking God for the cup and the broken bread, including the petition "as this broken bread was scattered upon the mountains, and being gathered together became one, so let your church be gathered together from the ends of the earth into your kingdom" (ch. 9). These prayers preserve a stratum of Christian eucharistic devotion older than the developed anaphora of [[hippolytus]]; whether they represent the full eucharistic prayer or a paschal-meal grace remains debated.

Church order: the work describes a community in transition. Apostles and prophets are itinerant; the test of a true prophet is partly material ("if he stays three days he is a false prophet"; "if he asks for money in the Spirit, do not listen to him"). The community also "elects bishops and deacons, men worthy of the Lord," who are to be honored alongside the prophets and teachers (ch. 15). The threefold ministry of bishops-presbyters-deacons familiar from the later 2nd c. is not yet visible.

The eschatological coda (ch. 16) urges vigilance and predicts the coming "world-deceiver" (κοσμοπλάνος) who will perform signs and wonders, the gathering of the elect, the resurrection of the dead (though only of the saints, not of all), and the Lord's appearance. The eschatology is severe and brief, drawing on Matthean and Pauline categories.

Reception: cited or used by [[eusebius]] (listed among the antilegomena), [[athanasius]] (in his Letter 39 of 367 the *Didache* is among the books "appointed by the fathers to be read by those who newly join us"), the *Apostolic Constitutions* (which incorporate large portions of it), and the *Apostolic Church Order*. The work was lost in the West after the early 5th c. The 1873 rediscovery transformed liturgics, ethics, and early-church-history scholarship; the *Didache* is now one of the most studied non-canonical Christian texts and the foundational document of the Apostolic Fathers corpus.
