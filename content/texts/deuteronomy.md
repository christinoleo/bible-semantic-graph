Fifth and final book of the [[torah]]. Moses' farewell discourses on the plains of Moab: retrospective on the wilderness, restatement of the law, blessings and curses, succession to [[joshua]], death of [[moses]].

Source-critically distinct from the J/E/P of the rest of the Pentateuch: "D" is its own stratum in the [[documentary-hypothesis]] and the literary engine of the Deuteronomistic History ([[book-of-joshua]] through [[2-kings]]). The dominant scholarly hypothesis since de Wette (1805) identifies an early form of Deuteronomy with the "book of the law" found in the temple under [[josiah]] in 622 BCE (2 Kgs 22), making the core of D a 7th-c. Judahite reform document later extended in the exilic period.

Structure: three Mosaic discourses — historical retrospective (Dt 1-4); central legal corpus (Dt 5-26), reframing the Decalogue and the Covenant Code with centralization of worship as its programmatic innovation; blessings and curses with covenant ratification (Dt 27-30); succession, the Song of Moses, the Blessing of Moses, and death of Moses on Mount Nebo (Dt 31-34).

Themes: covenant fidelity, exclusive worship of [[yhwh]] at a single chosen place, retributive theology (obedience → blessing, disobedience → exile), love of God as the law's animating principle (the Shema, Dt 6:4-5), social-ethical concern for ger, orphan, and widow. Deuteronomy's covenant form has been compared to Neo-Assyrian vassal treaties (notably Esarhaddon's), suggesting both literary genre and political subtext.

Major figures: [[moses]], [[joshua]], with retrospective references to the patriarchs and exodus generation.

Reception: the most cited Pentateuchal book in the New Testament — central to [[jesus]]' temptation narrative and to his summary of the law (Dt 6:5 paired with Lev 19:18). The Shema is the daily liturgical core of rabbinic Judaism. Deuteronomic theology shapes the prophetic critique of monarchy and exile, and modern source criticism uses Deuteronomy as the anchor point for absolute dating of Pentateuchal layers.
