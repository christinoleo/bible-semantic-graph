Undisputed Pauline letter, written ca. 53-55 CE from Ephesus (16:8). Response to oral reports from "Chloe's people" (1:11) and to a letter from the Corinthian church (7:1, "now concerning the matters about which you wrote"). [[paul]] had founded the Corinthian church on his second journey (cf. [[acts]] 18); [[priscilla]] and [[aquila]] were among the founding community. Mention of an earlier letter (5:9, "I wrote to you in my letter…") shows 1 Cor is at least the second piece of Pauline correspondence with Corinth — sometimes called the "previous letter" and now lost.

Corinth: bustling Roman colony, refounded 44 BCE, double-port commercial hub, religiously plural (Aphrodite cult, Isis, imperial cult), socially stratified. The congregation appears socioeconomically mixed (1:26, "not many were wise…powerful…of noble birth"), with internal tensions structured along status lines.

Structure: thanksgiving (1:1-9); divisions and the message of the cross (1-4); incest and lawsuits (5-6); responses to Corinthian questions introduced by περὶ δέ ("now concerning…", 7:1, 25; 8:1; 12:1; 16:1, 12) — marriage and singleness (7); idol-meat and Christian freedom (8-10); worship, head coverings, the Lord's Supper (11); spiritual gifts and the body of Christ (12-14, with the love hymn 13 at the center); resurrection of the dead (15); collection for Jerusalem and travel plans (16).

Major issues addressed: factions oriented around teachers (Paul, Apollos, [[peter]]/Cephas, Christ — 1:12) and the rhetorical-philosophical pretensions of "wisdom"; the porneia of a man with his stepmother (5); appeals to pagan courts (6); marriage and abstinence in light of imminent eschatology (7, "the appointed time has grown short", 7:29); food sacrificed to idols and the limits of liberty (8-10); abuses at the Lord's Supper (11:17-34, the earliest narrative of institution, 11:23-26); ecstatic phenomena, glossolalia, and orderly worship (12-14); denial of bodily resurrection (15, possibly proto-Gnosticizing or Hellenistic-philosophical).

Theological contributions: the cross as foolishness/wisdom (1:18-25); body-of-Christ ecclesiology (12); love-hymn (13); the most extended NT discussion of resurrection (15) — Christ's resurrection as ἀπαρχή (firstfruits), Adam/Christ contrast, the spiritual body (σῶμα πνευματικόν); the eucharistic warrants (11:23-26).

Reception: chapter 13 is the most-quoted Pauline passage in Western literature; 15 anchors Christian doctrine of bodily resurrection; the household/marriage material (7, 11:2-16, 14:33b-36) is central to debates over women's roles in church and home. 14:33b-36 ("women should be silent in the churches") is widely judged by critical scholarship to be a non-Pauline interpolation, conflicting with 11:5 where women audibly pray and prophesy.

Major figures: [[paul]] (author), Sosthenes (co-sender, 1:1), Chloe (1:11), Apollos (Alexandrian Christian teacher, 1:12; 3:4-9; 16:12), [[peter]]/Cephas (1:12; 9:5; 15:5), Stephanas, Fortunatus, Achaicus (16:17), [[priscilla]] and [[aquila]] (16:19).
