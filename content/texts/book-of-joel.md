Three or four chapters (Hebrew and English versifications differ: MT has four chapters; English Bibles compress to three). Brief, sharply structured, with no superscription locating the prophet historically — dating is consequently the most uncontroversially contested datum in the book.

Range of proposed datings: 9th-c. pre-exilic (Keil, conservative defenders, on the absence of king-references and the centrality of priests); late pre-exilic; immediately post-exilic (late 6th c.); mid-Persian period (5th-4th c., the current critical majority, on linguistic grounds, the developed cult, the absence of the northern kingdom, allusions to other prophets, and proto-apocalyptic features). Hellenistic dating (3rd c.) is a minority position.

Structure: a devastating locust plague is taken as harbinger of the [[day-of-yhwh|Day of YHWH]] (1:1-2:17); divine response — restoration of crops, outpouring of the spirit, eschatological judgment of the nations in the Valley of Jehoshaphat (2:18-3:21). The locust catastrophe of ch. 1 has been read literally (an actual swarm), allegorically (a foreign army), and proto-apocalyptically (cosmic foreshadow).

Themes: [[day-of-yhwh|Day of YHWH]] (yôm YHWH) — Joel uses the phrase five times, more than any other prophet, oscillating between immediate ecological catastrophe and final eschatological judgment; collective repentance and fasting; democratization of the spirit ("I will pour out my spirit on all flesh — your sons and daughters shall prophesy," 2:28); the nations' judgment for crimes against Judah and the diaspora. Joel quotes earlier prophets liberally (e.g., 3:10 inverts Isa 2:4 / Mic 4:3 — plowshares back into swords) which the late-dating school takes as evidence of literary dependence.

Reception: Joel 2:28-32 is the centerpiece of [[peter]]'s [[acts|Pentecost]] sermon (Acts 2:17-21) — the foundational Christian text for charismatic and prophetic spirit-theology. [[romans]] 10:13 quotes Joel 2:32 ("everyone who calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved"), with [[paul]] re-applying "name of the Lord" Christologically. Patristic and rabbinic traditions both develop the day-of-the-Lord eschatology heavily.
