Collection of childhood miracle-stories of [[jesus]] between approximately age 5 and age 12, framed as a series of vignettes set in Nazareth and the surrounding villages. Despite the title, the work is unrelated to the Coptic sayings [[gospel-of-thomas]] from Nag Hammadi; the shared pseudonymous ascription to "Thomas" is the only connection, and even that is not universal across the manuscript tradition (Greek manuscript A names "Thomas the Israelite philosopher" — likely [[didymos-judas-thomas]]; some Syriac and Latin manuscripts give the work no Thomas-attribution at all). Not in any canon.

Surviving textual tradition is exceptionally complex. The work survives in two principal Greek recensions (one long, one short), a Syriac recension that may be closest to the original, multiple Latin recensions, Slavonic, Ethiopic, Georgian, and Arabic versions (the Arabic Infancy Gospel incorporates IGT material into a longer composite work). The earliest direct textual witness is a Syriac manuscript of the 5th-6th c.; references in [[irenaeus]] (*Adversus Haereses* 1.20.1, mentioning a story of the young Jesus and the alphabet that matches IGT 6-8) push the composition back to ~150 CE or earlier.

The episodes are arresting and unsettling to readers expecting hagiographic uniformity. At age five, Jesus fashions twelve sparrows from clay on the Sabbath; when a Jewish man complains to [[joseph-of-nazareth]], the child claps his hands and the sparrows come alive and fly away (IGT 2). When another child disperses pools of water Jesus has gathered, Jesus curses him and the child withers (IGT 3); a child who runs into Jesus and bumps him is similarly cursed and dies (IGT 4). The neighbors complain to Joseph; Joseph rebukes Jesus; Jesus blinds the accusers temporarily until Joseph pulls his ear hard, after which Jesus relents (IGT 5).

Schoolmaster scenes (IGT 6-8, 14-15): Jesus is taken to a teacher to learn his letters; the child outwits the teacher by demonstrating he already knows the cosmic-mystical significance of each letter of the alphabet; subsequent teachers fare no better. A second teacher strikes him and is struck dead; a third teacher gracefully recognizes Jesus's divine wisdom and is praised for it. The episode mixes proto-orthodox Christology (Jesus knows the divine secrets of language from infancy) with the temperamental-prodigy motif (curses on those who cross him).

Beneficent miracles balance the cursing scenes: Jesus heals James of a serpent bite (IGT 16), revives a child fallen from a roof and another killed in an axe accident (IGT 9-10), miraculously stretches a beam of wood for Joseph the carpenter (IGT 13), and finally reaches the canonical episode of being lost and found in the Temple ([[gospel-of-luke]] 2:41-52) which forms the closing scene (IGT 19).

Theological function: scholars debate whether IGT is essentially proto-orthodox (presenting a powerful Christ from infancy in line with developing high Christology, with the temperamental scenes serving as evidence of latent divinity tested by ignorant humans) or popular-edifying (drawing on Greco-Roman wonder-child topoi like the youthful Pythagoras or Apollonius of Tyana) or even subtly subversive. The earlier scholarly tendency to read IGT as gnostic has largely been abandoned; the text is now read as a popular vernacular Christian narrative with a complex and shifting moral economy.

Reception: enormously popular in medieval Christianity East and West, with manuscript distribution far exceeding any of the gnostic or apostolic-fatherly apocrypha. The IGT episodes — clay sparrows, schoolmasters, dyer's vat (in expanded recensions) — pass through Latin into vernacular literatures and inform the apocryphal infancy scenes in medieval mystery plays, in the Qur'an (Surah 3:49; 5:110, the clay-birds miracle), and in Renaissance Italian art. The work is one of the most widely-circulated and least-studied of the New Testament apocrypha; modern critical editions (Hock 1995, Aasgaard 2009) have only recently begun to do it textual-historical justice.
