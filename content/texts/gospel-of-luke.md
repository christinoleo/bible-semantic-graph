Third canonical Gospel, longest of the four (~19,500 words), first volume of the Luke-Acts diptych. Address to "most excellent Theophilus" (1:3; cf. [[acts]] 1:1) and the formal Hellenistic prologue (1:1-4) — declaring methodical investigation of prior accounts and orderly composition — situate the work as Greco-Roman historiography, not bare evangelical proclamation. Critical dating: ca. 80-100 CE.

Authorship: traditional ascription to [[luke]] "the beloved physician" (Col 4:14) and Pauline companion (the "we-passages" in [[acts]]) — defended by Irenaeus, the Muratorian fragment, the anti-Marcionite prologue. Critical scholarship variously treats the author as anonymous, possibly a Pauline associate at one remove but not necessarily an eyewitness; competence in Septuagintal Greek and Hellenistic rhetoric is taken to indicate an educated Gentile-Christian or Hellenistic-Jewish author. The medical-language argument (Hobart 1882) was decisively undermined by Cadbury.

Sources: [[gospel-of-mark]] (~35% of Luke, reordered and stylistically polished); Q sayings shared with [[gospel-of-matthew]]; substantial "L" material (infancy narrative, parables — Good Samaritan, Prodigal Son, Rich Man and Lazarus — Emmaus road appearance). The Farrer hypothesis treats Luke as drawing directly on Matthew, dispensing with Q.

Structure: prologue (1:1-4), infancy narrative paralleling [[john-the-baptist]] and [[jesus]] (1-2), Galilean ministry (3-9), the great "travel narrative" toward Jerusalem (9:51-19:27, containing most L-material), passion and resurrection in Jerusalem (19-24). Geography is theologically loaded: ministry moves inexorably toward Jerusalem; in Acts the trajectory reverses outward to Rome.

Lukan theology: programmatic concern for the poor and outcast (Magnificat 1:46-55; Beatitudes/Woes 6:20-26 — Luke's "blessed are you poor" against Matthew's spiritualizing "poor in spirit"); women as disciples and witnesses ([[mary-mother-of-jesus]], Elizabeth, Anna, Martha, Mary Magdalene, the women at the tomb); Holy Spirit (more references than Matthew/Mark combined); table fellowship as locus of salvation; universal scope (genealogy back to Adam, not just Abraham); prayer; joy.

Major figures: [[jesus]], [[mary-mother-of-jesus]] (centerpiece of infancy narrative), [[joseph-of-nazareth]], [[john-the-baptist]], [[peter]], [[pontius-pilate]], [[herod-antipas]].

Reception: source of the Christmas story (manger, shepherds, angels) and many of the most-recited parables in Western literature. Magnificat, Benedictus, and Nunc Dimittis canticles structure Christian daily office. Liberation theology (Gutiérrez, Sobrino) reads Luke as theological foundation for preferential option for the poor. Marcion (mid-2nd c.) used a truncated Luke as his sole Gospel, prompting orthodox defense of the fourfold canon.
