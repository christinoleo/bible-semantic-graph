Erotic love poetry — voices of a young woman and a young man celebrating mutual desire in dialogue and monologue, set against gardens, vineyards, urban streets, and royal courts. One of the five Megillot, read at Passover. The most thematically anomalous book in the Hebrew Bible: no mention of [[yhwh]] (depending on reading of Song 8:6), no covenant, no law, no narrative arc — only the language of erotic love.

Attribution to [[solomon]] in the superscription (Song 1:1) is honorific (Solomon as patron of poetry; cf. 1 Kgs 4:32 crediting him with 1,005 songs); critical scholarship treats this as later editorial framing and places composition variously across the Persian and Hellenistic periods (4th-3rd c. BCE), on linguistic grounds (late Hebrew, Persian loanwords, possibly Greek influence). Structural analyses range from "anthology of independent love poems" to "unified dramatic lyric" with two or three speaking voices.

Themes: mutual desire, female voice and agency (the woman speaks first and most), the language of love drawn from agriculture, perfumery, architecture, royal court, military imagery; the body imagined through repeated *wasf* (descriptive poems praising parts of the beloved's body); the natural world as erotic theater. Comparative work has shown extensive parallels with Egyptian love poetry of the New Kingdom.

The interpretive history is dominated by allegorical readings — necessary, perhaps, for the book's canonization. Rabbinic tradition (Rabbi Akiva: "the Song of Songs is the holy of holies") reads the love between the woman and the man as the love of Israel and [[yhwh]], elaborated in *Shir HaShirim Rabbah* and in Jewish mystical tradition. Patristic and medieval Christian reception reads the Song as Christ and the church (Origen) or Christ and the individual soul (Bernard of Clairvaux's 86 sermons on the first two chapters), or Christ and Mary (later medieval Marian readings).

Modern critical reception (since the 18th c.) has recovered the literal-erotic reading as primary, with allegory as later interpretive overlay; feminist scholarship has foregrounded the woman's voice and the book's near-egalitarian erotic theology, set against the patriarchal grain of much of the rest of the canon.
