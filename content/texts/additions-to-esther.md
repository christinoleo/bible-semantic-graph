Six Greek expansions interpolated into the Greek (LXX) text of [[book-of-esther]], absent from the Hebrew (Masoretic) Esther. Jerome, translating the Vulgate, extracted them from their narrative positions and relocated them as an appendix to Esther, where they were assigned the chapter numbers 10:4-16:24 in older Latin Bibles. Modern scholarship designates them by the letters A through F (the Göttingen LXX numbering, now standard in critical and most modern editions), restored to their LXX narrative positions:

- A — Mordecai's apocalyptic dream and his discovery of the eunuchs' plot (placed before MT 1:1)
- B — text of the king's first decree against the Jews (after MT 3:13)
- C — Mordecai's and Esther's prayers (after MT 4:17)
- D — Esther's audience before the king, with miraculous fainting and royal reversal (replacing MT 5:1-2)
- E — text of the king's counter-decree (after MT 8:12)
- F — Mordecai's interpretation of his dream, the etiology of Purim, and the colophon (after MT 10:3)

Date and composition. Additions A, C, D, and F are likely translated from a Hebrew or Aramaic Vorlage and may belong to the earliest stratum of the Greek Esther tradition; additions B and E (the two royal decrees) are composed in florid Hellenistic Greek and were almost certainly composed directly in Greek by the LXX translator or a near contemporary. The colophon (F, the closing verses) dates the Greek translation to "the fourth year of Ptolemy and Cleopatra" — most plausibly 78/77 BCE under Ptolemy XII Auletes and Cleopatra V, though other identifications (Ptolemy VIII, ca. 114 BCE; Ptolemy IX, ca. 78 BCE) have been defended. This colophon is one of the few precisely datable references to Septuagintal translation activity outside the Letter of Aristeas.

Theological function. The notorious absence of the divine name in Hebrew Esther — God is never mentioned, prayer is absent, miracles do not occur, and the deliverance unfolds through "coincidence" — was a theological scandal in the Hellenistic period. The Additions remedy this comprehensively. They name God repeatedly; supply prayers (Add C) in which Mordecai disclaims Haman's accusation that the Jews were disloyal to the king (he refused to bow only because no man should be honored as God) and Esther abjures pagan food, royal status, and Gentile bed; insert a theophanic moment in D where the king's face softens in answer to Esther's swoon; and frame the whole narrative within Mordecai's apocalyptic dream of two dragons (himself and Haman) at the springs of a torrent. The result is a thoroughly theologized Esther in which Purim is salvation by divine providence, not by uncanny fortune.

Reception and canon. The early church received the Greek Esther (with Additions) as scripture; [[origen]] worked with the longer text; [[jerome]] famously dispensed with the Additions in his Vulgate base text but appended them at the end with explanatory notes — a practice often misread by later generations as evidence that he treated them with full canonical respect. Hippo, Carthage, Florence, and Trent affirmed canonicity (in their relocated appendix form). Excluded from the Tanakh (which preserves Hebrew Esther alone). Reformers placed the Additions in the Apocrypha; the King James Apocrypha prints them as "The Rest of the Chapters of the Book of Esther which are found neither in the Hebrew, nor in the Chaldee." The textual situation is further complicated by the existence of a second, shorter Greek version (the Alpha Text / Lucianic Text) which also contains the Additions in distinct form, and which some scholars argue preserves an older Hebrew Esther independent of the MT.
