The most contested of the NT writings on authenticity grounds — widely regarded as the latest NT writing (commonly dated 100-150 CE) and as a clear case of pseudepigraphy in mainstream critical scholarship. Even relatively conservative critical voices (e.g., Bauckham) acknowledge that 2 Peter is pseudonymous while defending its theological legitimacy as testamentary genre. Conservative and confessional scholarship continues to defend Petrine authorship in the mid-60s CE.

Arguments against Petrine authorship: (1) markedly different Greek style and vocabulary from [[1-peter]] (already noted by Jerome and Origen); (2) extensive literary dependence on [[jude]] (in ch. 2 — virtually all of Jude is reused), making 2 Peter post-Jude; (3) reference to the Pauline letters as collected scripture ("there are some things in [the letters] hard to understand, which the ignorant and unstable twist as they do the other scriptures", 3:16) — presupposes a collected Pauline corpus and the elevation of those letters to scriptural status, both processes of the late 1st / early 2nd century; (4) the apologetic against "scoffers" who say "where is the promise of his coming?" (3:4) presupposes the parousia delay as a community-level theological problem — characteristic of second-generation Christianity; (5) the testamentary framing ("knowing that the putting off of my body will be soon", 1:14) is a known pseudepigraphic device. The external history is also telling: 2 Peter is the most disputed NT book in the early canon (Eusebius lists it among the antilegomena); it appears in no major canonical list before the 3rd century.

Structure: opening exhortation to godliness through divine power (1:3-11); apostolic witness and the prophetic word (1:12-21, with the transfiguration recalled, 1:16-18); polemic against false teachers (2 — closely paralleling [[jude]]); the parousia delay problem and the day of the Lord (3).

Key passages: 1:4 — "partakers of the divine nature" (θείας κοινωνοὶ φύσεως) — the most explicit NT formulation of theosis/deification, foundational for Eastern Orthodox soteriology. 1:19-21 — "no prophecy of scripture is a matter of one's own interpretation, for no prophecy ever came by the will of man, but men spoke from God being carried along by the Holy Spirit" — a classic biblical-inspiration proof-text, often paired with [[2-timothy]] 3:16. 3:8 — "with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" — the standard early-church explanation of the parousia delay.

The parousia-delay problem (3:1-13): scoffers mock the unfulfilled coming, arguing that "all things continue as they were from the beginning of creation". The author counters with (1) the flood as counter-example to uniformitarian assumption (3:5-7); (2) divine temporal scale not human (3:8); (3) divine patience as soteriologically motivated (3:9, "not wishing that any should perish but that all should reach repentance"); (4) the day will come "like a thief" with cosmic dissolution (3:10-13, "the heavens will pass away with a roar, and the heavenly bodies will be burned up").

Major figures: [[peter]] (author or claimed author), with allusion to [[paul]] ("our beloved brother Paul also wrote to you according to the wisdom given him", 3:15-16) — the only NT writing outside [[acts]] to explicitly mention Paul.

Reception: 1:4 anchors Eastern Orthodox theosis theology; 1:21 anchors Protestant inerrancy and verbal-inspiration doctrines; 3:10 fuels modern apocalyptic readings (though the textual variant "will be found" vs. "will be burned up" matters for ecological theology); the letter's canonical struggle is itself a key case in the history of canon formation.
