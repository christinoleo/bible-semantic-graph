Greek-language Jewish sapiential work pseudonymously attributed to [[solomon]], composed in Alexandria, most likely under early Roman rule (ca. 50 BCE - 50 CE; some scholars push later, to the reign of Caligula, on the basis of polemics against ruler-cult and zoolatry that fit Egyptian-Roman tensions). The book is among the most stylistically Hellenized texts in the broader biblical corpus — composed in cultured Greek with hexameter-sensitive rhythm, Stoic vocabulary, Platonic and Middle-Platonic categories, and rhetorical tropes drawn from contemporary Greek paideia — while remaining doggedly Jewish in content: Exodus is its midrashic backbone, idolatry its principal target, Torah-fidelity its end.

Authorship and date. Solomonic attribution is literary convention — the speaker frequently identifies himself as the king "whom you have chosen" (9:7) — but the Greek-rhetorical style, the absence of Solomon from the Alexandrian milieu, and the developed eschatology preclude historical Solomonic authorship. The unity of the book is contested (older criticism partitioned it into two or three sources of distinct authorship); the current consensus treats Wisdom as a single composition by a Hellenistic Jewish author writing for a bilingual Alexandrian Jewish audience under pressure from both Greek philosophy and Egyptian cult.

Structure: three discernible parts. Part I, "Book of Eschatology" (chs. 1-5) — exhortation to righteousness, the wicked counsel of the impious, immortality of the righteous (3:1: "the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God"), the judgment scene where the wicked confront the vindicated just one (chs. 4-5). Part II, "Book of Wisdom" (chs. 6-9) — Solomon's prayer for Wisdom, the famous encomium of Sophia (7:22-8:1: "for she is a breath of the power of God, and a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty… a reflection [apaugasma] of eternal light"), Solomon's marriage to Wisdom. Part III, "Book of History" (chs. 10-19) — Wisdom's providential leading from Adam through the Exodus, with extended diatribe against idolatry (chs. 13-15) — natural theology of God's invisible perfections inferred from creation (13:1-9), genealogy of idols, polemic against Egyptian zoolatry.

Theology. Personified Sophia is the book's signature contribution — present at creation, ordering all things, sought by Solomon as bride, identified with the spirit of the Lord. Her attributes in 7:22-26 (twenty-one in total: intelligent, holy, unique, manifold, subtle, mobile, clear, unpolluted, distinct, invulnerable, loving the good, keen, irresistible, beneficent, humane, steadfast, sure, free from anxiety, all-powerful, overseeing all, penetrating through all spirits) supply categories taken up by [[philo-of-alexandria|Philo]] for the Logos and by NT and patristic Christology for Christ. Wisdom 7:25-26 stands behind [[hebrews]] 1:3 (apaugasma tēs doxēs) and shapes pre-Nicene language of the Son as "radiance" and "exact imprint" of the Father.

Other innovations: explicit immortality of the soul, framed in Greek-philosophical (not bodily-resurrectional) idiom (3:1-9; 8:19-20 — Solomon receives a "good soul"); death as the work of the devil entering through envy (2:24, the first explicit identification of the Eden serpent with the devil); preexistent or quasi-preexistent souls implied (8:19-20); a universalist natural theology (13:1-9, the locus classicus quoted at length by Rom 1) holding that "from the greatness and beauty of created things comes a corresponding perception of their Creator," with culpability for failing to make the inference.

Reception. Cited or alluded to throughout the NT — Pauline polemic against idolatry (Rom 1) is virtually a midrash on Wisdom 13-15; the suffering righteous one of Wisdom 2:12-20 is heard in the Passion narratives; the Sophia Christology of [[hebrews]] 1:3 echoes Wisdom 7:25-26; [[james]]'s account of wisdom from above (Jas 3:13-17) tracks Wisdom's catalogue. [[augustine]] cited Wisdom hundreds of times — especially Wis 11:20 ("you have ordered all things by measure and number and weight") for his metaphysics of created order; medieval Christology, Mariology (Wisdom 7 read of the Virgin), and the cult of the Sacred Heart drew on Wisdom imagery. Hippo, Carthage, Florence, Trent affirmed; Reformers excluded.
