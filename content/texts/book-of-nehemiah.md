Continuation of [[book-of-ezra]] (one book in the Hebrew tradition). Mission of [[nehemiah]] the cupbearer-turned-governor under Artaxerxes I: wall-rebuilding, social reform, public Torah reading and covenant renewal.

Critical scholarship treats large blocks of Nehemiah as drawn from a "Nehemiah Memoir" — first-person material commonly considered authentic Persian-period source documents and among the most historically reliable narrative sections of the Hebrew Bible. Final redaction belongs to the same Ezra-Nehemiah project, in the late Persian or early Hellenistic period.

Structure: Nehemiah's commissioning by Artaxerxes, journey, and inspection of the ruined walls (Neh 1-2); wall-rebuilding under continuous opposition from Sanballat, Tobiah, and Geshem (Neh 3-6); list of returnees (Neh 7, paralleling Ezra 2); public Torah reading by [[ezra]], Festival of Booths, and covenant renewal (Neh 8-10); repopulation of Jerusalem (Neh 11-12); Nehemiah's second-term reforms — Sabbath enforcement, dismissal of foreign wives, expulsion of a Tobiah-affiliated priest (Neh 13).

Themes: walls as boundary marker (physical, social, ethnic), Torah as constitutional charter of the post-exilic community, public liturgy as identity formation, governance under imperial sponsorship, the cost of reform in the face of internal accommodation. Neh 8 is the textual founding moment for the rabbinic institution of public Torah reading.

Reception: in rabbinic tradition Nehemiah is somewhat overshadowed by Ezra, who is credited with the canonization of Torah; Christian reception often allegorizes wall-rebuilding as ecclesiastical restoration. Modern scholarship treats Nehemiah's first-person material as a crucial witness to Persian provincial administration and to the social construction of post-exilic Yehud.
