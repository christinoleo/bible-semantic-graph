Authorship unknown. The text is anonymous; the manuscript tradition's grouping with the Pauline corpus is editorial. Origen famously concluded "who wrote the epistle, only God knows" (Eusebius, HE 6.25). Mainstream critical and most modern conservative scholarship reject Pauline authorship on stylistic (the most polished Greek in the NT, classical periodic style), theological (high-priestly Christology has no Pauline parallel; the author groups himself with the second generation, 2:3 "attested to us by those who heard"), and procedural (no Pauline opening salutation) grounds. Candidates proposed across history: Apollos (Luther), Barnabas (Tertullian, North African tradition), Priscilla (Harnack — making the work the only NT writing by a woman, with anonymity explaining the masculine self-reference grammatically), [[luke]], Clement of Rome, Silas. No identification commands assensus.

Genre: not a letter but a "word of exhortation" (13:22, λόγος τῆς παρακλήσεως) — a homily or sermon, with a brief epistolary postscript (13:18-25) appended. The body alternates expository exposition and paraenetic application in five major movements.

Dating: most likely before 70 CE (the destruction of the Temple is nowhere mentioned, despite an argument that would have devastatingly served the author's polemic for the obsolescence of the sacrificial cult), though many critics place it 70-90 CE on grounds that the second-generation language and developed Christology require later composition. Use of the present tense for temple sacrifice ("they offer", "they bring") is most naturally pre-70, but the author quotes [[exodus]] / [[leviticus]] tabernacle ordinances (not Temple practice) which use present-tense throughout the LXX, weakening the inference.

Audience: Jewish-Christian community under pressure to revert to mainstream Judaism (whether from external persecution or internal exhaustion), addressed with "you have not yet resisted to the point of shedding blood" (12:4) and a recollection of an earlier persecution they had endured (10:32-34).

Structure: Christ superior to angels (1-2), superior to Moses and Joshua (3-4), Christ as high priest in the order of Melchizedek (5-7), better covenant and better sanctuary (8-9), better sacrifice (10), faith catalog (11), discipline and warning (12), final exhortations (13).

Central theological move: Christ as eternal high priest "in the order of [[melchizedek]]" (a figure from [[genesis]] 14, expanded via Ps 110:4), superseding the Levitical priesthood and rendering the [[leviticus]] cultus obsolete. The new-covenant citation of [[jeremiah]] 31 (Heb 8:8-12) is the longest OT quotation in the NT and structures the argument that "in speaking of a new covenant, he has made the first one obsolete" (8:13). Hebrews 9-10 reads Christ's death as the eschatological Day of Atonement: not the blood of bulls and goats but his own blood, not annually repeated but "once for all" (ἐφάπαξ, 7:27; 9:12, 26; 10:10).

Reception and the "supersessionism" problem: Hebrews supplies the NT's most explicit framework for reading the OT cultic system as superseded, and has been the single most influential text in Christian supersessionist theology — the view that the church replaces Israel and that the old covenant is abrogated. Post-Holocaust Christian theology (Soulen, Bader-Saye, others) has worked extensively to reread Hebrews against supersessionism — variously by treating it as intra-Jewish polemic among first-century Jews, by foregrounding the temporal sequence ("better" rather than "instead of"), or by accepting cultic supersessionism while rejecting the broader replacement of Israel.

Faith catalog (11): the "hall of faith" — [[abel]], [[noah]], [[abraham]], [[sarah]], [[isaac]], [[jacob]], [[joseph]], [[moses]], [[rahab]], [[gideon]], [[samson]], [[david]], [[samuel]], the prophets — among the most-preached chapters in Christian history.

Major figures (in the text's exposition): [[jesus]] as eternal high priest; [[moses]] (faithful servant in God's house, surpassed by Christ as son); [[melchizedek]] (the priest-king of [[genesis]] 14, type of Christ); the long [[hebrews]] 11 catalog.

Reception: foundational for Christian sacrificial soteriology and atonement theology; central in Reformation rejection of the Mass as repeated sacrifice (Heb 10:10, 12, 14); the [[melchizedek]] typology is foundational for Catholic priesthood theology and Mormon priesthood structure; "Jesus Christ the same yesterday and today and forever" (13:8) is among the most quoted NT verses.
