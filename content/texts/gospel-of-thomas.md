Coptic sayings-gospel preserved in full only in the Nag Hammadi Codex II (NHC II,2), discovered in upper Egypt in 1945. Consists of 114 numbered logia of [[jesus]] introduced by a prologue ("These are the secret sayings that the living Jesus spoke and Didymos Judas Thomas recorded") and a brief frame ascription to "Didymos Judas Thomas." Three earlier Greek fragments (P.Oxy. 1, 654, 655) preserve portions of the same work in Greek, dating ~200 CE — securing both the Greek original and the second-century composition window. Not in any canon.

Genre is distinct from the narrative Gospels: no birth narrative, no passion, no resurrection account, almost no narrative connective tissue. The 114 logia are dominical sayings, some clustered loosely by catchword or theme, presented as direct revelation rather than embedded in narrative. The closest formal parallel in the New Testament tradition is the reconstructed Q source, which is similarly a sayings collection — though Q is hypothetical and Thomas is documented.

Content overlap: roughly half the sayings have synoptic parallels (e.g., logion 20 = mustard-seed parable; logion 26 = speck/log; logion 96 = leaven-in-meal parable; logion 109 = treasure-hidden-in-field). The other half is unparalleled in the canonical Gospels — including some sayings (e.g., logion 97, the woman carrying a jar of meal that empties; logion 98, the man who tested his strength by driving his sword through a wall) considered by some scholars (Crossan, the Jesus Seminar) plausibly to preserve authentic early dominical material. Where Thomas's version of a saying parallels Matthew or Luke, it is often shorter and more aphoristic, suggesting (to maximalists like Koester) an early stratum, or (to minimalists like Meier) a later abbreviating tendency.

Theological cast: the sayings press toward an interior, gnosis-oriented Christology. The Kingdom is "spread out upon the earth and men do not see it" (logion 113), is "inside of you and outside of you" (logion 3), is to be found by the one who recognizes the self ("If you bring forth what is within you, what you bring forth will save you," logion 70). Several logia (logion 30 on the divided self, logion 11 on the dead and the living, logion 49-50 on the elect from the light) employ vocabulary that aligns with later second-century gnostic systems, while others read as straightforwardly Jewish-wisdom or Jewish-Christian.

Provenance and date: the prevailing range is 80-150 CE, with significant scholarly disagreement. The maximalist case (Davies, Patterson, DeConick) treats the core as pre-70 Jewish-Christian sayings tradition with later layers; the minimalist case (Goodacre, Gathercole, Meier) treats the work as a mid-to-late-2nd-c. composition systematically dependent on the canonical Gospels. The textual data — substantial differences in saying-form, occasional verbatim agreements with Matthew or Luke against Mark — admit either reading.

The "Thomas community" hypothesis (Koester, Patterson) posits a distinct Syrian Christian community centered on Thomas-tradition (cf. *Acts of Thomas*, *Book of Thomas the Contender*) that valued sayings of [[jesus]] over narrative gospel-form. The polemical posture of [[gospel-of-john]] (with its diminished Thomas figure of John 20:24-29) and of Thomas (with its critical view of Petrine and Matthean authority figures, logion 13) is sometimes read as inter-communal Christian dispute in late-1st / early-2nd c. Syria.

Reception: known to [[hippolytus]] and [[origen]] by title and partial quotation; condemned as heretical in patristic catalogues; lost to direct knowledge in the West for ~1500 years until the Oxyrhynchus papyri and the Nag Hammadi discovery; one of the most studied early-Christian non-canonical texts since 1945, central to debates about the formation of Jesus-tradition, the synoptic problem, the date and shape of the gospel-canon, and the diversity of early Christianity.
