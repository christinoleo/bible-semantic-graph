Diaspora novella, composed ca. 225-175 BCE, set fictively in 8th-c. Nineveh after the Assyrian deportation of the northern tribes. Pious [[tobit]] of the tribe of Naphtali, blinded by sparrow droppings while sleeping in his courtyard after burying a fellow Israelite, sends his son [[tobias]] to recover a deposit in Media. The angel [[raphael-archangel]], disguised as the kinsman "Azarias," escorts Tobias; en route Tobias catches a fish whose organs become exorcistic and ophthalmic remedies. In Ecbatana Tobias marries [[sarah-of-tobit]] — not Abraham's [[sarah]] — whose seven prior bridegrooms had been slain on the wedding night by the demon Asmodeus; Raphael drives Asmodeus to "the remotest parts of Egypt" with the burnt fish-heart and liver. The party returns; Tobias' fish-gall restores Tobit's sight; Raphael reveals himself.

Original language disputed until Qumran. Four Aramaic and one Hebrew fragment of Tobit were recovered from Cave 4 (4Q196-200), settling that the book circulated in both Semitic languages in the late Second-Temple period; the Greek Long Recension (Sinaiticus) tracks the Aramaic closely, while the Short Recension (Vaticanus/Alexandrinus) compresses it. Jerome translated the Vulgate Tobit from a now-lost Aramaic text in a single night, by his own report.

Generically a hybrid: Hellenistic-era romance, folktale (Stith Thompson type 507A, "The Grateful Dead"), didactic wisdom miscellany, and halakhic exemplum. Tobit's burial of Israelite corpses (1:17-19; 2:3-8) and Sarah's endogamous marriage (6:12; 7:10) embody Torah piety in Diaspora. The book's ethical core is the "Two Ways" instruction of Tobit to Tobias (ch. 4): almsgiving rescues from death, honor your mother, marry within the kin, the Golden Rule in its earliest extant negative form (4:15 — "what you hate, do to no one"). The closing hymn (ch. 13) anticipates eschatological Jerusalem rebuilt of sapphire and gold, motif later in [[revelation]] 21.

Theology: hidden providence — Raphael operates incognito throughout, revealed only at the end (12:11-22) as "one of the seven angels who stand ready and enter before the glory of the Lord," seeding the developed angelology of [[1-enoch]] and later Jewish and Christian tradition. The named demon Asmodeus (from Persian Aeshma-daeva) is the OT's most personalized evil spirit. Prayer, almsgiving, and burial of the dead are framed as efficacious works — a passage Catholic and Orthodox traditions cite for the theology of merit and intercession, and which the Reformers contested.

Canonical history: included in the LXX and Vulgate; cited approvingly in patristic literature ([[polycarp]], [[clement-of-alexandria]], [[cyprian]], [[augustine]]); affirmed at Hippo (393), Carthage (397), and Trent (1546). Jerome flagged its non-Hebrew (i.e., non-Tanakh) status but translated it under episcopal pressure. Excluded from the Tanakh and from Protestant canons at the Reformation, though Luther kept it in the Apocrypha as "useful and good to read." The Qumran finds have softened older claims that Tobit was a purely Hellenistic Greek composition.

Reception: Tobias and the fish became a popular subject in Christian iconography (Verrocchio, Rembrandt, Pieter Lastman); Raphael as patron of travelers, the sick, and matchmakers derives from this book. The wedding-night prayer of Tobias and Sarah (Tob 8:5-7) is incorporated into Catholic and Orthodox marriage liturgies. Modern reception emphasizes Tobit as a Diaspora ethics text alongside [[book-of-esther]] and [[book-of-daniel]] court tales.
