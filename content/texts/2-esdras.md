Composite Latin work of three layers: a Christian prologue (chs. 1-2, "5 Ezra"), a Jewish apocalyptic core (chs. 3-14, "4 Ezra" proper), and a Christian appendix (chs. 15-16, "6 Ezra"). The middle stratum is one of the most theologically searching Jewish apocalypses to survive, written in response to the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE. The book is excluded from the Catholic canon (relegated to the Vulgate appendix by Trent), absent from Greek and Slavonic Orthodox, present in Ethiopian Tewahedo, and read but uncanonical in the Anglican and Russian Orthodox traditions.

The Jewish core is pseudepigraphically attributed to [[ezra]] but set fictively "thirty years after the destruction of our city" — i.e., 557 BCE on the narrative surface, but transparently 100 CE in real reference, with Babylon = Rome. Seven visions structure the work: three dialogues with the angel Uriel on theodicy (3:1-9:25); three symbolic visions — the mourning woman who becomes Zion (9:26-10:59), the eagle from the sea (chs. 11-12, reworking [[book-of-daniel]] 7), and the man from the sea (ch. 13); a final commissioning vision in which Ezra dictates 24 public + 70 secret books (ch. 14), an etiology for the Hebrew canon and esoteric apocalyptic literature.

The dialogues press hard on theodicy: if God's people suffer and the wicked prosper, what does election mean? Uriel's answers — that the present age is corrupt, that the few will be saved while the many perish, that human reason cannot fathom divine judgment — sit in unresolved tension with Ezra's protests, in a way unusual for the genre. Some scholars (Stone, Najman) read the dialogues as the author's own struggle dramatized, with Ezra's eventual reconciliation in the visions enacting a literary-theological resolution.

Original language was almost certainly Hebrew or Aramaic, lost; the surviving witnesses are Latin (the most complete), Syriac, Ethiopic, Armenian, Georgian, Arabic (two versions), and Coptic fragments. The Greek translation that mediated most of these is itself lost. The "missing fragment" between 7:35 and 7:36 in many Latin manuscripts was restored from the Amiatinus tradition in the 19th century.

The Christian framing chapters (1-2, 15-16) are independent compositions (~2nd-3rd c. CE) that reorient the work: chs. 1-2 announce God's rejection of Israel in favor of the Gentile church and feature a vision of the saints on Mount Zion (cf. [[revelation]] 7); chs. 15-16 are oracular prophecies of woe on the nations.

Reception: 4 Ezra was hugely influential on medieval Christian eschatology — Columbus quoted 6:42 ("six parts thou hast dried up") to argue the Earth was mostly land — and on Reformation debates about apocrypha. The pessimistic anthropology ("a grain of evil seed was sown in Adam's heart from the beginning," 4:30) is sometimes cited as a Jewish parallel to Pauline original-sin theology, though the comparison is contested.
