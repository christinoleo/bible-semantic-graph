Greek Jewish historiographical work covering the period from [[josiah]]'s Passover (621 BCE) through [[ezra]]'s reading of the Law in Jerusalem (mid-5th c. BCE). Title is unstable across traditions: "1 Esdras" in modern English (and in the Greek Septuagint as Esdras A), "3 Esdras" in the Latin Vulgate appendix where it sits alongside Vulgate "4 Esdras" (= [[2-esdras]]) outside the Catholic canon proper. Excluded by Trent from the Catholic canon; included in the Greek and Slavonic Orthodox canon and in Ethiopian Tewahedo.

Roughly nine-tenths of the book is parallel to portions of [[2-chronicles]] 35-36, [[book-of-ezra]], and [[book-of-nehemiah]] 7:38-8:12, in a divergent Greek recension. The textual relationship is contested: some scholars (Cross, Pohlmann) treat 1 Esdras as preserving a more primitive Greek form of a Hebrew/Aramaic Chronicler's history, others (Williamson) as a secondary compilation drawing on the canonical Ezra-Nehemiah.

The unique material is the "Tale of the Three Bodyguards" (1 Esd 3:1-5:6): three young guards in [[darius-i]]'s court debate what is strongest — wine, the king, or women — with a fourth answer, "truth," winning. The victor turns out to be [[zerubbabel]], who is then granted permission to return to Jerusalem and rebuild. The wisdom-contest genre is at home in Persian-period Jewish court tales (cf. [[book-of-daniel]] 1-6, [[book-of-esther]]); the framing harmonizes the postexilic return with sapiential rhetoric.

The book reorders events relative to canonical Ezra-Nehemiah, omits [[nehemiah]]'s memoir entirely, and ends mid-sentence in the Greek (8:96 / 9:55), suggesting either deliberate truncation or a damaged exemplar. [[josephus]] in *Antiquities* 11 follows 1 Esdras rather than canonical Ezra for the return-period narrative, a key datum for its Second-Temple-era circulation and authority.

Dating is debated: a Maccabean-era Greek composition (~150-100 BCE) is the most common estimate, though some posit a Hellenistic Egyptian provenance. The Aramaic stratum in canonical Ezra (4:8-6:18; 7:12-26) is rendered into Greek here, providing comparative evidence for Septuagint translation technique on Aramaic source material.

Reception: cited liberally by early church fathers ([[origen]], [[jerome]], [[augustine]]) under the title "Esdras" without clear distinction from canonical Ezra-Nehemiah, contributing to lasting numbering confusion. Jerome included it in the Vulgate appendix while denying its canonicity; Trent (1546) affirmed this exclusion for the Roman canon. Cranmer's Articles list it among books the Church of England reads "for example of life and instruction of manners."
