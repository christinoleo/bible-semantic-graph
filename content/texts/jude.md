Short, intense polemical letter (25 verses, single chapter) by "Jude (Ἰούδας), a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James" (v. 1). The self-identification points to [[jude-brother-of-jesus]] (the brother of the Lord, brother of [[james-brother-of-jesus]] — cf. Mt 13:55; Mk 6:3). Authenticity debate: defenders accept authorship by Jude the Lord's brother (mid-1st c.); critics judge the developed concept of "the faith once for all delivered to the saints" (v. 3) and the polished Hellenistic Greek incompatible with a Galilean Jewish-Christian, dating ca. 80-100 CE as pseudepigraphal. Mainstream critical scholarship divides; Bauckham (1983) makes the most extensive defense of authenticity.

Occasion: the author had intended to write about "our common salvation" but is forced to write urgently to "contend for the faith once for all delivered to the saints" (v. 3) because false teachers have infiltrated the community. The opponents are characterized as immoral (turning grace into license, v. 4), denying "our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ" (v. 4), "defiling the flesh, rejecting authority, and reviling glorious ones" (v. 8), engaging in self-aggrandizing speech (v. 16), being "worldly, devoid of the Spirit" (v. 19). Identification of these opponents is debated: libertine proto-Gnostics, Jewish-Christian itinerant prophets, syncretistic teachers — but no specific doctrinal system is named.

Structure: salutation (1-2); occasion and call to contend (3-4); three-part triplet pattern: paradigmatic divine judgments on past rebels (5-7, the wilderness generation, the fallen angels, Sodom and Gomorrah); woes and present analogues (8-13, Cain, Balaam, Korah); citation of Enoch and prediction of judgment (14-16); apostolic prophecy and exhortation to the faithful (17-23); doxology (24-25).

Citations of Jewish pseudepigrapha: Jude is the only NT writing to explicitly cite extra-canonical Jewish apocalyptic literature as prophetic. Jude 9 ("the archangel Michael, contending with the devil, was disputing about the body of Moses…") draws on the Assumption of Moses (a now-fragmentary pseudepigraphon). Jude 14-15 explicitly quotes 1 Enoch (1:9): "Behold, the Lord comes with ten thousands of his holy ones, to execute judgment on all…" The Enoch citation poses a long-running canonical puzzle: Jude treats Enoch as authoritative prophecy, but later Christian canon tradition (apart from the Ethiopian Orthodox Church, which canonizes 1 Enoch) does not. The puzzle is one factor in early-church reservations about Jude's canonical status (mentioned as disputed by Eusebius).

The Michael / Moses tradition (v. 9) is similarly extra-biblical. These two citations contributed to historical canonical doubt and continue to fuel debates about the relation of canonical and extracanonical literature in early Christianity.

Doxology (vv. 24-25): "Now to him who is able to keep you from stumbling and to present you blameless before the presence of his glory with great joy, to the only God, our Savior, through Jesus Christ our Lord, be glory, majesty, dominion, and authority, before all time and now and forever. Amen." Among the most-quoted benedictions in Christian liturgy.

Relation to [[2-peter]]: 2 Peter 2 reuses extensive material from Jude (paradigmatic judgments, Balaam, false teachers' immorality, water/sea metaphors) with verbal parallels too close for coincidence. Direction of dependence almost universally 2 Peter on Jude (Jude's tighter argument and Enoch citation, which 2 Peter omits, suggests 2 Peter sanitized the borrowing).

Major figures: [[jude-brother-of-jesus]] (claimed author), [[james-brother-of-jesus]] (named in the address as the author's brother — implicitly legitimizing the author by association), Michael the archangel, Enoch (the prophet), Cain, Balaam, Korah, Adam (in the Enochic citation).

Reception: relatively neglected in the lectionary and homiletical tradition (Catholic lectionary uses Jude only briefly); the doxology has substantial liturgical use; the Enoch citation is a major historical-canonical case study in early Christian use of extracanonical literature.
