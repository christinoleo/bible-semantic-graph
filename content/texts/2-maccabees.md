Greek-language epitome of a lost five-volume history by Jason of Cyrene (2 Macc 2:23). Despite its title, 2 Maccabees is not a sequel to [[1-maccabees]] but a parallel account, narrower in chronological scope (ca. 180-161 BCE — from [[onias-iii]] to the defeat of Nicanor), with sharply different theological register: explicitly providential, miraculous, martyrological, and apocalyptically inflected. Where 1 Maccabees is a sober dynastic chronicle, 2 Maccabees is a pathos-laden Hellenistic "pathetic history" in the manner of Polybius' enemies — Phylarchus, Duris — written to move and instruct.

Composition. Original Greek (not a translation from Hebrew, unlike 1 Maccabees), late 2nd c. BCE, almost certainly Alexandrian. Genre: the epitomator's prologue (2:19-32) and epilogue (15:37-39) frame the work in unmistakably Hellenistic-historiographic terms — selection of material, concession to readers' fatigue, the metaphor of building. The work is prefaced by two embedded festival letters from Jerusalem Jews to Egyptian Jews (1:1-2:18) urging observance of Hanukkah, datable on internal grounds to 124 and (possibly) 164 BCE.

Structure: prefatory letters (1:1-2:18); preface of the epitomator (2:19-32); Heliodorus' attempted Temple robbery and miraculous repulsion (ch. 3); Hellenizing intrigues under [[onias-iii]], Jason, and Menelaus (chs. 4-5); martyrdom narratives (chs. 6-7) — the elderly scribe [[eleazar-maccabeus]] tortured to death rather than eat pork, and the mother and her seven sons tortured serially while professing resurrection; campaigns of Judas Maccabeus (chs. 8-15), ending at Nicanor's defeat.

Theology and innovations. 2 Maccabees contains the OT's most explicit articulations of (i) creatio ex nihilo (7:28 — "God made [the heavens, earth, and all that is in them] out of things that did not exist"), (ii) bodily resurrection of the righteous (7:9, 11, 14, 23, 29; 12:43-45; 14:46), (iii) intercessory prayer of the saints in heaven (15:12-16: deceased Onias and Jeremiah pray for the living), and (iv) prayer and atonement for the dead (12:39-45: Judas' offering for fallen soldiers found wearing idolatrous amulets). The 12:39-45 pericope is the central Catholic prooftext for purgatory and prayers for the dead; the [[council-of-trent]] cited it explicitly against Reformers, while the Reformers' rejection of the deuterocanon is functionally inseparable from their rejection of these theological claims. The seven-brothers narrative (ch. 7) is the foundational martyrology of Western literature — the immediate model for 4 Maccabees, [[hebrews]] 11:35 ("women received their dead by resurrection; others were tortured, refusing to accept release"), and Christian martyrological tradition through the patristic period.

Themes: Temple as cosmic center; sin (Hellenization, priestly corruption) provokes divine pedagogical chastisement, not abandonment; martyrdom as vicarious efficacy and resurrection guarantee; piety expressed in liturgical observance (the book is the prime source for the institution of Hanukkah and the Nicanor's Day festival); active angelology — heavenly cavalry appears in battle (3:24-26; 10:29-30; 11:8). Where 1 Maccabees credits Hasmonean swords, 2 Maccabees credits God acting through them, with frequent epiphanies and visions.

Canonical history. LXX, Vulgate; affirmed at Hippo, Carthage, Florence, Trent. The book's distinctive theology — resurrection, prayers for the dead, intercession of saints, creatio ex nihilo as scriptural — is precisely what made its canonical status load-bearing for medieval Catholic and Orthodox doctrine and load-bearing in the opposite direction for the Reformers, who excluded it. Eastern Orthodox tradition includes it without controversy; 3 and 4 Maccabees, considered separately, have varying Orthodox reception (3 Maccabees in Eastern Orthodox canon; 4 Maccabees as a Greek appendix; both excluded by Rome and the Reformers). The Ethiopian Orthodox "Meqabyan" books are independent works unrelated to either 1 or 2 Maccabees.
