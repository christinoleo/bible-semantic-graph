General epistle traditionally ascribed to [[peter]], addressed to "the chosen exiles of the Dispersion" in five Roman provinces of northern Asia Minor (Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, Bithynia — 1:1). Sent "from Babylon" (5:13) — almost universally read as Roman Christian code for Rome (cf. [[revelation]]'s use of "Babylon" for Rome).

Authenticity debate: divided. Arguments against direct authorship by Peter the apostle: (1) polished Greek and rhetorical sophistication beyond what is plausible for a Galilean fisherman, even allowing for some Greek (the appeal to amanuensis Silvanus, 5:12, is the traditional explanation); (2) extensive use of the Septuagint rather than Hebrew OT; (3) the addressed provinces overlap with Pauline mission territory (Galatia, Asia), and the letter's idiom is more Pauline-adjacent than Petrine-distinct; (4) the persecution context (4:12-19, "the fiery ordeal") fits later periods (Domitian or Trajan) better than Nero. Arguments for authenticity: (1) Silvanus ([[silas]]) as secretary plausibly explains the Greek; (2) the letter shows distinct theology, not derivative Paulinism; (3) the persecution may be informal local hostility (no imperial edict need be presupposed); (4) external attestation is early and unanimous (2 Pet 3:1 references it; Polycarp; Papias). Mainstream critical scholarship leans pseudepigraphic, dating ca. 75-95 CE; confessional and conservative scholarship defends authentic Petrine authorship in the early 60s.

Structure: salutation and trinitarian blessing (1:1-12); call to holiness and the imperishable inheritance (1:13-2:10, with extensive baptismal imagery — "born anew", "newborn infants", "living stones"); household and civic conduct in a hostile world (2:11-3:12, including submission to authorities, the household code with slaves and wives, Christ as suffering exemplar 2:21-25); responding to undeserved suffering (3:13-4:19, including the Spirit-preaching-to-spirits passage 3:18-22); pastoral exhortations to elders and the flock (5:1-11); closing greetings (5:12-14).

Major themes: Christian identity as resident-alien "exiles" (πάροικοι, παρεπίδημοι, 1:1, 17; 2:11) in a hostile world; the suffering Christ as paradigm for Christian suffering (esp. 2:21-25, the most extended NT meditation on the [[book-of-isaiah]] 53 servant); the church as eschatological Israel (2:9-10, "you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession" — citing language from [[exodus]] 19 and [[book-of-isaiah]]); baptismal renewal (3:21, "baptism, which corresponds to this, now saves you").

The "spirits in prison" (3:18-22): one of the most opaque passages in the NT — "[Christ] was put to death in the flesh but made alive in the spirit, in which he went and proclaimed to the spirits in prison, who formerly did not obey when the patience of God waited in the days of Noah". Three major readings: (1) Christ's descent to the underworld between death and resurrection to preach to dead generations (patristic and Catholic tradition; basis of the descensus clause in the Apostles' Creed); (2) the pre-incarnate Christ preaching through Noah to the antediluvian generation (Augustine, Calvin); (3) the post-resurrection ascending Christ proclaiming victory to fallen angels (cf. 1 Enoch tradition — Selwyn, Dalton, mainstream modern reading). 4:6 extends the puzzle ("the gospel was preached even to the dead").

Reception: structures Christian theology of martyrdom and persecution; 2:9 ("a royal priesthood") is the Reformation locus classicus for the priesthood of all believers; 3:18-22 underwrites the harrowing-of-hell tradition in patristic and medieval theology; the household codes (2:18-3:7) shape Christian marriage and slavery doctrine. Often called "the most beautifully written" NT letter for its rhetorical polish.

Major figures: [[peter]] (author or claimed author), [[silas]]/Silvanus (5:12, scribe), [[mark]] (5:13, "my son").
