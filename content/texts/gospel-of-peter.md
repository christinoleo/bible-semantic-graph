Fragmentary passion-resurrection narrative pseudepigraphically attributed to the apostle [[peter]], pseudonymously authored ("I, Simon Peter…" 14:60). The principal surviving witness is the Akhmim fragment (P. Cair. 10759), a parchment codex of the late 8th or early 9th c. discovered in 1886-87 in a monk's grave at Akhmim, Egypt, containing what appears to be a passion-resurrection extract from a larger work. Two smaller papyrus fragments (P.Oxy. 2949, possibly P.Oxy. 4009) may also belong to it. Not in any canon.

The extant Akhmim text begins mid-sentence with the hand-washing scene before [[pontius-pilate]], moves through the trial (with [[herod-antipas]] active in the condemnation alongside Pilate — a feature distinctive to this gospel), the crucifixion, the burial by [[joseph-of-arimathea]], the posting of guards (elaborated beyond [[gospel-of-matthew]] 27:62-66), and a visible resurrection in which two angels and a giant Christ-figure emerge from the tomb followed by a walking, talking cross. The narrative breaks off at the calling of [[peter]] and Andrew to fish.

Theology: marked by what early critics called docetism — the doctrine that Christ only seemed to suffer. The cry from the cross is rendered "My Power, my Power, why hast thou forsaken me?" (5:19, distinct from [[gospel-of-matthew]]/[[gospel-of-mark]]'s "My God, my God") and immediately follows "and he was taken up," language that for [[serapion-of-antioch]] (~190 CE) in the patristic report transmitted by [[eusebius]] suggested a separation of the divine Christ from the human Jesus at the crucifixion. The book's resurrection narrative — visible to the guards, with a cosmic stature ("the heads of the two reached to heaven, but his who was led by them surpassed the heavens") — sits closer to apocalyptic vision than to the canonical resurrection accounts.

Anti-Jewish polarization is sharper than in any canonical Gospel: "the Lord" (a recurring substitution for "Jesus") is condemned by "the Jews" rather than by Roman judicial process; [[pontius-pilate]] is exonerated as much as possible; [[herod-antipas]] and the Jewish authorities bear primary blame ("then the Jews and the elders and the priests, perceiving what great evil they had done to themselves, began to lament," 7:25). This intensified anti-Judaism is one of the markers of its post-70 polemical context.

Composition: 2nd c. CE, debated whether early-2nd (Crossan, who controversially argued an underlying "Cross Gospel" pre-dating the canonical passion narratives) or mid-to-late-2nd (the mainstream view, treating the work as a creative synthesis and dramatic expansion of canonical material, with Matthew the principal source). Crossan's hypothesis is largely unaccepted; the dependence-on-canonical-Gospels reading is consensus.

Reception: Serapion of Antioch, visiting the church at Rhossus, initially permitted the book's use but withdrew permission after reading it carefully ("most was of the right teaching of the Saviour, but some things were added") and condemned it as docetic (Eusebius *Hist. eccl.* 6.12.3-6). The story is the earliest preserved discussion of canonical-discernment in patristic literature and a key moment in the formation of the New Testament canon. The work disappeared from circulation after Serapion's verdict and was lost until the Akhmim discovery. Its 1892 publication renewed scholarly interest in the relationship between canonical and non-canonical Gospel traditions.
