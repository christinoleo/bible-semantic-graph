Five acrostic poems mourning the destruction of Jerusalem by Babylon (586 BCE). Hebrew title ʾÊḵāh ("How…?") taken from the first word; LXX/Vulgate title Θρῆνοι / Threni ("Dirges"). In the Hebrew canon placed among the [[ketuvim|Writings]] as one of the Five Scrolls (Megillot); the Christian canon follows the LXX in attaching it to [[book-of-jeremiah]].

Chs. 1, 2, 4 are each 22-verse alphabetic acrostics (one ʾāleph-to-tāw cycle). Ch. 3 triples the acrostic (three verses per letter, 66 verses total). Ch. 5 is non-acrostic but retains 22 lines, suggesting the alphabet still governs its shape. The qinah meter (3+2 stress pattern) dominates — the characteristic Hebrew funeral cadence.

Traditional attribution to [[jeremiah]] derives from [[2-chronicles]] 35:25 and the LXX preface, plus the eyewitness intensity of the destruction described. Modern critical consensus treats the book as anonymous, composed shortly after 586 by one or more poets in Judah (not in exile — the perspective is in-country, surveying ruins). Linguistic and theological features (e.g., the speaker of ch. 3 reads as a representative figure rather than a known biographical Jeremiah) tell against the Jeremianic ascription.

Themes: theodicy, deserved-but-disproportionate judgment, divine abandonment, the silencing of cultic and royal institutions, raw lament without resolution. The book never closes its grief — ch. 5 ends interrogatively ("unless you have utterly rejected us…"). The famous central confession of hope (3:22-24, "his mercies are new every morning") is bracketed by anguish on both sides, not a triumphant midpoint.

Reception: read liturgically on Tisha b'Av in Jewish tradition, mourning the destruction of both Temples. Christian liturgy uses portions during Holy Week (Tenebrae offices). A foundational text for theologies of lament, post-Shoah Jewish theology, and modern protest-of-suffering readings (Brueggemann, Linafelt, O'Connor). Comparative ancient Near Eastern parallels include the Sumerian city-laments (Lament for Ur, Lament for Sumer and Ur).
