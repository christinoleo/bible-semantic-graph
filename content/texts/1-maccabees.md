Sober Hasmonean dynastic history covering ca. 175-134 BCE, from the accession of [[antiochus-iv-epiphanes]] to the death of [[simon-maccabeus]] and the accession of John Hyrcanus I. The book chronicles the persecution under Antiochus IV (forcible Hellenization, prohibition of Torah, the abomination of desolation in the Temple, 167 BCE); the priestly revolt of [[mattathias]] at Modein; the campaigns of his sons [[judas-maccabeus]] (164: Temple rededicated, the etiology of Hanukkah, 4:36-59), [[jonathan-maccabeus]] (high priest, 152-142), and [[simon-maccabeus]] (independent rule, 142-134); and concludes with Simon's assassination at Dok.

Composition. Original language Hebrew (lost), translated into Greek; survives as LXX 1 Maccabees. Composed late 2nd or early 1st c. BCE in Jerusalem, almost certainly under the patronage of the Hasmonean court — most likely under John Hyrcanus I (135-104 BCE) or his sons. Genre: emulation of the Deuteronomistic and Chronistic historiographic style — annalistic, decree-citing (it preserves verbatim royal letters, treaty texts, Roman correspondence, 1 Macc 8; 12; 14-15), terse, restrained. Greek translation likely Alexandrian, before Josephus, who paraphrases the book at length in Antiquities 12-13.

Theology and politics. 1 Maccabees is the central apologetic text for the Hasmonean dynasty's combined high-priestly and royal authority — an unusual fusion (the Hasmoneans were Aaronic but not Zadokite, and not Davidic), defended here by appeal to zeal, military success, and divine vindication. The book is pointedly non-apocalyptic and non-miraculous: God acts through Maccabean swords, not visions; "heaven" replaces "God" as the agent-noun (a periphrasis avoiding the Tetragrammaton); there is no resurrection, no angelic warfare, no martyrdom theology of vicarious atonement. Compare and contrast [[2-maccabees]], which retells overlapping material with explicit miracles, named angels, resurrection of the just, and a different theological register.

Structure: introduction (1-2) — Alexander, his successors, Antiochus' decrees, the desecration, Mattathias' revolt and deathbed exhortation; Judas' wars (3-9:22) — early victories, purification of the Temple, expanding campaigns, death at Elasa; Jonathan (9:23-12:53) — alliances with Sparta and Rome, high-priestly investiture; Simon (13-16) — independence, treaty with Demetrius II, the "peaceful" reign, assassination by his son-in-law Ptolemy at Dok and Hyrcanus' succession.

Themes: zeal (qin'a) as covenantal virtue — Mattathias is figured as a new [[phinehas]] (1 Macc 2:26, 54), Judas as a new [[joshua]] / [[david]]; Torah-fidelity as identity marker under empire; covenantal continuity through martial sanctity; the "kingdom" of Israel rebuilt by armed defense rather than apocalyptic intervention. The deathbed catalogue of patriarchal exempla in 1 Macc 2:51-60 is a compressed canonical theology of faithful endurance.

Canonical history: in the LXX, Vulgate, and Peshitta; cited by [[origen]] and [[augustine]]; affirmed at Carthage and Trent. Excluded from the Tanakh — likely because rabbinic Judaism's break with the Hasmoneans (and later with the Sadducean-priestly establishment) made dynastic apologetic unwelcome; the Hanukkah festival, however, was retained, with its etiology preserved orally and in the Talmud (Shabbat 21b) rather than in 1 Maccabees itself. Reformers excluded as Apocrypha; the historical core remains a primary source for any modern reconstruction of the Maccabean revolt, often read in tandem with [[2-maccabees]], Josephus, and the Qumran evidence.
