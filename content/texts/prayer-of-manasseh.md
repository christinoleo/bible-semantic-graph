Brief penitential prayer of fifteen verses attributed pseudepigraphically to king [[manasseh]] of Judah. The composition expands [[2-chronicles]] 33:18-19, which mentions but does not record the prayer Manasseh offered during his Assyrian captivity — a textual gap that invited later supplementation. Included in the Greek and Slavonic Orthodox canon and in Ethiopian Tewahedo; placed in the Vulgate appendix by Jerome and confirmed there (not in the canon proper) by Trent; printed among the Apocrypha in Anglican and Lutheran Bibles.

Structure follows classical Jewish penitential prayer form: invocation with divine epithets and attributes (vv. 1-4), appeal to God's mercy with reference to the patriarchs (vv. 5-8), confession of personal sin in stark superlative ("I have sinned more than the number of the sand of the sea," v. 9), petition for forgiveness (vv. 11-13), and doxological close (vv. 14-15). The verse "you have promised repentance and forgiveness to those who have sinned against you" (v. 7) became a key proof-text for the universal availability of repentance in patristic and rabbinic-era Jewish thought.

Origin: a Jewish composition of the late Second-Temple period (~2nd-1st c. BCE), almost certainly in Greek (the elegant Septuagintal style argues against a translated Hebrew original, though a Hebrew Vorlage cannot be ruled out). It does not appear in the LXX uncials Vaticanus or Sinaiticus; its earliest Greek attestation is in the Didascalia Apostolorum (3rd c. CE) and the Apostolic Constitutions (~380 CE), both of which embed the full text within a context urging Christian repentance — likely the channel through which it entered Christian liturgical use.

The historical [[manasseh]] (~697-643 BCE) is portrayed in [[2-kings]] 21 as Judah's worst apostate king, executed by Assyrian-style ritual deportation in [[2-chronicles]] 33, where Chronicles uniquely adds a repentance and restoration not present in Kings — a redactional softening that the prayer's author exploits. The Chronicler's pro-Davidic theology required even the worst Davidide be redeemable; this prayer supplies the missing textual evidence.

Liturgical use: a fixture of the Byzantine Great Compline service throughout Lent, of Roman pre-Vatican II Tenebrae, of Anglican Forms of Prayer for the Dying, and of Lutheran penitential rites. Its terseness and emotional directness — appropriate for a sinner's confession without metrical or doxological clutter — make it functionally the model penitential prayer in the Christian East.

Manuscript tradition: surviving Greek text in the Codex Alexandrinus (within the Odes appendix), Codex Turicensis, and dozens of liturgical psalters; Syriac, Ethiopic, Armenian, and Old Slavonic versions; Latin only as a marginal supplement to the Vulgate from the 13th c. onward.
