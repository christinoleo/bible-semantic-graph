First and longest of the three Johannine epistles. Not formally a letter — no salutation, no addressee, no closing — but a tract or homily addressed to a community in crisis. Vocabulary, idiom, and theology are so close to [[gospel-of-john]] that common Johannine authorship or at minimum common community provenance is the dominant view, though direct identity of author with the Gospel's "Beloved Disciple" is contested. Author identifies as "the elder" (cf. [[2-john]] 1; [[3-john]] 1) but not by personal name. Critical dating: ca. 90-110 CE, possibly slightly after the final form of the Gospel.

Crisis: a schism. "They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would have continued with us" (2:19). The opponents — characterized as "antichrists" (2:18-22; 4:3) — apparently deny "that Jesus is the Christ" (2:22), "that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh" (4:2-3), and the importance of ethical obedience and love of fellow believers. Reconstructed identification: a proto-docetic or proto-Gnostic secession from the Johannine community, denying the reality of Christ's incarnate human existence and dissolving the link between belief and ethics. Brown (1979) reconstructs the secession as a hyper-spiritualizing reading of the [[gospel-of-john]] itself — the dissidents drew from the same Gospel as the author of 1 John, but read its high Christology against its incarnational and ethical claims.

Structure: notoriously hard to outline; spiraling, recursive presentation of three intertwined tests of authentic faith — doctrinal (right confession of Christ), ethical (obedience to God's commands), and relational (love of fellow believers). Opening prologue (1:1-4, paralleling [[gospel-of-john]] 1:1-18 but emphasizing tactile witness — "that which we have heard, seen with our eyes, touched with our hands"); walking in the light vs. darkness (1:5-2:11); little children, young men, fathers (2:12-17); the antichrists and the anointing (2:18-27); children of God and children of the devil (2:28-3:24); testing the spirits (4:1-6); God is love and the love commandment (4:7-5:5); witnesses to the Son and assurance (5:6-21).

Key passages and theological emphases: "God is light" (1:5) and "God is love" (4:8, 16) — paired definitional statements. The incarnational confession as criterion (4:2, "every spirit that confesses Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God") — the verse that gives the anti-docetic reading. The love commandment as test of genuine knowledge of God (4:7-21, "we love because he first loved us"). Assurance of eternal life (5:13). The "Johannine comma" (5:7-8 in the Textus Receptus: "the Father, the Word, and the Holy Spirit, and these three are one") is a Latin gloss not present in any Greek manuscript before the 14th century — universally rejected by modern critical text editions, though defended by some KJV-only and traditional-text advocates.

Atonement theology: 2:2 — "he is the propitiation (ἱλασμός) for our sins, and not for ours only but also for the sins of the whole world" — central text in debates over the extent of the atonement (universal vs. definite/limited) and over the propitiation/expiation translation (sin-removal vs. wrath-appeasement).

Sin and the believer: an apparent tension (1:8, "if we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves" vs. 3:9, "no one born of God makes a practice of sinning") — resolved variously as habitual vs. occasional sin, eschatological vs. present reality, or polemical exaggeration against perfectionist secessionists.

Major figures: the elder (anonymous author), Christ as "advocate" (παράκλητος, 2:1 — the same term used in [[gospel-of-john]] for the Holy Spirit). [[john-apostle]] is the traditional author per ecclesiastical tradition.

Reception: foundational for incarnational and atonement theology; the "God is love" formula has been the single most-cited NT statement about divine character in modern theology; the schism and antichrist material shape Christian heresiology and apocalyptic imagination; 1 John's tests of authentic Christianity are central to Reformed and Pietist traditions of assurance.
