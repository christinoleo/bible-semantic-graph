Undisputed Pauline letter, written from imprisonment (1:7, 13-14, 17). Provenance of the imprisonment is debated: traditionally Rome (early 60s CE), but a Caesarean (mid-50s) or Ephesian (mid-50s, no narrative attestation but inferable from references in 1 Cor 15:32; 2 Cor 1:8) imprisonment is widely considered, with the Ephesian option preferred by many on grounds of the implied frequency of travel between Paul and Philippi.

Philippi: Roman colony in Macedonia, originally evangelized by [[paul]] on his second journey (cf. [[acts]] 16:11-40, including the conversions of [[lydia]] and the jailer). The community is Paul's most beloved and only one from which he accepted financial support (4:15-16; cf. [[2-corinthians]] 11:9). The letter functions as a Greco-Roman "letter of friendship", thanking the Philippians for a recent gift conveyed by Epaphroditus (2:25-30; 4:10-20).

Partition theories: some critics (esp. Schmithals, Bornkamm) partition Philippians into 2 or 3 originally distinct letters on the basis of the abrupt "finally" in 3:1 and the sharp tonal break at 3:2 ("look out for the dogs…"). Most current commentaries treat the letter as unitary, reading the digression as rhetorical.

Structure: thanksgiving (1:3-11); Paul's situation in prison and the advance of the gospel (1:12-26); call to humility grounded in the Christ-hymn (1:27-2:18); commendation of [[timothy]] and Epaphroditus (2:19-30); polemic against Judaizing rivals and Paul's own credentials (3:1-4:1); exhortations and acknowledgment of the gift (4:2-23).

The Christ-hymn (2:6-11): the most studied poetic-liturgical passage in the NT. Pre-Pauline hymn cited by Paul or Pauline composition? Two-strophe or three-strophe? Adam-Christology (Dunn) or pre-existence/incarnation (Wright, Bauckham, Fee)? The participial μορφῇ θεοῦ ὑπάρχων ("being in the form of God") and the "emptying" (ἐκένωσεν, kenōsis) are the key crux; the hymn underwrites Patristic incarnational theology and 19th-c. kenotic Christology (Thomasius, Forsyth) and remains the primary biblical proof-text for incarnation. Phil 2:10-11 echoes [[book-of-isaiah]] 45:23 ("every knee shall bow") — a passage about YHWH — now applied to Christ.

Other key passages: "to live is Christ and to die is gain" (1:21); Paul's renunciation of Jewish credentials as "rubbish" (σκύβαλα, 3:8); the "prize of the upward call" (3:14); "rejoice in the Lord always" (4:4); "I have learned to be content in whatever circumstances" (4:11); the peace that passes understanding (4:7).

Major figures: [[paul]] (author), [[timothy]] (co-sender, 1:1), Epaphroditus (the courier), Euodia and Syntyche (women in conflict, 4:2-3), Clement, "the bishops and deacons" (1:1 — earliest reference to local church office in the Pauline corpus). [[lydia]] is not named but was the church's foundational convert ([[acts]] 16).

Reception: the Christ-hymn shapes patristic Christology, especially the Cappadocian and Antiochene debates; kenotic Christology becomes a major 19th-20th-c. theological movement; the letter is the model of "friendship letter" Christian piety; Phil 4:13 ("I can do all things…") is among the most-quoted (and most-decontextualized) NT verses in popular religion.
