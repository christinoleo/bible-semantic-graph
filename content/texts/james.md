General epistle traditionally ascribed to [[james-brother-of-jesus]] (the Lord's brother, head of the Jerusalem church, [[acts]] 15, 21; [[galatians]] 1:19; 2:9). Address: "to the twelve tribes in the Dispersion" (1:1) — read variously as literal address to Jewish-Christian diaspora communities, as symbolic address to the church as eschatological Israel, or as pseudepigraphic literary frame.

Authenticity debate: contested. Arguments against authentic authorship by James of Jerusalem: (1) the polished Hellenistic Greek (perhaps the best in the NT alongside Hebrews) seems improbable for a Galilean Jewish leader; (2) absence of references to specifically Jamesian concerns (food laws, circumcision) from [[acts]] and [[galatians]]; (3) faint Christological footprint (only 1:1 and 2:1 name Christ); (4) the faith-vs-works rhetoric presupposes the Pauline (or post-Pauline) controversy, suggesting dating after Pauline missions. Arguments for authenticity: (1) the Jewish-wisdom idiom and synagogal-style paraenesis fit a Palestinian Jewish-Christian setting; (2) close echoes of Jesus-tradition sayings (especially the Sermon on the Mount) without literary dependence on the synoptics suggest pre-Gospel oral tradition; (3) the simple "James" without further qualification implies someone of unique stature; (4) thin Christology can equally indicate early dating. Dating ranges from the 40s (very early, before Pauline letters) to ca. 80-100 CE.

Genre: paraenesis (moral exhortation) in the wisdom-tradition style, structurally closer to [[proverbs]] or Ben Sira than to Pauline epistles. Loosely organized, catchword-linked, with a notable absence of sustained argument and a high density of imperatives (~50 in 108 verses). The Sermon-on-the-Mount kinship is unmistakable (cf. Jas 1:5 / Mt 7:7; Jas 2:13 / Mt 5:7; Jas 4:11-12 / Mt 7:1-2; Jas 5:12 / Mt 5:34-37).

Major themes: testing and endurance (1:2-18), hearing and doing (1:19-27), partiality and the "royal law" of love (2:1-13), faith without works is dead (2:14-26), the tongue (3:1-12), wisdom from above (3:13-18), worldliness and humility (4:1-12), warning to the rich (4:13-5:6), patience until the Lord's coming (5:7-11), oaths, prayer, and confession (5:12-20).

The faith-works problem (2:14-26): "you see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone" (2:24) — using [[abraham]]'s offering of [[isaac]] (Gen 22) as proof, the same OT episode [[paul]] cites for justification by faith (Rom 4; Gal 3) but framed differently. Three positions: (1) the contradiction is real and irreconcilable (Luther's view, in part); (2) James and Paul are operating with different definitions of "faith" (James: bare intellectual assent vs. Paul: living trust) and "works" (James: ethical deeds vs. Paul: works of the Mosaic law as boundary markers) — the standard harmonization in modern critical and confessional scholarship; (3) James is directly responding to Pauline slogans, correcting a misreading rather than Paul himself.

Luther's "epistle of straw": in his 1522 Septembertestament preface, Luther called James "a right strawy epistle" (eine rechte stroherne Epistel) for its perceived contradiction with Romans-Galatians soteriology, demoting it to the appendix of his NT. Reformed and Anglican traditions retained it as canonical; Luther's depreciation did not become normative even within Lutheranism.

Distinctive material: social critique of the wealthy (5:1-6, prophetic-style woe oracle); anointing of the sick by elders for healing (5:14-15, central to Catholic sacrament of anointing and Pentecostal healing practice); the tongue as untamable fire (3:6-8).

Major figures: [[james-brother-of-jesus]] (claimed author), [[abraham]] (2:21-23), [[rahab]] (2:25 — paired with Abraham as exemplar of faith-and-works), Elijah (5:17-18, model of effective prayer), Job (5:11, model of patient endurance).

Reception: anointing of the sick (5:14-15) is the biblical anchor of Catholic anointing/unction; broadly underwrites Christian social ethics critical of wealth; foundational for "active faith" theologies in evangelical and Wesleyan traditions; James is the favorite NT book of the Social Gospel movement (Rauschenbusch); rehabilitation of James against Lutheran depreciation is a major theme in 20th-c. Lutheran (Käsemann, with reservations) and broader Protestant scholarship.
