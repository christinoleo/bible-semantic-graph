The dogmatic definition issued by the Council of [[chalcedon]] at its fifth session (22 October 451), the fourth ecumenical doctrinal pronouncement — formally a *horos* ("boundary, definition") rather than a baptismal *symbolon*, but functionally a creed and conventionally grouped with them. The core formula: "we all teach harmoniously [that he is] the same perfect in Godhead, the same perfect in manhood, truly God and truly man, the same of a rational soul and body, *homoousios* with the Father as to his Godhead, and the same *homoousios* with us as to his manhood, like us in all things except sin... acknowledged in two natures *without confusion, without change, without division, without separation* (ἀσυγχύτως, ἀτρέπτως, ἀδιαιρέτως, ἀχωρίστως), the distinction of the natures being in no way taken away by the union, but rather the property of each nature being preserved, and concurring in one Person (*prosōpon*) and one Subsistence (*hypostasis*) — not parted or divided into two Persons, but one and the same Son and only-begotten God the Word, Lord Jesus Christ."

The Definition's working materials are openly named in its preamble: the [[nicene-creed|Nicene]] and [[nicene-constantinopolitan-creed|Nicene-Constantinopolitan]] creeds (reaffirmed as the sufficient symbol of faith — Chalcedon insists it is not legislating a new creed but defending the old), the synodical letters of [[cyril-of-alexandria|Cyril]] (II and III to [[nestorius]]) for the Alexandrian-unity side, and the [[tome-of-leo|Tome of Leo]] (449) for the Western-Antiochene distinction side. The four adverbs (*asynchytōs, atreptōs, adiairetōs, achōristōs*) become the structural skeleton of subsequent orthodox Christology — two negations against [[eutychianism|Eutyches]] (no confusion, no change of the natures into each other), two against [[nestorianism|Nestorius]] (no division, no separation of the one Person). The Definition is rejected by the non-Chalcedonian (Oriental Orthodox) churches, who hold to [[cyril-of-alexandria|Cyril]]'s *mia physis tou Theou Logou sesarkōmenē* and read "in two natures" as Nestorianizing; modern ecumenical dialogues since 1971 have largely concluded the disagreement is substantially terminological.
