Three or four chapters (MT: three; English Bibles: four — the final six verses are split off as ch. 4). Closes the [[twelve|Book of the Twelve]] and, in the Christian OT canon ordering, the OT itself — Malachi's last verses point forward to "Elijah the prophet" coming before "the great and terrible day of YHWH" (4:5-6), a redactional bridge that arguably shapes the entire Tanakh's eschatological horizon.

"Malachi" (malʾāḵî, "my messenger") may be a personal name or a title drawn from 3:1 ("behold, I send my messenger") — both ancient Jewish tradition (b. Megillah 15a identifies Malachi with [[ezra]]) and modern critical scholarship have entertained the latter possibility, making the book technically anonymous. Dating ca. mid-5th c. BCE, on the basis of a functioning Second Temple (presupposed throughout), the use of the Persian title peḥāh (governor, 1:8), and concerns paralleling [[book-of-ezra]] and [[book-of-nehemiah]] (mixed marriages, neglect of tithes, priestly corruption).

Distinctive form: disputation oracles. Six (or seven) units each follow a pattern — divine assertion / objection from the audience ("but you say…") / divine rebuttal. Topics: YHWH's love for Israel disputed via Edom (1:2-5); priestly contempt for sacrifice (1:6-2:9); mixed marriages and divorce (2:10-16, including the famously translated "I hate divorce," 2:16, whose syntax is genuinely disputed); the God of justice and the coming messenger (2:17-3:5); robbing God in tithes (3:6-12); the futility-vs.-fear-of-YHWH dispute and the book-of-remembrance (3:13-4:3); appendix calling for Torah remembrance and Elijah's coming (4:4-6).

Themes: priestly corruption and the demand for pure offerings (1:11's "in every place incense is offered to my name, and a pure offering" — heavily disputed text: contemporary Diaspora practice? eschatological universal worship? a critique of Jerusalem's defective cult by appeal to better Gentile honor of YHWH?); marriage, divorce, and intermarriage as covenant infidelity; tithes; the [[day-of-yhwh|Day of YHWH]] differentiating righteous from wicked; the Elijah-figure as forerunner.

The closing oracle (3:1; 4:5-6) — the messenger who prepares the way, identified as Elijah returning before the Day of YHWH — provides the explicit hinge between the OT and the NT in Christian reading. [[gospel-of-matthew]] 11:10 cites Mal 3:1 of [[john-the-baptist]]; Matt 11:14 and 17:10-13 identify John as the returning Elijah. The Hebrew canon's reordering — placing Malachi before, but the Writings after — produces a different shape ending with [[2-chronicles]]; the Christian sequence ending with Malachi creates the famous "400 silent years" between the testaments.

Reception: in addition to the Elijah-John-the-Baptist link, Mal 1:2-3 ("Jacob have I loved, but Esau have I hated") is cited in [[romans]] 9:13 for election theology. Mal 3:6 ("I YHWH do not change") is foundational for divine-immutability arguments. Rabbinic tradition (b. Sotah 48b) treats Malachi as the last prophet, after whom prophecy ceases until the eschaton.
