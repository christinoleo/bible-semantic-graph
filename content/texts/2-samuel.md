Fourth book of the Former Prophets. Reign of [[david]] from accession at Hebron through the troubled later years in Jerusalem: consolidation, dynastic promise, Bathsheba affair, Absalom revolt, succession question.

Continues directly from [[1-samuel]] (originally one scroll). The Court History or Succession Narrative (2 Sam 9-20 + 1 Kgs 1-2) is widely treated as one of the earliest and most psychologically sophisticated extended prose narratives in world literature; Leonhard Rost's source-critical proposal still anchors the discussion. The Davidic dynastic oracle of 2 Sam 7 is one of the most theologically generative passages in the Hebrew Bible and a central seed of later messianic expectation.

Structure: David's accession at Hebron and conflict with the house of [[saul]] (2 Sam 1-4); capture of Jerusalem and ark transfer (2 Sam 5-6); the Davidic covenant via [[nathan-prophet]] (2 Sam 7); wars and administration (2 Sam 8-10); [[bathsheba]] and the murder of Uriah, Nathan's parable and rebuke, death of the child (2 Sam 11-12); rape of Tamar, Absalom's revenge and rebellion, flight and return of David, death of [[absalom]] (2 Sam 13-19); Sheba's revolt (2 Sam 20); appendices including the Song of David and the catalogue of David's mighty men (2 Sam 21-24).

Themes: divine election surviving moral failure, dynastic promise as unconditional grant (2 Sam 7), prophetic confrontation of royal power (Nathan), familial sin and political consequence, the cost of monarchy. The Bathsheba narrative establishes a model of prophetic critique that resounds through the literary prophets.

Reception: 2 Sam 7 is the textual nucleus of Jewish messianic expectation and Christian messianic claim — [[jesus]] as "Son of David". Rabbinic tradition softens David's culpability; the Penitential Psalms (esp. Ps 51) tie liturgically to the Bathsheba narrative. Modern literary scholarship (Alter, Sternberg) treats the Court History as a masterwork of compressed characterization.
