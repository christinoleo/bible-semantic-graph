Second book of the Former Prophets. Tribal-period narratives of charismatic deliverers ("judges", *shofetim*) between the conquest under [[joshua]] and the rise of monarchy under [[saul]] and [[david]].

Part of the Deuteronomistic History; the framing prologue (Judg 2:11-19) imposes a cyclical scheme — apostasy, oppression, cry to [[yhwh]], deliverer raised, rest, relapse — onto disparate older tribal traditions. Critical scholarship treats much of the underlying material as among the oldest in the Hebrew Bible (the Song of Deborah, Judg 5, is widely dated to the 12th-11th c. BCE on linguistic grounds), while the framing and final form are Deuteronomistic.

Structure: double prologue on the incomplete conquest and recurring apostasy (Judg 1-3:6); the major judges — Ehud, [[deborah]] and Barak, [[gideon]], Jephthah, [[samson]] — with minor judges interspersed (Judg 3:7-16); double epilogue (Judg 17-21) on the migration of the Danites, the idolatrous shrine of Micah, and the outrage at Gibeah and near-extinction of Benjamin, framed by the refrain "in those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes" — read as Deuteronomistic apology for monarchy.

Themes: covenant infidelity, tribal disunity, charismatic versus institutional leadership, ambiguous heroism (Jephthah's vow, Samson's recklessness, Gideon's ephod), the violence of the period (the Levite's concubine). Feminist scholarship (Trible, *Texts of Terror*) has foregrounded the book's catalogue of violence against women.

Reception: rabbinic and patristic readings tend to recuperate the judges as exempla of faith (Heb 11), often suppressing the moral ambiguities. Modern critical readings restore the ambiguity and treat Judges as a sustained meditation on the failure of decentralized covenant rule.
