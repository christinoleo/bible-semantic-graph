Heavenly-journey apocalypse pseudepigraphically attributed to [[baruch]] son of Neriah but bearing no clear textual connection to [[2-baruch]] beyond the shared pseudonym. Survives in Greek (two recensions) and in a more expanded Old Slavonic version with significant divergences. Composition is generally placed in the late 1st through 3rd c. CE, after the destruction of the Second Temple, in a Jewish (or Jewish with Christian editorial layer) milieu probably in the eastern Diaspora. Not in any canon.

Frame: Baruch laments the destruction of Jerusalem ("Lord, why hast thou set fire to thy vineyard…?", 1:2), and an angel appears to escort him through five heavens (or seven in some recensions and in the Slavonic, conformed to later cosmology). The number of heavens — five vs seven — is one of the textual fingerprints used to date the work and locate it in the evolving Jewish-Christian cosmology of late antiquity.

The five heavens contain: first, the punished builders of the Tower of Babel and their humanoid descendants; second, more Babel-builders and storks; third, the serpent that ate Hades, the sun chariot with the angel Phamael, the phoenix that shields the cosmos from solar destruction, and the moon (an extensive bestiary unique among apocalypses); fourth, the plain where the souls of the righteous gather as birds, and where the manna miraculously appears; fifth, the gate of heaven where Michael the chief angel receives the prayers of the righteous brought up by Michael's subordinate angels in baskets.

Theological emphases: the heavenly cult is real and ongoing despite earthly Temple destruction; angels mediate prayer (an angelology much developed beyond canonical material); the cosmos has functional anti-evil safeguards (the phoenix-shield); the punishments of the wicked are spatially mapped and visible. There is little eschatological prediction in the historical sense; the work is essentially cosmological and consolatory rather than predictive.

The Christian editorial layer in the Greek (most clearly at 4:15, on the planting of the vine by Sammael becoming a place of redemption "through the blood of [Christ]") is unevenly distributed and easily excised, suggesting a Jewish substrate redacted by Christian copyists. The Slavonic version is more Christianized still. Disentangling the layers is a key task in critical reception.

Reception: little use in mainstream Christian tradition; the work was peripherally known in Byzantine circles and more substantially in Slavic Christian piety. The "phoenix" cosmological motif influenced medieval bestiary traditions. Modern critical recovery dates from M. R. James's 1897 edition. The work is important comparatively for its position between the older Enochic ascent tradition ([[1-enoch]] 14, the Book of Watchers) and later Hekhalot mystical heavenly ascents, marking a transitional stage in Jewish-Christian mysticism.
