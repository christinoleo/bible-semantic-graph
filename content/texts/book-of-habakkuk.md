Three short chapters. Distinctive among the prophets for being structured as a dialogue between the prophet and YHWH — [[habakkuk]] addresses YHWH with complaint, receives an answer, complains again, receives a second answer, and then closes with a theophanic psalm. The book is the OT's most concentrated short treatise on theodicy.

Dating fixed by 1:6 — "I am raising up the Chaldeans" — placing composition in the late Assyrian / early Neo-Babylonian transition, conventionally ca. 605 BCE (after Nebuchadnezzar's victory at Carchemish, before the Babylonian invasions of Judah). Some critical scholarship treats the psalm of ch. 3 as a separate, independent composition appended later — it has its own superscription, musical notations (Selah three times, "to the choirmaster: with stringed instruments"), and theophanic-hymn genre. The Qumran pesher (1QpHab) commentates only chs. 1-2, possibly attesting an earlier two-chapter form.

Structure: first complaint — why does YHWH tolerate violence within Judah? (1:2-4); first divine answer — YHWH is raising the Chaldeans as instrument (1:5-11); second complaint — but the Chaldeans are more wicked than those they punish, how can YHWH use them? (1:12-2:1); second divine answer — "the righteous shall live by his faithfulness," followed by five woes against the oppressor (2:2-20); theophanic psalm — YHWH coming in storm from Teman, the prophet's terror and trust (3:1-19).

Hab 2:4b ("the righteous shall live by his ʾĕmûnāh") is the most theologically consequential half-verse in the Minor Prophets. ʾĕmûnāh ranges from "faithfulness" / "steadfastness" (more probable in original context, applied to the righteous person's persistence under trial) to "faith" / "trust" (the receptive sense developed in NT usage). LXX translates "the righteous shall live by my faithfulness" (the antecedent being God's), while [[paul]] reads it through ho dikaios ek pisteōs zēsetai. The interpretive history of this verse runs through Augustine, Aquinas, and Luther — for whom it became the foundational text of Reformation soteriology (justification by faith).

Themes: theodicy, the patience of the righteous under unjust suffering, divine sovereignty over geopolitical evil, the prophet's right to interrogate YHWH (Habakkuk shares this with [[jeremiah]]'s confessions and [[book-of-job]]). Ch. 3's cosmic theophany draws on archaic Yahwistic storm-warrior imagery (Sinai, Paran, the sun and moon standing still).

Reception: Hab 2:4 is cited three times in the NT ([[romans]] 1:17; [[galatians]] 3:11; [[hebrews]] 10:38), making it possibly the most theologically loaded OT verse in the apostolic corpus. The Qumran pesher 1QpHab applies the oracles to the conflict between the Teacher of Righteousness and the Wicked Priest — an extraordinary window into Second Temple sectarian hermeneutics.
