Shortest Pauline letter (335 words in Greek), undisputed Pauline authorship, written from imprisonment alongside [[colossians]] (the same set of associates appears in both — Onesimus, Tychicus, Epaphras, [[mark]], Aristarchus, Demas, [[luke]]). Provenance disputed: Rome (early 60s, traditional), Caesarea, or Ephesus (mid-50s) — the same options as [[philippians]] and [[colossians]]. The Ephesian hypothesis fits the implied geography best (Colossae is a short walk from Ephesus; a long journey from Rome).

Occasion: Onesimus, a slave of Philemon (a Colossian Christian house-church host), has somehow come into Paul's company in prison and become a Christian (10, "my child whom I have begotten in my chains"). Paul sends him back to his master Philemon with this letter, asking that he be received "no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother" (16). The traditional reconstruction (Onesimus as runaway slave guilty of theft, "if he has wronged you at all or owes you anything, charge it to my account", 18-19) is the long-dominant reading. Alternative reconstructions: Onesimus as runaway seeking out Paul as amicus domini (mediator-friend of his master) — a Roman legal procedure not technically constituting fuga; or Onesimus sent by Philemon to attend Paul in prison (Knox, Winter) — making the letter a request to retain Onesimus's service rather than return him.

Rhetoric: a masterclass in tactical persuasion. Paul begins with extensive praise of Philemon (4-7), names his apostolic authority (8) but refuses to wield it ("for love's sake I prefer to appeal", 9), introduces Onesimus only after lengthy setup (10), wordplay on Onesimus's name (Greek "useful", 11: "formerly useless to you but now useful to you and to me"), self-binding financial guarantee (18-19), the reminder "you owe me even your own self" (19), and the final pressure-applying request to prepare a guest room — implying Paul intends to inspect Philemon's compliance personally (22).

What Paul does not say: he does not explicitly demand manumission. The letter has been read across the entire spectrum — from "Paul subverts slavery from within by transforming the master-slave relation into siblinghood" (manumission implicit) to "Paul leaves slavery's structural reality untouched, only spiritualizing the relationship". The verb ἀποτίνω (19) and the language of "more than a slave" leave the legal status ambiguous.

Reception in slavery debates: the most weaponized short letter in NT history. Pro-slavery exegetes (Antebellum American defenders of chattel slavery; pre-emancipation Anglican apologists) cited Philemon as proof Paul accepted slavery and returned runaways. Abolitionists (Wesley, the Quakers, later Wilberforce, Garrison, Stowe) read the letter as undermining slavery's foundations through Christian fraternity and as not in fact returning Onesimus to chattel status. Modern critical and liberationist readings tend to read the letter as ambiguous on chattel status but theologically subversive of master-slave hierarchy.

Major figures: [[paul]] (author), [[timothy]] (co-sender), Philemon (recipient — note: the canonical slug map does not contain a node for him), Apphia ("our sister", possibly Philemon's wife), Archippus ("our fellow soldier", possibly their son or another household member), Onesimus (the slave), the same circle of associates as [[colossians]] 4.

Reception in canon-formation: included in the Pauline corpus from earliest collections; tertullian engages it; ignatius of antioch's letter to the ephesians names a later bishop "Onesimus" — taken by some (Knox) as evidence that the slave Onesimus eventually became bishop of Ephesus and curated the Pauline letter collection that includes his own commendation.
