In the Christian canon Ruth follows [[judges]] (set "in the days when the judges judged") and precedes [[1-samuel]]; in the Tanakh it sits in the Ketuvim/Writings as one of the five Megillot, read liturgically at Shavuot.

A short, tightly constructed novella in four chapters. A Moabite widow, [[ruth]], binds herself to her Israelite mother-in-law [[naomi]] and to Naomi's God; gleans in the fields of [[boaz]], a kinsman of Naomi's late husband; is taken in levirate-style marriage by Boaz; bears Obed, grandfather of [[david]] — establishing a Moabite ancestress in the Davidic line.

Dating is contested: traditional ascription to [[samuel]] is rejected; some scholars place composition in the United Monarchy (legitimating David's lineage), others in the post-exilic period (counter-voice to Ezra-Nehemiah's anti-foreign-marriage program in [[book-of-ezra]] and [[book-of-nehemiah]]). The book's quiet rebuke of ethnic exclusivism is most striking on the latter dating.

Themes: *hesed* (covenant loyalty) crossing ethnic and gender lines, redemption (*goel*) and levirate, female agency, providence working through ordinary economic and legal mechanisms (gleaning law of [[leviticus]] 19, levirate of [[deuteronomy]] 25), inclusion of the foreigner. No explicit theophany, no miracle — God acts only through human *hesed*.

Reception: rabbinic tradition (Ruth Rabbah) treats Ruth as paradigmatic proselyte. Christian reception emphasizes Ruth's inclusion in the genealogy of [[jesus]] (Mt 1:5) as foreshadowing Gentile incorporation. Modern feminist readings (Trible, Fewell-Gunn) explore the book's quiet female-centered theology and its more troubling silences.
