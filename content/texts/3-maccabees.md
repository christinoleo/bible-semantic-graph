Hellenistic-Jewish narrative in Greek, despite its title containing no Maccabean figures and no events from the Hasmonean revolt. The book recounts the supposed persecution of Egyptian Jews under [[ptolemy-iv-philopator]] (221-204 BCE) after his attempt to enter the Jerusalem temple, and their miraculous deliverance. Included in the Greek and Slavonic Orthodox canon and in Ethiopian Tewahedo; excluded from the Catholic and Protestant canons; Jerome did not translate it.

The narrative arc: Ptolemy IV, victorious at Raphia (217 BCE), visits Jerusalem and attempts to enter the Holy of Holies; he is struck down by divine action through the high priest Simon's prayer. Returning to Alexandria in humiliation, he turns on the Jewish community, ordering them registered, branded with an ivy-leaf (Dionysus's symbol), and reduced to slaves. He then orders all Egyptian Jews trampled by 500 drunken war elephants in the Alexandrian hippodrome. Three days of divine intervention thwart this — the elephants turn on the king's own forces — and Ptolemy reverses his policy, granting the Jews a festival in commemoration.

Historicity is essentially nil. The elephant story is told (with different protagonists) by [[josephus]] (*Against Apion* 2.5) of Ptolemy VIII Physcon (~145 BCE), suggesting either two independent folkloric transmissions or one displaced backward in time. The Raphia visit by Ptolemy IV is plausible but the temple-entry attempt is unattested elsewhere. The book is best read as a Diaspora theological novella rather than as historiography.

Composition: Alexandrian Egyptian Jewish authorship in the late 1st c. BCE or early 1st c. CE is the consensus, on the basis of Septuagintal Greek style, intimate knowledge of Ptolemaic administration, and concerns about Diaspora identity under foreign rulers. The book functions partly as an etiology for an otherwise unknown Egyptian-Jewish festival.

Themes: divine protection of the temple and the people (the Jerusalem-temple defense in chs. 1-2 mirrors the Alexandrian community's deliverance in chs. 3-7); rhetorical prayers in the [[book-of-esther]] Greek-additions tradition; sharp polemic against apostasy (Jews who accepted Dionysian initiation are punished by their faithful brethren). The eulogy of martyr-readiness and the elaborate prayer of Eleazar (ch. 6) parallel similar set-pieces in [[2-maccabees]] and [[4-maccabees]].

Reception: liturgical use in the Eastern churches but little Western reception. Included in Codex Alexandrinus and Codex Venetus. The Russian Orthodox tradition cites it in proper lections; Romanian and Slavonic biblical printings include it; Ethiopian Tewahedo distinguishes it from their own [[meqabyan]] (Ethiopian "Maccabees") which is unrelated in content.
