Second volume of Luke-Acts (cf. 1:1, "the first book I wrote, Theophilus") — same author, same addressee, continuous theological program. Critical dating tracks [[gospel-of-luke]]: ca. 80-100 CE, with some pushing later (Tyson, Pervo: into the 2nd c., reading Acts as response to Marcion). Title "Acts of the Apostles" is a 2nd-c. convention; the work in fact focuses on two protagonists ([[peter]] in 1-12, [[paul]] in 13-28) and is silent on the missions of the other apostles.

Geographic-theological program (1:8): "you shall be my witnesses in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth." Narrative trajectory: Jerusalem → Antioch → Asia Minor → Greece → Rome. The book ends abruptly with [[paul]] under house arrest in Rome (28:30-31), preaching unhindered — read variously as deliberate theological closure (gospel reaching imperial capital fulfills 1:8), as predating Paul's death (a minority argument for early dating), or as planned third volume never written.

Sources and historicity: the "we-passages" (16:10-17; 20:5-15; 21:1-18; 27:1-28:16) shift to first-person plural narration during certain Pauline journeys — traditionally cited as evidence of [[luke]]'s eyewitness presence; alternatively read as literary device (sea-voyage convention) or as use of a travel diary source. Comparison with the undisputed Pauline letters reveals tensions: differences in Paul's itinerary, the role of the Jerusalem council ([[acts]] 15 vs [[galatians]] 2), and the smoothed-over relations between Paul and the Jerusalem leadership.

Speeches (Pentecost 2; Stephen 7; Cornelius 10; Areopagus 17; Miletus 20; trial defenses 22, 24, 26) account for ~30% of the text. Their composition is the central historical question: Thucydidean convention permitted ancient historians to compose speeches expressing what the speaker "ought to have said." Dibelius and the form-critical school treat the speeches as Lukan compositions encoding kerygmatic theology; conservative scholarship defends substantial traditional material underlying them. Either way, the speeches articulate Luke's distinctive theology of salvation history.

Structure: ascension and Pentecost (1-2), Jerusalem community (3-5), [[stephen]] martyrdom and Hellenist scattering (6-8), conversion of [[paul]] (9), Cornelius and Gentile inclusion (10-11), [[peter]]'s imprisonment and Herodian episode (12), Pauline missionary journeys (13-21), arrest and trials (21-26), voyage to Rome and shipwreck (27-28).

Major themes: Holy Spirit as agent of mission (Pentecost, "Acts of the Holy Spirit" is sometimes proposed as truer title), Gentile inclusion ([[peter]]/Cornelius, [[acts]] 15 Jerusalem council, Pauline mission), pattern of Jewish rejection → Gentile reception, suffering of the witnesses ([[stephen]], James son of Zebedee, [[paul]]), legitimacy of the Pauline mission within the apostolic church.

Major figures: [[peter]] (chs. 1-12, then recedes), [[paul]] (13-28), [[stephen]] (first martyr, 6-7), [[barnabas]] (Paul's sponsor and partner), [[silas]] (companion on second journey), [[timothy]], [[john-apostle]], [[james-brother-of-jesus]] (Jerusalem leader, ch. 15, 21), [[priscilla]] and [[aquila]], [[lydia]] (first European convert, 16:14-15).

Reception: foundational for ecclesiology — the question of "earliest Christianity" runs through Acts. Tübingen school (F.C. Baur, 19th c.) read Acts as later harmonization of Petrine and Pauline parties. Pentecostal and charismatic movements take Acts 2 as paradigm for ongoing Spirit experience. Liberation and missiological readings mine the boundary-crossing trajectory.
