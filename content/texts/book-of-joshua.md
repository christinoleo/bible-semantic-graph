First of the Former Prophets in the Tanakh; first historical book of the Christian Old Testament. The conquest narrative: crossing the Jordan, fall of Jericho, southern and northern campaigns, allotment of the land among the tribes, covenant renewal at Shechem.

Part of the Deuteronomistic History (Joshua–[[2-kings]]), the long historiographic arc shaped by Deuteronomic theology — fidelity rewarded, apostasy punished. Critical scholarship treats Joshua as theological historiography rather than archival report; the conquest as narrated (rapid, total, unified, divinely commanded *herem* warfare) conflicts with archaeological evidence at Jericho, Ai, and other key sites, and with the more gradual emergence-of-Israel picture indicated in [[judges]] and in archaeology (highland settlement pattern, indigenous origin hypotheses, peasant revolt theories — Mendenhall, Gottwald, Finkelstein).

Structure: conquest narrative (Josh 1-12), including the Rahab pericope and the Jericho and Ai accounts; tribal allotments (Josh 13-21); concluding narratives (Josh 22-24) with the Transjordanian altar incident and Joshua's covenant renewal speech at Shechem ("choose this day whom you will serve"), followed by Joshua's death.

Themes: divine warrior, land as gift and as obligation, total obedience to Mosaic command, covenant continuity from Moses to Joshua. The *herem* (devotion to destruction) commands have generated sustained ethical and theological controversy — patristic allegorization, medieval just-war discussion, and modern critiques of biblical violence (Schwartz, Crossan, Boyd).

Major figures: [[joshua]] as Mosaic successor, [[caleb]] as faithful spy, [[rahab]] the Jerichoite who shelters the Israelite scouts and is incorporated into Israel.

Reception: rabbinic tradition treats Joshua as transmitter of Torah; Christian typology connects "Yehoshua" to "Yeshua"/[[jesus]] (same name, Greek Iēsous), and the conquest is read allegorically (Origen) or supersessionistically. Modern Israeli national imagination has often drawn on Joshua, generating its own contested reception.
