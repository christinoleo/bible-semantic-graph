Post-exilic narrative of return from Babylonian exile and rebuilding of the Jerusalem temple, followed by the mission of [[ezra]] the scribe. In the Hebrew Bible, Ezra and [[book-of-nehemiah]] are a single book; the Septuagint and Vulgate divide them.

Author and date: traditional attribution to Ezra is held only for portions; critical consensus treats Ezra-Nehemiah as the work of a Chronistic-related but distinct redactor, finalized in the late Persian period (late 5th-4th c. BCE). The book's "memoirs" sections (esp. the first-person material) may incorporate authentic court documents (e.g., the Aramaic correspondence in Ezra 4-6, written in Imperial Aramaic).

Structure: Cyrus decree and first return under Sheshbazzar and Zerubbabel (Ezra 1-2); altar restoration and temple foundation (Ezra 3); Samaritan opposition and stalled work (Ezra 4); resumption under Haggai and Zechariah and completion of the Second Temple in 516 BCE (Ezra 5-6); arrival of Ezra under Artaxerxes (Ezra 7-8); the mixed-marriage crisis and forced dissolution of foreign marriages (Ezra 9-10).

Themes: continuity of post-exilic community with pre-exilic Israel via Davidic-Levitical lineage and temple cult, Torah as constitutive of restored Israel (Ezra "set his heart to study the law of YHWH and to do it and to teach"), ethnic boundary maintenance via the mixed-marriage reform, Persian imperial sponsorship of local cultic restoration. The "holy seed" theology of Ezra 9 stands in pointed tension with the inclusive theology of [[book-of-ruth]] and parts of [[book-of-isaiah]] (esp. Isa 56).

Reception: foundational for rabbinic Judaism, which traces the canonization of Torah and the institution of public Torah reading to Ezra (the Talmud calls him a second Moses). Modern scholarship has used Ezra-Nehemiah as the central case study for the social formation of Second Temple Judaism, ethnic boundary-drawing in colonial/imperial contexts, and the politics of return-migration.
