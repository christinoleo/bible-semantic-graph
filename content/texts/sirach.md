Most extensive wisdom collection surviving from Second-Temple Judaism. Composed in Hebrew in Jerusalem ca. 195-175 BCE by [[jeshua-ben-sira]] (Yehoshua ben Eleazar ben Sira), a Jerusalem scribe and teacher of Torah, and translated into Greek in Alexandria ca. 132 BCE by his grandson, whose preserved prologue is the earliest external reference to a tripartite Hebrew canon ("the Law and the Prophets and the other books of our ancestors"). Sirach thus sits on the cusp of the Hellenistic crisis — pre-Maccabean, pre-apocalyptic, written under the high priest Simon II (whom the book eulogizes, ch. 50), and showing a Jerusalem scribal sapiential culture confident in Torah, Temple, and the priesthood.

Textual history. The Hebrew original was lost to Western tradition for a millennium, surviving in extensive Greek (LXX), Latin (Vetus Latina / Vulgate), Syriac, and other versions. Beginning in 1896, fragments of medieval Hebrew copies were recovered from the Cairo Geniza (covering roughly two-thirds of the book); subsequent finds at Masada (1st c. BCE/CE) and Qumran (2Q18; 11QPs^a Sirach) confirmed the Hebrew Vorlage's antiquity and gave modern scholars critical control of the Greek translation. Significant divergences between the Hebrew, Greek (with a shorter and longer Greek recension), Syriac, and Latin make Sirach's textual criticism among the most intricate in the deuterocanon.

Structure. The book lacks a single overarching plan; it accumulates compositional units of varying length on a rolling set of themes. Chs. 1-23 emphasize the fear of the Lord, friendship, restraint of speech, parental honor, social virtue. Chs. 24-43 broaden into the celebrated Wisdom autobiography (ch. 24 — Wisdom proceeds from the mouth of the Most High, takes up residence in Israel and the Temple, "I took root in an honored people, in the portion of the Lord, his heritage"), instructions on professions (38:24-39:11, the celebrated comparison of artisans and the scribe), creation hymns (42:15-43:33). Chs. 44-50, the "Praise of the Fathers" (Hebrew: shevach avot), is a canonical-historical roll-call from [[enoch]] through Simon II — the most extensive intra-canonical "canon of worthies" in Second-Temple literature, and a witness to which biblical figures and books were authoritative ca. 180 BCE. Ch. 51 contains a closing prayer of thanksgiving and an autobiographical acrostic on Wisdom (the latter also attested in Hebrew at 11QPs^a).

Theology. Sirach effects the most thoroughgoing identification of Wisdom with Torah in the Jewish corpus — Wisdom is hypostatized (ch. 24) in language echoing [[proverbs]] 8, yet identified explicitly with "the book of the covenant of the Most High God, the law that Moses commanded us" (24:23). The fear of the Lord is the unifying virtue and refrain (1:11-20; 19:20; 25:6-11). Sirach is firmly retributive in this-worldly terms — reward and punishment are visible within ordinary life — and lacks the developed afterlife and resurrection theology of contemporary works like [[book-of-daniel]] or [[1-enoch]]; Sheol is the common shadowy end. The Praise of the Fathers articulates an unbroken covenantal-priestly continuity culminating in Simon II in the Temple, an apologetic for Zadokite priestly legitimacy that pre-dates and contrasts with the later Hasmonean rearrangement.

Reception. The most-cited deuterocanonical book in patristic literature; Cyprian's third-century treatises use it as freely as any canonical text, and the Latin title "Ecclesiasticus" (church-book) reflects its centrality in catechesis and ethics. Sirach 1, 24, and 51 inform Christian Wisdom-Christology (the Johannine prologue, Pauline epistles, Hebrews). In rabbinic Judaism, Ben Sira is uncanonical but cited approvingly in the Talmud and Midrash — over a hundred quotations, sometimes by name, sometimes with the formula "as it is written"; the rabbis preserved it as a valued sapiential collection while excluding it from miqra. Hippo, Carthage, Trent affirmed; Reformers excluded as Apocrypha but recommended for ethical reading (Luther: "an excellent and good book"). Sirach 50's portrait of the high priest Simon, Sirach 38's reflection on physicians, and Sirach 44's celebrated incipit ("Let us now praise famous men, and our fathers in their generations") have had long literary afterlives.
