Philosophical-rhetorical Greek treatise framed as a diatribe demonstrating that "pious reason is sovereign over the passions" (1:1). The author rehearses, at exegetical-philosophical length, the martyrdoms of the priest [[eleazar-maccabeus]], the seven brothers, and their mother — episodes already narrated in [[2-maccabees]] 6:18-7:42 — as case studies in Stoic-influenced moral psychology. Included in an appendix to the Greek Orthodox Bible and in Ethiopian Tewahedo; not in Catholic, Protestant, or Slavonic canons.

Genre is unique in the Septuagintal corpus: a Hellenistic protreptic/diatribe in the style of Cynic-Stoic moral philosophy, but with biblical content. The opening "philosophical thesis" (1:1-3:18) lays out a Stoic taxonomy of the soul (reason vs the passions: pleasure and pain, with subspecies) and argues for the supremacy of reason informed by Torah. The second half (3:19-18:24) is the historical demonstration: Eleazar, the brothers, the mother. The Maccabean martyr stories are not so much narrated as homiletically dissected.

Authorship is anonymous and pseudo-Josephan in some manuscript traditions (incorrectly — the Greek differs sharply from Josephus). Eusebius and Jerome already knew the work as separate. Dating is debated within a range of ca. 19-118 CE — terminus post quem the rule of Sejanus, terminus ante quem the Bar Kokhba revolt. Composed in elegant Atticizing Greek, plausibly in Antioch, Alexandria, or Asia Minor.

Theological-philosophical synthesis: the author identifies Torah-observance with Stoic εὐσέβεια (piety) and ἀνδρεία (manly courage); the martyrs achieve apatheia through the Law. The book introduces (or popularizes) the idea of vicarious atoning martyrdom: the death of the seven and their mother "purified the homeland" and served as "a ransom for the sin of our nation" (17:21-22; cf. 6:29). This vocabulary anticipates and partially parallels developing Christian atonement theology and is one of the closest pre-Christian Jewish parallels to substitutionary-atonement language.

Reception: deeply influential on Christian martyrological literature. Christian copyists transmitted the work assiduously precisely because of its martyrology and atonement themes. The cult of the "Maccabean Martyrs" in the early church (with relics venerated at Antioch from the 4th c.) drew explicit theological scaffolding from 4 Macc; [[gregory-of-nazianzus]] and [[john-chrysostom]] preached panegyrics on them. The seven brothers were eventually venerated as Christian saints despite being pre-Christian Jews — a remarkable case of inter-traditional canonization.

Modern scholarship treats 4 Macc as a key witness for Hellenistic-Jewish philosophical writing, alongside [[philo-of-alexandria]] and the [[wisdom-of-solomon]], and for the development of pre-Christian martyr-theology that fed into early Christian Christology.
