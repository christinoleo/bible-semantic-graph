Jewish apocalyptic testament in the form of [[moses]]'s farewell address to [[joshua]] before his death, dictating a sealed prophecy of Israel's future from the conquest through an eschatological consummation. Survives only in a single 6th-c. Latin palimpsest discovered by A. M. Ceriani at the Ambrosian Library in 1861, breaking off mid-text. The Greek and Hebrew/Aramaic originals are lost. Not in any canon.

Title ambiguity: ancient lists (Stichometry of Nicephorus, Athanasius's festal lists, Gelasian decree) cite two related but distinct works, the *Assumption of Moses* (Ἀνάληψις Μωϋσέως) and the *Testament of Moses* (Διαθήκη Μωϋσέως). Whether the Ceriani palimpsest preserves one of them, both originally-joined, or only an excerpt is contested. The surviving text is plainly a testament (deathbed-prophecy genre, no assumption-narrative), so many scholars (Tromp, Charles) prefer the title "Testament of Moses" for the extant work and treat "Assumption" as a now-lost continuation in which Moses was bodily taken up, possibly featuring the Michael-Satan dispute that [[jude]] cites.

Jude's citation: [[jude]] 9 reports that [[michael-archangel]], disputing with the devil over the body of [[moses]], "did not bring against him a railing judgment, but said, 'The Lord rebuke thee.'" This story does not appear in the canonical Hebrew Bible but is widely attested by patristic writers ([[origen]], *De principiis* 3.2.1; [[clement-of-alexandria]]) as drawn from the Assumption of Moses. The lost ending of the Latin palimpsest plausibly contained this episode, providing one of the firmest links between New Testament epistolary literature and the pseudepigrapha.

Surviving content (12 chapters): Moses commissions Joshua and predicts the future — the conquest, the divided monarchy, the Babylonian exile, the return, the Hasmoneans, the "rash king" (commonly identified as Herod the Great), and a Roman successor. The historical horizon thus reaches into the early 1st c. CE, supporting a composition date ~7-30 CE, after Herod's death (4 BCE) and around the disturbances under Varus and Archelaus. Chapter 9 introduces the enigmatic "Taxo" of the tribe of Levi who takes his seven sons into a cave to die rather than transgress the commandments — a martyrdom narrative whose precise referent is debated (a Maccabean episode, Bar-Kokhba-era event, or general typology).

The eschatology (ch. 10) is sharply theocentric: God himself, not a messiah, appears as the eschatological deliverer; angelic powers tremble, the sun is darkened, the horns of the moon are broken, Israel is exalted to the stars. This is one of the rare Jewish apocalypses without an explicit messianic figure, a fact often noted in debates about Second-Temple messianism.

Reception: cited by Origen as canonical scripture among some, then progressively marginalized in Christian transmission. Rediscovered modern interest dates from Ceriani's 1861 publication; the standard critical edition is Tromp (1993). The book remains essential for reconstructing the religious worldview of late Second-Temple Jewish apocalypticism and for grounding [[jude]]'s otherwise unattributable citations in concrete textual tradition.
