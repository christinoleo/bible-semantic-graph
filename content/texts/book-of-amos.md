Nine chapters traditionally treated as the earliest of the "writing prophets" — [[amos]] of Tekoa active ca. 760-750 BCE under Jeroboam II of Israel and Uzziah of Judah (Amos 1:1). A Judahite shepherd and dresser of sycamore figs (7:14) who prophesies in the northern shrine at Bethel and is expelled by the priest Amaziah (7:10-17, the famous biographical confrontation). The disclaimer "I am no prophet, nor a prophet's son" (7:14) is variously translated as denial of professional-prophet status or as a temporal claim ("I was no prophet…").

Structure: oracles against the nations (1:3-2:16, eight rhetorically parallel oracles culminating in Israel itself — a homiletical trap, the audience nodding through judgments on Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, Moab, Judah, and then implicated themselves); the body of oracles and exhortations (3-6); five visions of judgment (7:1-9:10, interrupted by the Amaziah narrative); the restoration epilogue (9:11-15).

The restoration epilogue is widely held by critical scholarship to be a later, post-exilic Judahite addition — its hopeful tone, references to the fallen "booth of David," and shift in geographical horizon contrast sharply with Amos's relentless announcement of Israel's end ("the end has come upon my people," 8:2). Conservative scholarship defends its originality.

Themes: social justice and economic critique — Amos is the OT's sharpest prophet on inequality, oppression of the poor, corruption of the courts ("they sell the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals," 2:6), luxury of the elite (4:1, "cows of Bashan"; 6:4-7, the marzeah-banquets), and the inseparability of cult from ethics ("I hate, I despise your feasts… let justice roll down like waters," 5:21-24). Election as responsibility, not privilege ("you only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you," 3:2). The [[day-of-yhwh]] as darkness, not light (5:18-20) — Amos inverts a popular national-hope motif.

Reception: foundational for prophetic social-justice traditions in Jewish ethics, Christian liberation theology (Gutiérrez, Boff cite Amos repeatedly), and the American civil rights movement — Martin Luther King Jr.'s "let justice roll down like waters" (Amos 5:24) became iconic. [[acts]] 15:16-17 cites the LXX of Amos 9:11-12 in James's speech endorsing Gentile inclusion. Rabbinic tradition (b. Makkot 24a) lists Amos's reduction of the commandments to one ("seek me and live," 5:4) among the great prophetic summations.
