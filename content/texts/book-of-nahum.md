Three chapters, one of the most rhetorically polished poetic compositions in the Hebrew Bible — opens with a partial acrostic hymn (1:2-8, alphabet incomplete, possibly damaged in transmission) followed by sustained, vivid battle imagery. A triumphal oracle on the impending fall of [[nineveh]], the Assyrian capital.

Nahum of Elkosh (location unknown — proposed sites in Galilee, Judah, and northern Israel) is otherwise unidentified. Dating is narrowly fixed by internal evidence: the book references the fall of Thebes (No-Amon) to Assyria in 663 BCE as a past event (3:8-10) and presupposes Nineveh's downfall as future or imminent — Nineveh fell to the Medo-Babylonian coalition in 612 BCE. Composition is therefore commonly placed in the 620s-610s, possibly late Josianic.

Structure: hymnic theophany asserting YHWH's character as avenger (1:2-11); oracle of Nineveh's fall and Judah's relief (1:12-2:2); the assault on Nineveh — chariots, troops, defenders' panic (2:3-13); woe-oracle and taunt (3:1-19) with the city personified as a fallen prostitute and as a doomed lion's den (Assyrian royal iconography weaponized against itself).

Themes: divine vengeance against imperial cruelty — Assyria's atrocities (deportations, flayings, terror as policy) had been the dominant geopolitical horror of the ancient Near East for two centuries. Nahum is the OT's most concentrated celebration of an enemy's destruction. The book has no call to repentance for Nineveh; YHWH's wrath is non-negotiable. Comfort for Judah (1:15, "behold, on the mountains the feet of him who brings good tidings… celebrate your feasts").

The theological tension with [[book-of-jonah]] is acute and intentional in the canonical shape of the [[twelve|Twelve]]. Jonah's Nineveh repents and is spared; Nahum's Nineveh is annihilated. Critical reading: two prophetic theologies of YHWH's relation to imperial powers are juxtaposed without resolution. Traditional harmonization: Nineveh repented in Jonah's day, relapsed by Nahum's, and was destroyed for the relapse. Either way, the canon refuses to reduce divine action to a single algorithm.

Reception: Nah 1:15 ("the feet of him who brings good tidings") is echoed in [[book-of-isaiah]] 52:7 and cited in [[romans]] 10:15. Patristic interpretation often spiritualized Nineveh as the devil's stronghold to mute the book's celebratory violence — a discomfort that persists in modern lectionaries, which sparingly use Nahum. A pesher commentary on Nahum (4QpNah) from Qumran applies the oracles to Hellenistic-period politics, demonstrating early reception as a typological key.
