Theological novella of beleaguered Israel delivered by a widow's deceit and a sword. The Assyrian general [[holofernes]], dispatched by "Nebuchadnezzar king of the Assyrians" (an anachronism — Nebuchadnezzar was Babylonian, not Assyrian), besieges the (fictional) Judean town of Bethulia. As the town nears surrender, [[judith]] — a wealthy, beautiful, observant widow of Manasseh — leaves with her maid for the enemy camp, charms Holofernes over four days, gets him drunk in his tent, and decapitates him with his own sword. She returns with the head; the Assyrians panic at dawn; Israel routs them. Judith leads the victory song (ch. 16), retires to her estate, manumits her maid, and dies at 105 in peace.

Date and provenance. The opening verse — "It was the twelfth year of the reign of Nebuchadnezzar, who ruled over the Assyrians in the great city of Nineveh" — is a sustained, deliberate jumble of historical periods: Nebuchadnezzar misplaced as Assyrian, Nineveh long-fallen, Holofernes and Bagoas as Persian-era names, the postexilic temple cult presupposed. Most critical scholars read this as deliberate genre-signaling: the book is a parabolic fiction, not a chronicle, likely composed in Hebrew in the mid-to-late 2nd c. BCE in the Hasmonean period, when its themes (Torah-observant resistance, sanctity of Jerusalem, dietary fidelity in pagan contexts) resonated with the [[1-maccabees|Maccabean]] situation. The Hebrew original is lost; surviving witnesses are Greek (LXX), Old Latin, and Jerome's Vulgate (translated, like Tobit, from an Aramaic text in haste).

Structure: two unequal halves. Chs. 1-7 set the geopolitical stage — Nebuchadnezzar's campaigns, his vengeance on the West, Holofernes' march, Bethulia's siege, Achior the Ammonite's speech on Israel's God-defended history (5:5-21). Chs. 8-16 narrate Judith's prayer (ch. 9, echoing the destruction of Simeon and Levi's enemies at Shechem), her infiltration, the assassination (12:10-13:10), the rout, and the canticle (16:1-17).

Themes: deception as righteous instrument (Judith lies repeatedly to Holofernes), feminine agency in the patriarchal-narrative mold of [[jael]] and [[deborah]], the kashrut-observant Jew operating cleanly inside pagan space, the impotence of military might before God who "crushes wars" (16:2), the centrality of Jerusalem and its Temple, the inviolability of Israel when Torah is kept. Theological discomfort with Judith's seductive deceit shaped a long apologetic tradition.

Canonical history: in the LXX and Vulgate; cited by [[clement-of-rome]] (1 Clem 55) as exemplum of fortitude alongside [[esther]]. Patristic and medieval reception generally enthusiastic; rabbinic Judaism preserved no copy and the book is absent from Qumran. The Council of Trent confirmed it; Reformers excluded it. Jerome called it canonical "by usage."

Reception: Judith became a stock subject of Christian art and ethics. Donatello's bronze and Caravaggio's, Artemisia Gentileschi's, and Klimt's paintings of Judith with Holofernes' head span medieval allegory (humility crushing pride; Mary crushing Satan), Renaissance civic-republican iconography (Florence as Judith), Baroque psychological realism, and modern fin-de-siècle eroticism. Hildegard, Christine de Pizan, and modern feminist criticism each read Judith as an authorizing precedent for female agency, with varying ethical readings of her means.
