Four chapters of third-person narrative — formally unique among the [[twelve|Twelve]], which otherwise contain oracles. Jonah preaches one short sentence (3:4); the rest is story. The book reads less as collected prophecy than as theological short fiction about a prophet.

[[jonah]] ben Amittai is named at 2 Kings 14:25 as a prophet in the reign of Jeroboam II (early 8th c.), giving the book a notional setting in the period of Assyrian ascendancy. Critical scholarship overwhelmingly treats the book as post-exilic composition (5th-4th c.) using the earlier Jonah as a literary figure. Indicators: late-biblical Hebrew with Aramaisms; the description of [[nineveh]] as an enormous past-tense city ("Nineveh was an exceedingly great city," 3:3); theological universalism that fits Persian-period intra-Jewish debate; apparent intertextual play with [[book-of-jeremiah]] 18 and other earlier texts. Conservative scholarship maintains 8th-c. composition and full historicity.

Plot: YHWH commissions Jonah to preach against [[nineveh]]; Jonah flees toward Tarshish; storm at sea; sailors throw him overboard; the great fish; Jonah's psalm of thanksgiving from the fish's belly (ch. 2, often regarded as a redactional insertion of an independent psalm); recommissioning; Nineveh's mass repentance — humans and animals in sackcloth; YHWH relents; Jonah's anger and the qiqayôn plant; the book's closing question ("should I not be concerned about Nineveh?") left unanswered.

Genre debate: history (traditional), parable (Augustine called it a typum, but parable proper is a modern category), allegory (Israel-as-Jonah swallowed and disgorged from exile), satire (Jonah as the failed prophet, repeatedly outdone in piety by Gentile sailors and Ninevites), didactic novella (current critical consensus). The category matters because it determines what kind of claim the book is making about Nineveh's actual fate.

Themes: divine compassion extends to enemies — Jonah's complaint (4:2) reverses the classic YHWH-credo of [[exodus]] 34:6-7, citing it as a grievance ("I knew you are a gracious and compassionate God…"). Universalism / particularism tension; the freedom of God to relent; the prophet's vocation when one's preaching succeeds. The contrast with [[book-of-nahum]] (Nineveh as object of unrelieved divine wrath) is so sharp that the [[twelve|Twelve]] juxtaposes incommensurate theologies of the same city.

Reception: [[jesus]] cites the "sign of Jonah" — three days and nights, plus the Ninevites' repentance as judgment against an unrepentant generation ([[gospel-of-matthew]] 12:39-41; 16:4; [[gospel-of-luke]] 11:29-32). Christian typology reads Jonah as type of Christ's death and resurrection. Rabbinic and Christian liturgy use Jonah on Yom Kippur (Jewish) and the Easter Vigil (Christian) for the same theological reason: deliverance from depths.
