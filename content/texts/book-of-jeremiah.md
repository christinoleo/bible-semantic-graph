Long, formally heterogeneous prophetic book covering the career of [[jeremiah]] from his call ca. 627 BCE (13th year of [[josiah]]) through the fall of Jerusalem (586 BCE) and the post-conquest flight to Egypt. Spans roughly forty years and four Judahite kings ([[josiah]], Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, [[zedekiah]]).

Textual situation is anomalous: the Septuagint Jeremiah is ~13% shorter than the Masoretic Text and reorders the oracles against the nations (LXX places them mid-book; MT relegates them to chs. 46-51). 4QJerᵇ and 4QJerᵈ from Qumran attest the shorter LXX-type recension, confirming two parallel editions circulated in antiquity. Critical scholarship generally treats the LXX as closer to an earlier edition.

Source-critical analysis (Mowinckel, Duhm) typically isolates three strands: (A) poetic oracles of the prophet himself; (B) biographical prose narratives, frequently attributed to [[baruch]] son of Neriah, Jeremiah's scribe; (C) deuteronomistic prose sermons that share idiom with [[deuteronomy]] and the [[2-kings|Deuteronomistic History]], reflecting later redaction. The Baruch-scroll episode (Jer 36) is the book's own etiology for its written transmission.

Structure: call narrative and early oracles (1-25), biographical prose with conflict narratives (26-45, including the "Confessions of Jeremiah" — laments accusing YHWH of deception, 11:18-12:6; 15:10-21; 17:14-18; 18:18-23; 20:7-18), oracles against the nations (46-51), historical appendix on Jerusalem's fall (52, doublet of [[2-kings]] 24-25).

Themes: covenant lawsuit and judgment, false prophets ([[book-of-jeremiah|Hananiah]] conflict, ch. 28), the "new covenant" written on the heart (31:31-34, cited in [[hebrews]] 8), the seventy years of exile (25:11-12; 29:10), Babylon as YHWH's instrument and then as object of judgment. Jeremiah submits to Babylon politically while opposing it theologically.

Reception: paradigm of the suffering prophet. [[lamentations]] is traditionally appended to his career. [[gospel-of-matthew]] cites Jer 31:15 (Rachel weeping) for the slaughter of the innocents. The new-covenant motif is foundational for NT eucharistic theology. Patristic and rabbinic exegesis both treat Jeremiah's "confessions" as a key resource for the theology of complaint and theodicy.
