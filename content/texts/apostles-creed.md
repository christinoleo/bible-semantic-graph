The fixed twelve-article Western baptismal creed — "I believe in God the Father almighty, creator of heaven and earth; and in Jesus Christ his only Son, our Lord, who was conceived by the Holy Spirit, born of the Virgin Mary, suffered under Pontius Pilate, was crucified, died, and was buried; he descended into hell, the third day he rose again from the dead, he ascended into heaven, sits at the right hand of God the Father almighty, from there he will come to judge the living and the dead; I believe in the Holy Spirit, the holy catholic Church, the communion of saints, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the body, and the life everlasting. Amen." The textus receptus is first attested in Pirminius's *Scarapsus* (c. 750); it descends from the [[old-roman-symbol]] through Gallican and Spanish baptismal traditions and supersedes those regional variants in the Carolingian period as the standard Western baptismal symbol.

The medieval legend assigning one article to each of the [[twelve-apostles]] (attested in Rufinus and in the *Pseudo-Augustinian* Sermon 240) is etiological, not historical — the creed is not a literal apostolic composition but the church's own confessional summary of apostolic teaching, the [[regula-fidei|rule of faith]] in catechetical form. The Eastern church never received the Apostles' Creed and uses the [[nicene-constantinopolitan-creed|Nicene-Constantinopolitan]] form liturgically; the Reformation retained the Apostles' Creed across Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, and Methodist traditions, typically as the primary baptismal symbol alongside the Nicene at the Eucharist.
