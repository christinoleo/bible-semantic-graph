Shortest book in the Hebrew Bible — twenty-one verses, undivided into chapters. A single oracle against [[edom]] for complicity in the destruction of Jerusalem. The prophet "Obadiah" (ʿōḇaḏyāh, "servant of YHWH") is otherwise unidentified — possibly a real figure, possibly a generic designation; thirteen Obadiahs appear elsewhere in the OT and none is plausibly identified with this one.

Dating: most likely composed in the immediate aftermath of Jerusalem's fall in 586 BCE, when Edomite participation in the sack — or at minimum profiting from it — became a touchstone of post-exilic grievance (see [[lamentations]] 4:21-22; [[psalms]] 137:7; [[book-of-ezekiel]] 25:12-14, 35; Mal 1:2-5). Minority view (Wellhausen, Sellin) dates portions to the pre-exilic period.

Structure: oracle proper against Edom (vv. 1-14) with three movements — Edom's pride and coming downfall (1-9), the specific indictment for behavior on the day of Jerusalem's calamity (10-14), the [[day-of-yhwh|Day of YHWH]] against the nations and restoration of Israel (15-21).

The opening verses (1-9) share extensive verbal overlap with [[book-of-jeremiah]] 49:7-22, an oracle against Edom — the direction of dependence is debated (Obadiah quoting Jeremiah; Jeremiah quoting Obadiah; both quoting a common source). The parallel demonstrates intertextual circulation of anti-Edom material in the prophetic corpus.

Themes: lex talionis between nations ("as you have done, it shall be done to you," v. 15); the Day of YHWH universalized; restoration of Israel's territory including possession of Edom and Philistia; the kingdom belongs to YHWH (v. 21, the book's closing line). Edom — descendant of [[esau]], twin brother of [[jacob]] / Israel — functions throughout the prophetic corpus as the archetypal kin-betrayer.

Reception: Edom's symbolic role expands in later Jewish tradition: Rome becomes "Edom" in rabbinic and apocalyptic literature (precisely because Herod was Idumean), and later the Christian Roman empire and even Christendom itself become "Edom." Obadiah is rarely cited directly in the NT but its day-of-YHWH-against-the-nations theme feeds into [[revelation]]'s judgment cycles.
