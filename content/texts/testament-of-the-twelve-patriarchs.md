Twelve sequential farewell addresses, one from each of [[jacob]]'s sons, structured on a common pattern: the dying patriarch gathers his sons, narrates a representative episode from his own life (often drawn from [[genesis]] but heavily expanded), draws moral and theological lessons from it, predicts the future of his tribe, and exhorts his descendants to virtue. The Greek text survives in several recensions; Armenian and Slavonic versions; Hebrew fragments at Qumran of related but not identical Aramaic/Hebrew Levi and Naphtali material (4QTLevi, 4QTNaph). Not in any canon.

Composition is famously contested. The 19th-c. consensus (Schnapp, Charles) treated the work as a Jewish 2nd-c.-BCE composition with later Christian interpolations. De Jonge's mid-20th-c. revisionist position treated it as a fundamentally Christian work of the 2nd c. CE that has incorporated and reshaped Jewish materials. The Qumran finds substantially vindicated the older view that pre-Christian Hebrew/Aramaic Testament-Levi and Testament-Naphtali traditions existed and were source-material; the surviving Greek Testaments are best read as Christian editions of Jewish source-material composed sometime between ~150 BCE and ~200 CE, with the final Christian editor(s) responsible for explicit Christological passages (e.g., TLevi 18, TJud 24).

Each Testament has a "moral signature." Testament of Reuben centers on his sin with Bilhah ([[genesis]] 35:22) and discourses on the "spirit of fornication"; Simeon on envy and the cooling of his envy of [[joseph]]; Levi on priesthood and Levitical-priesthood eschatology, with elaborate visions of seven heavens (TLevi 2-3); Judah on the kingship and on the "two ways" of monarchy; Dan on anger, with a prediction that the antichrist-figure (Beliar) will rise from his tribe; Naphtali on the natural order and the just-ordered life; Gad on hatred; Asher on the two-spirits doctrine of moral psychology; Issachar on simplicity (ἁπλότης); Zebulun on compassion; Joseph on chastity (with extensive expansion of the Potiphar's-wife scene); Benjamin on the pure mind.

The "two spirits" anthropology of Testament of Asher and of Testament of Judah 20 — every person inhabited by a spirit of truth and a spirit of error — parallels the dualistic anthropology of the Qumran *Community Rule* (1QS 3-4) and may inform Pauline flesh/spirit and Johannine truth/falsehood antitheses. The Levi-Judah priest-king diarchy of TLevi 18 and TJud 24 (a star arising from Jacob, a man arising from Levi who shall be a high priest forever) anticipates or reflects the dual-messiah expectation visible at Qumran and is a Christianized re-reading focal point.

Christian interpolations: explicit predictions of [[jesus]] (e.g., TLevi 18:6-12 with baptism and Spirit-descent imagery; TBen 9:3-5 with the rending of the temple veil; TSim 7:1-2 with God appearing on earth), passion predictions, and references to the church. Most scholarship now agrees these are layered onto a Jewish substrate rather than freestanding original composition, though the layers cannot always be cleanly separated.

Reception: known and read by [[origen]] (who cites them in *Homilies on Joshua*) and Tertullian's Latin contemporaries; quoted by [[jerome]]; survived in monastic Greek manuscripts and entered the West through Robert Grosseteste's 1242 Latin translation, where it enjoyed wide medieval circulation as edifying patriarchal wisdom. Modern critical reception dates from Charles (1908) and the Qumran-era discoveries (Milik, Greenfield, Stone) that demonstrated the underlying Aramaic Levi material.
