Third book of the [[torah]]. Cultic-legal core: priestly purity code, sacrificial system, dietary laws, and the Holiness Code.

Source-critically near-monolithic P, with the Holiness Code (Lev 17-26, "H") commonly distinguished as a slightly later priestly stratum with its own theological signature (refrain "you shall be holy, for I, [[yhwh]] your God, am holy"). Final composition typically placed in the exilic-to-Persian period; the [[documentary-hypothesis]] treats Leviticus as the clearest single window onto P.

Structure: sacrificial taxonomy (Lev 1-7) — burnt, grain, peace, sin, guilt offerings; consecration of [[aaron]] and the priesthood (Lev 8-10), including the Nadab-Abihu incident; purity laws on diet, childbirth, skin disease, bodily emissions (Lev 11-15); Day of Atonement ritual (Lev 16) — scapegoat, high priest in the holy of holies; Holiness Code (Lev 17-26) covering blood prohibition, sexual ethics, calendar of festivals, sabbatical and jubilee, blessings and curses; appendix on vows and tithes (Lev 27).

Themes: holiness as separation, ritual purity, atonement, divine presence requiring mediation. The book's logic is spatial — concentric zones of holiness around the tabernacle — and the priest as boundary-maintainer. Modern anthropological readings (Mary Douglas, *Purity and Danger*) recover the dietary laws as a coherent symbolic system rather than arbitrary taboo.

Reception: structuring force for Second Temple Judaism and rabbinic halakhah; Christian supersessionist readings (Hebrews) treat the sacrificial system as typologically fulfilled and abrogated in Christ. Day of Atonement liturgy survives as Yom Kippur. Lev 18 and 20 sit at the center of contemporary debates over sexuality. The Holiness Code's "love your neighbor as yourself" (Lev 19:18) is cited by [[jesus]] as a summary of the law.
