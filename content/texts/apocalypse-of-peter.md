Early-2nd-c. Christian apocalypse pseudepigraphically attributed to the apostle [[peter]], containing one of the earliest detailed Christian tours of heaven and hell. Two recensions survive: a Greek text preserved in the Akhmim fragment (same manuscript as the [[gospel-of-peter]], discovered 1886-87) covering parts of the work, and a fuller Ethiopic version preserved in a 1907 manuscript published by Grébaut. The two recensions diverge substantially; critical scholarship treats the Ethiopic as closer to the original. Not in any canon — but originally considered for the New Testament canon and listed in the Muratorian Fragment (~170-200 CE).

The frame: [[jesus]] on the Mount of Olives is asked by [[peter]] (in private) for signs of his Parousia and the end of the age — a Petrine echo of the Synoptic Olivet Discourse ([[gospel-of-matthew]] 24, [[gospel-of-mark]] 13). The answer is a vision in which Peter is shown the eschatological judgment and the post-judgment fates of the righteous and the damned.

The descriptions of hell are detailed, graphic, and organized by lex talionis: liars hung by the tongue, blasphemers by the tongue over fire, fornicators hung by the genitals, women who aborted in a lake of suppurating refuse, idol-makers tortured by their idols, men and women who exchanged their roles in burning brimstone, usurers in a pit of foul matter up to their knees. The catalog of punishments matched-to-sins becomes the literary template for nearly every subsequent Christian hell-vision through Dante's *Inferno* (mediated via the *Apocalypse of Paul*, which expands and reworks the Petrine material). The Ethiopic preserves a final passage in which God may have mercy on some of the damned at the saints' intercession — a universalist tendency that is muted or absent in later receptions.

The descriptions of paradise are briefer and more conventional: a field of flowers and fragrant trees, white-robed inhabitants with shining faces, a serene atmosphere. The aesthetic asymmetry (rich hell, sparse heaven) is itself characteristic and gets transmitted into later medieval apocalypses.

Canonical status: the Muratorian Fragment lists it as received by some though "some of us do not want it to be read in church"; [[clement-of-alexandria]] cites it as scripture in his *Hypotyposeis*; the *Eclogae Propheticae* attributed to him quotes it without polemic; Eusebius (*Hist. eccl.* 3.25.4) lists it among the antilegomena (disputed books) and ultimately rejects it; subsequent canonical lists (Athanasius's festal letter of 367, the council of Carthage in 397, the Gelasian Decree) exclude it. Its initial near-canonical status — comparable to the [[shepherd-of-hermas]] — and eventual exclusion are a window onto the contested late-2nd / early-3rd-c. process of canon formation.

Composition: probably Roman or Egyptian, ~125-150 CE, drawing on Jewish apocalyptic-tour traditions ([[1-enoch]] 17-36, the [[testament-of-the-twelve-patriarchs]] visionary material) and on Greco-Roman tours of the underworld (Plato's *Republic* 10, *Phaedo*, Virgil's *Aeneid* 6) refracted through Christian eschatological categories. Possible connections to the Bar-Kokhba period (~132-135 CE) have been argued from references to a "false messiah" persecuting the faithful, but this is contested.

Reception: dominant Greek and Latin literary descendants are the *Apocalypse of Paul* (4th c.) and ultimately Dante's *Divina Commedia*. In the Ethiopic tradition the work remained in liturgical-edifying use long after its decanonization in the Greek and Latin churches. Modern critical recovery dates from the Akhmim publication (1892) and especially the Ethiopic publication (1910), which dramatically clarified the work's extent and the dependence of all later Christian hell-visions on Petrine source-material.
