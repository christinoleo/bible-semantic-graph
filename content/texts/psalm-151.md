Brief Davidic psalm preserved outside the canonical 150 in the Septuagint and in Qumran manuscript 11QPs-a, where it appears at the end of a Psalter scroll otherwise mostly familiar. Included in the Greek and Slavonic Orthodox canon and in Ethiopian Tewahedo; absent from the Hebrew Masoretic Psalter, the Catholic canon, and the Protestant canon; an appendix in some Vulgate manuscripts but rejected by Trent.

The LXX version, in seven verses, is autobiographical: David recounts being the youngest of his brothers, shepherd of his father's flock, anointed by [[samuel]] (alluded to via "his prophet"), and slayer of [[goliath]] — events drawn from [[1-samuel]] 16-17. The superscription explicitly disclaims canonical status: "this psalm is autographed of David, and outside the number" (ἔξωθεν τοῦ ἀριθμοῦ), an editorial flag confirming both its Davidic ascription and its supernumerary position.

The Qumran discovery in Cave 11 (11QPs-a, published by Sanders 1965) transformed scholarship. The Hebrew text turned out to be two separate psalms (here called 151A and 151B), longer and more developed than the Greek, with rich pastoral and apparently bridal-mystical imagery ("my hands made a pipe, my fingers a lyre"; "to him who fashions all"). The Septuagint version is a condensed, theologically smoothed Greek translation of one Hebrew exemplar — direct evidence that the Greek translators worked from a Hebrew/Aramaic Vorlage rather than composing freely.

The 11QPs-a scroll's structure has provoked debate: does it represent a divergent canonical Psalter (with David as author of 150+ psalms, not exactly 150), or a liturgical-devotional collection drawing on canonical Psalms but not a canonical edition itself? Sanders argued for the first (the Qumran community's Psalter); Skehan and Talmon for the second. Subsequent fragments at Cave 4 and Masada complicate any single answer; pluriformity in late-Second-Temple Psalter shape is now widely accepted.

Theologically the psalm participates in the recurring [[david]]-as-shepherd-and-musician topos central to later David traditions: the youngest chosen over the elder ([[1-samuel]] 16:10-13), the lyre-player who soothed [[saul]] (16:23), the giant-slayer (ch. 17). The Greek explicitly Christianizes the giant's defeat ("I took away the disgrace from the sons of Israel"), which the Qumran Hebrew describes more concretely.

Liturgical use: chanted in Byzantine Orthros and in the offices of various Eastern churches; Russian Synodal Bibles include it within the Psalter; modern critical editions (Rahlfs, Göttingen) print it after Psalm 150 with the supernumerary marker. Modern scholarly editions (Sanders, Flint) print the Hebrew alongside the Greek for comparison.
