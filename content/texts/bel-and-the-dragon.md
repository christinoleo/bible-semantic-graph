Two short anti-idolatry tales appended to [[book-of-daniel]] in Greek versions, narratively continuing the court-tale cycle of Dan 1-6 into the reign of Cyrus the Persian. In the Vulgate the unit forms Daniel 14; the LXX places it after [[susanna]] at the end of Daniel. The text is preserved, like Susanna, in both Old Greek and Theodotionic recensions, with notable variations.

Tale 1 (Bel, vv. 1-22). King Cyrus worships the Babylonian deity Bel (Akkadian Bēl, the title of Marduk; cf. [[marduk]]), whose temple consumes vast daily offerings — twelve bushels of fine flour, forty sheep, six measures of wine. [[daniel]] refuses to worship the idol, claiming Bel is mere bronze and clay; the king challenges him. Daniel proves the deception by surreptitiously scattering ashes on the temple floor after the offerings are set out and sealing the doors; the next morning the food is consumed but the footprints of the priests, their wives, and their children — entering through a concealed door under the table — incriminate them. Cyrus has the priests executed and the idol destroyed.

Tale 2 (the Dragon, vv. 23-42). A great living serpent (Greek drakōn, often "dragon" in translation, but more straightforwardly a large snake) is worshipped at Babylon as a god. Daniel offers to kill it without sword or staff; he feeds it cakes of pitch, fat, and hair, which cause it to burst. The outraged populace forces Cyrus to throw Daniel into the lions' den — here a second time, narratively distinct from Dan 6 — where he survives for seven days. The prophet [[habakkuk]] in Judea is miraculously transported by an angel (carrying him by his hair) to bring Daniel a meal of stew, then returned. On the seventh day Cyrus discovers Daniel alive, releases him, and his accusers are thrown to the lions and devoured.

Composition. Original language disputed; an Aramaic or Hebrew Vorlage is possible but not demonstrable. Aramaic Daniel-cycle fragments at Qumran (4Q551 "Daniel-Susanna(?)" and 4Q243-245 "Pseudo-Daniel") attest a wider Aramaic Daniel literature in the late Second-Temple period, but none of these is identifiable as the Vorlage of Bel. Date: late Persian to mid-Hellenistic period (4th-2nd c. BCE), generally placed close to Susanna and to the wider Daniel-cycle expansions.

Genre. Polemical-satirical court tale. The two episodes share an anti-idolatry didactic spine, executed through investigative forensics (Bel) and slapstick destruction (the Dragon) — closer in register to popular folktale than to either prophetic or apocalyptic Daniel. The Bel narrative reads as a detective story (one of the earliest in world literature, sometimes cited as such by historians of the genre); the Dragon-feeding episode mocks Babylonian cult by an inversion of sacrifice (the "god" eats and dies of indigestion). The Habakkuk-by-the-hair vignette is unmistakably comic.

Themes. Exposure of priestly fraud; the inanimateness of idols; the limits of empire (Cyrus' personal piety toward Bel does not survive its proof of fraudulence); Daniel's combined sapiential, forensic, and prophetic competence; God's care for his prophet across geography. The book is the OT-and-Apocrypha's most explicit polemic against living-animal worship.

Reception. Cited or alluded to by [[origen]] (Letter to Africanus, defending the deuterocanonical Daniel material against Africanus' philological objections), [[jerome]] (who included it in the Vulgate while flagging its non-Hebrew status), [[athanasius]]; less frequently cited than Susanna in patristic exegesis, more frequently illustrated in medieval and Renaissance art (Bel's broken idol, the cake-fed dragon, Daniel in the lions' den, Habakkuk lifted by the angel). Modern readings emphasize its satirical-polemical genre as a Hellenistic-Jewish response to the surrounding religious world. Excluded from the Tanakh and from Protestant canons; the King James Apocrypha prints it as "The History of the Destruction of Bel and the Dragon."
