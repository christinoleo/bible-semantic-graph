Sixty-six chapters traditionally ascribed to [[isaiah]] son of Amoz, 8th-c. Jerusalem prophet under [[hezekiah]]. Since [[documentary-hypothesis]]-era criticism (Döderlein 1775, Duhm 1892) mainstream scholarship partitions the book into at least three strata: First Isaiah (1-39, Assyrian-period oracles, Judah and Jerusalem under [[hezekiah]] and the [[ezekiel|earlier]] kings), Deutero-Isaiah (40-55, exilic, Babylonian setting, addresses Cyrus by name in 44:28-45:1), Trito-Isaiah (56-66, post-exilic Yehud, restoration disputes). Conservative scholarship preserves unitary authorship via predictive prophecy; critical consensus holds the multi-stratum view.

First Isaiah (1-39): woe-oracles against Judah's social injustice and cultic complacency; commission vision (Isa 6); the Immanuel sign (Isa 7:14, addressed to Ahaz during the Syro-Ephraimite crisis); oracles against the nations (13-23); Isaiah apocalypse (24-27, likely later insertion); Hezekiah/Sennacherib narrative (36-39, paralleled in [[2-kings]] 18-20).

Deutero-Isaiah (40-55): comfort oracles to Babylonian exiles, monotheistic polemic against idols, Cyrus as YHWH's anointed (45:1). Contains the four [[servant-songs|Servant Songs]] (42:1-4; 49:1-6; 50:4-9; 52:13-53:12) — identity of the servant (Israel collectively, the prophet, a future figure, the Davidic messiah) is the longest-running interpretive debate in the book.

Trito-Isaiah (56-66): inclusion of foreigners and eunuchs (56), false leadership polemic, new heavens and new earth (65-66). Reflects post-exilic intra-community conflict over temple, sacrifice, and the boundaries of the restored community.

Themes: holiness of YHWH (qādôš yiśrāʾēl), remnant theology, Zion's vindication, universal eschatology, suffering-and-vindication of the servant. The book is the OT's most theologically expansive prophetic corpus.

Reception: most quoted OT book in the NT alongside [[psalms]]. [[gospel-of-matthew]] mines Isa 7:14, 9:1-2, 53; [[acts]] 8 stages the Ethiopian eunuch reading Isa 53; [[romans]] 9-11 leans on Isaianic remnant theology. Rabbinic tradition makes Isaiah the prophet of consolation. The 1QIsaᵃ scroll from Qumran (2nd c. BCE) is the longest complete biblical manuscript among the Dead Sea Scrolls, attesting the book's textual stability.
