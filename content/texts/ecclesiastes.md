Skeptical-wisdom book voiced by *Qohelet* ("the Assembler" / "Preacher"), a sage who interrogates the value of wisdom, labor, pleasure, justice, and life itself under the recurring refrain *hevel hevalim* — "vapor of vapors, all is vapor" (often rendered "vanity"). One of the five Megillot, read at Sukkot.

Persona-attribution to [[solomon]] is signaled obliquely — "son of David, king in Jerusalem" (Eccl 1:1, 12) — but never names Solomon directly; critical scholarship unanimously treats the Solomonic frame as a *persona*, with composition placed in the late Persian or Hellenistic period (4th-3rd c. BCE) on linguistic grounds (late Hebrew, Aramaisms, Persian loanwords like *pardes*). Possible cross-pollination with Hellenistic philosophical schools — Cynicism, early Stoicism, Epicureanism — has been argued but remains debated; ANE precursors (the Egyptian *Dialogue of a Man with His Ba*, Mesopotamian skeptical wisdom) are equally relevant.

Structure: opening poem on the futility of toil (Eccl 1); royal autobiography of pursuit of pleasure and wisdom (Eccl 2); the famous *cattim* poem on times and seasons (Eccl 3); reflections on injustice, oppression, wealth, mortality, the limits of wisdom (Eccl 4-11); closing poem on aging and death (Eccl 12:1-8); epilogue in a different voice commending fear of God and Torah-keeping (Eccl 12:9-14), often regarded as a redactional softening.

Themes: *hevel* — vapor, breath, transience — as governing metaphor; the limits of human knowledge under "the sun"; pleasure as legitimate consolation within those limits ("eat your bread with joy"); the irreducibility of death; the failure of retributive theology held in canonical tension with [[proverbs]]; possible faint hope in divine accountability (Eccl 12:14).

Reception: rabbinic disputes over canonicity at Yavneh focused on Qohelet alongside the [[song-of-songs]]; the epilogue's commendation of Torah-piety likely secured its place. Patristic readings often allegorized Qohelet as voice of vanitas-mundi; medieval Jewish exegesis (Ibn Ezra, Rashi) explored the *hevel* metaphor with subtlety. Modern reception treats Qohelet as a uniquely candid biblical witness to existential limit, comparable to Job (with which it shares the wisdom-skeptical project) and to later existentialist literature.
