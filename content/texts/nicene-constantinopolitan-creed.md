The expanded form of the [[nicene-creed]] promulgated at [[constantinople-i]] (381), ratified at [[chalcedon]] (451), and used liturgically across most of historic Christendom — the creed most people mean when they say "the Nicene Creed." C adds to N a full pneumatological clause ("and in the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the giver of life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified, who spoke through the prophets"), a developed Christological narrative ("was incarnate of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary and was made man, was crucified for us under Pontius Pilate and suffered and was buried, and rose again on the third day according to the Scriptures, and ascended into heaven and sits on the right hand of the Father, and shall come again with glory to judge the living and the dead, whose kingdom shall have no end"), four ecclesiological-eschatological articles ("one holy catholic and apostolic Church; one baptism for the remission of sins; we look for the resurrection of the dead; and the life of the age to come"), and drops the anti-Arian anathemas of 325.

The new pneumatological clause is the council's principal dogmatic act — *kyrion* (Lord), *zōopoion* (life-giver), *ek tou Patros ekporeuomenon* (proceeding from the Father), *synproskynoumenon kai syndoxazomenon* (co-worshipped, co-glorified) — designed to refute the [[macedonianism|Pneumatomachoi]] without using the term [[homoousios]] (kept implicit, since the council's policy was to extend Nicaea, not re-debate its term). The Latin West later inserts *Filioque* — "and from the Son" — to make explicit a procession through the Son that Augustine had articulated; the Greek East rejects the interpolation as both an unauthorized creedal alteration and a theological error compromising the [[trinity|Trinitarian]] monarchy of the Father. The [[filioque]] dispute becomes the chief proximate doctrinal cause of the [[great-schism|East-West schism]] of 1054. C remains the dogmatic baptismal symbol confessed by Catholic, Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, Reformed, Methodist, and most other historic Christian traditions.
