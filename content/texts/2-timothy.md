Second Pastoral Epistle, framed as a valedictory or "testament" letter — [[paul]] writes from imprisonment in Rome (1:8, 16-17; 2:9) facing imminent execution (4:6-8, "I am already being poured out as a libation, and the time of my departure has come"). The most personally toned of the Pastorals; defenders of authenticity have argued 2 Timothy is the strongest candidate of the three for genuine Pauline authorship even if 1 Timothy and Titus are not.

Authenticity debate: shares the Pastorals' vocabulary, style, and ecclesiastical idiom problems (see [[1-timothy]]) and is therefore typically classified as pseudepigraphic in mainstream critical scholarship. The valedictory frame is read by critics as a pseudonymous device (the "testament" genre is common in Second Temple Judaism — Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs, etc.) creating a legacy voice for the apostolic generation. Conservative defenders argue 2 Timothy is composed close enough to Paul's death (mid-60s CE) to retain Pauline traces directly, possibly with secretarial composition explaining stylistic differences.

Structure: opening and call to courage in the gospel (1); charge to suffer as a good soldier of Christ Jesus, with images of soldier/athlete/farmer (2); warning of "the last days" and the corruption of those who oppose the truth (3); final charge to preach the word "in season and out of season", personal news, and farewell (4).

Key passages: 2 Tim 3:16-17 — "all scripture is breathed-out-by-God (θεόπνευστος, theopneustos) and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, for training in righteousness" — the central proof-text for doctrines of biblical inspiration, especially Protestant scholastic and modern evangelical formulations of inerrancy. The referent of γραφή here is the OT/LXX, not yet a closed NT canon; later traditions extend it. 2 Tim 4:6-8 ("I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith") is the model martyrological farewell.

Personal references: Demas has deserted, "in love with this present world" (4:10); Crescens has gone to Galatia, Titus to Dalmatia; only [[luke]] remains; [[mark]] is to be brought ("he is useful to me for ministry" — apparent reconciliation after the rift implied in [[acts]] 15:36-41); Carpus at Troas holds Paul's cloak and the parchments. Onesiphorus is commended (1:16-18, with intriguing past-tense language read by some as evidence Onesiphorus is deceased — fueling Catholic-Protestant debate over prayer for the dead). Alexander the coppersmith opposed Paul (4:14).

Eschatological emphasis: "the last days" are characterized by moral collapse and false teachers (3:1-9); the coming "appearing" (ἐπιφάνεια) of the Lord (4:1, 8) frames the urgency.

Major figures: [[paul]] (author or claimed author), [[timothy]] (recipient), [[luke]] (faithful companion to the end, 4:11), [[mark]] (4:11), Onesiphorus (1:16-18), Hymenaeus and Philetus (false teachers, 2:17, "their talk will spread like gangrene"), Demas (deserter, 4:10), Lois and Eunice (Timothy's grandmother and mother, 1:5 — model of generational faith).

Reception: theopneustos (3:16) is the central biblical-doctrine proof-text in the Reformation, Protestant scholasticism, and modern evangelical bibliology. Onesiphorus passages are debated in Catholic-Protestant doctrine on intercession for the dead. The valedictory frame supplies the archetype of "last words of the apostle" in Christian devotional and homiletical tradition.
