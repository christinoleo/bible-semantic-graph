Third book of the Former Prophets; with [[2-samuel]] originally a single scroll in the Hebrew (the division is Septuagintal, where the four "Books of Reigns/Kingdoms" cover Samuel and Kings). End of the judges era and rise of the Israelite monarchy.

Part of the Deuteronomistic History. Critical scholarship identifies multiple older source strata folded into the Deuteronomistic frame: an "ark narrative" (1 Sam 4-6 + 2 Sam 6), a "rise of David" or "history of David's rise" (1 Sam 16 - 2 Sam 5), and competing pro- and anti-monarchic strands (1 Sam 8-12 famously contains both a celebratory account of Saul's anointing and a sharp prophetic critique of kingship as apostasy). The "history of David's rise" is widely held to be the oldest sustained Hebrew prose narrative.

Structure: [[samuel]]'s birth and call (1 Sam 1-3); ark narrative and Philistine wars (1 Sam 4-7); demand for a king and anointing of [[saul]] (1 Sam 8-12); Saul's reign and rejection (1 Sam 13-15); David's rise — anointing by Samuel, Goliath, Saul's jealousy, David and [[jonathan-son-of-saul]]'s covenant, David as fugitive, sparing of Saul (1 Sam 16-30); death of Saul and Jonathan at Mount Gilboa (1 Sam 31).

Themes: charismatic vs dynastic leadership, prophetic-royal tension, divine election and rejection, the theological problem of monarchy as both gift and apostasy, friendship across rival lines (David and Jonathan). Saul emerges as one of the Hebrew Bible's most complex tragic figures.

Reception: rabbinic and patristic readings have long mined the David-Jonathan covenant, the witch of Endor episode (1 Sam 28) for debates over necromancy and afterlife, and the Hannah narrative as model prayer (her song prefigures the Magnificat of Mary in [[gospel-of-luke]]). Critical-historical scholarship has used Samuel-Kings as the testing ground for the very existence of a United Monarchy (Finkelstein vs Dever debates).
