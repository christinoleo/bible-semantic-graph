Long Roman Christian apocalyptic-paranetic work in five Visions, twelve Mandates, and ten Similitudes (parables), composed in Greek in the city of Rome in the early 2nd c. CE. The narrative voice is "Hermas," a former slave turned freedman engaged in commerce, who in successive visions encounters an aged woman (revealed as the Church), an angelic Shepherd, and other heavenly figures who instruct him on repentance, ecclesiology, and the Christian life. Included in [[codex-sinaiticus]] (4th c.) at the end of the New Testament — strong evidence of near-canonical status in some early-Christian communities — but excluded from the established canonical lists from the 4th c. onward. Not in any extant canon.

Authorship: the Muratorian Fragment (~170-200 CE) identifies the author as "Hermas, brother of Pius bishop of Rome" (Pius I, ~140-155 CE), a piece of testimony often taken at face value but possibly conventional or anachronistic. Internal evidence (mention of Clement in Vision 2.4.3, plausibly [[clement-of-rome]]; the Christological theology; the form of church order) admits dating anywhere from ~95 to ~150 CE. A multi-stage composition (Giet, Coleborne) over decades is plausible: the early Visions ~90-100, the Mandates and Similitudes ~120-150.

Theological controversies center on post-baptismal sin. The central proclamation of the work is that one second repentance is available for sins committed after baptism — a softening of what Hermas presents as the stricter ambient teaching that baptism cleanses one and definitively. Mandate 4.3 grants "one repentance" to those who have sinned after the seal of baptism, with no third opportunity. This contested concession became enormously influential in shaping penitential theology and the doctrine of the unforgivable second-after-baptism apostasy; Tertullian's strict Montanist rejection of the *Shepherd* (*De pudicitia*) is largely a counter to this position.

The five Visions (chs. 1-25 in modern continuous numbering): the aged-lady-as-Church visions, structured around progressive revelations and ending with the appearance of the Shepherd; the twelve Mandates (chs. 26-49) on faith, fear of God, truthfulness, chastity, patience, the two ways of righteousness and wickedness, the two angels in each person (cf. the two-spirits anthropology of [[testament-of-the-twelve-patriarchs]] and the Qumran *Community Rule*), and prayer; the ten Similitudes (chs. 50-114) including the famous Tower-Building vision (Sim. 9), where the Church is a tower being built of living stones representing varieties of Christian discipleship.

Christology and pneumatology in the *Shepherd* are unusual and have made the work theologically uncomfortable for later orthodoxy. The Holy Spirit is identified with the Son of God in some passages (Sim. 5.6.5: "the Holy Spirit, which is the Son of God"); the pre-existent Son is sometimes said to be the Spirit; an angelic Christology is in evidence; baptism is essential to salvation including for the patriarchs of the Old Testament, who are baptized in Hades by the apostles before being allowed into the Tower (Sim. 9.16). These features — common in late-1st- / early-2nd-c. Christianity — became increasingly difficult to integrate with Nicene categories, contributing to the work's eventual decanonization.

Reception: enormously popular in the 2nd-4th c. — [[irenaeus]], [[clement-of-alexandria]], and [[origen]] cite it as scripture; many Greek and Latin manuscripts survive; the *Shepherd* is bound into [[codex-sinaiticus]] and into early Latin biblical manuscripts; the work is mentioned in lectionaries and in monastic spiritual reading lists. Decanonization is gradual and never fully completed — the Ethiopian tradition continued to esteem it; modern Catholic and Orthodox reception treats it as a venerable patristic-era document but not scripture. The *Shepherd* is one of the most important Apostolic Fathers texts and a primary source for the social and theological life of 2nd-c. Roman Christianity.
