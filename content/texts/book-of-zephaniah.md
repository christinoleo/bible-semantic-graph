Three chapters. Superscription (1:1) provides the longest prophetic genealogy in the OT — Zephaniah son of Cushi son of Gedaliah son of Amariah son of Hezekiah — possibly identifying him as a descendant of King [[hezekiah]] (though the identification is uncertain) and certainly locating him in the Judahite aristocracy. Dated under [[josiah]] (640-609 BCE), most likely early in his reign before the 622 BCE Deuteronomic reform, given the book's polemic against ongoing syncretism (Baal, host of heaven, Milcom, those who "swear by YHWH and also by Milcom" — 1:4-5).

Structure: oracles of universal judgment, the [[day-of-yhwh|Day of YHWH]] over Judah (1:1-2:3); oracles against the nations — Philistia, Moab, Ammon, Cush, Assyria (2:4-15); oracles of judgment and salvation focused on Jerusalem and the gathering of the nations (3:1-20).

The book is dominated by Day-of-YHWH imagery. Zeph 1:14-16 ("the great day of YHWH is near… a day of wrath, a day of distress and anguish, a day of ruin and devastation, a day of darkness and gloom") furnishes the Latin Dies Irae of the medieval requiem, indirectly through the Vulgate. The book quotes liberally from earlier prophetic Day-of-YHWH material ([[book-of-amos]] 5, [[book-of-joel]]) and consolidates the motif.

Themes: universalized judgment — the opening oracle (1:2-3) envisions the un-creation of all flesh, possibly the OT's most cosmic judgment statement; remnant theology — "a humble and lowly people" (3:12, ʿānî wādal) who will survive and be the seed of restoration; gathering of the nations to worship YHWH (3:9, "I will change the speech of the peoples to a pure speech, that all of them may call upon the name of YHWH"); the divine warrior rejoicing over Jerusalem (3:17, "YHWH your God is in your midst, a warrior who gives victory; he will rejoice over you with gladness").

Critical scholarship debates the unity of the book — Zeph 2:7, 9 (references to a "remnant of Judah") and the restoration material in ch. 3 are often treated as post-exilic additions to a 7th-c. core. The oracle against Assyria (2:13-15) fits the Josianic moment well, since Assyrian power was visibly waning.

Reception: less cited in the NT than other minor prophets, but the Day-of-YHWH apocalyptic vocabulary it consolidates feeds into Synoptic eschatology (Mark 13 / Matt 24) and [[revelation]]. The Dies Irae (13th c., attributed to Thomas of Celano) cites Zephaniah by name and made Zephaniah a fixture of Western liturgical mortality-meditation through the dropping of the requiem sequence in 1970.
