Forty-eight chapters set entirely in Babylonian exile. [[ezekiel]] son of Buzi, a [[zadokite|Zadokite]] priest deported in 597 BCE with Jehoiachin, dates his oracles by year-from-deportation — the most precisely dated prophetic book (1:1-3; 8:1; 20:1; 24:1; 26:1; etc., spanning ca. 593-571 BCE).

Critical scholarship since Hölscher and Zimmerli has debated the degree of redaction. Zimmerli's two-volume Hermeneia commentary establishes a layered model: an authentic Ezekielian core (visions, sign-acts, dated oracles) with extensive "Fortschreibung" — successive redactional updating by a "school of Ezekiel" with priestly idiom. Greenberg and others defend a holistic reading of larger compositional units. The book's striking uniformity of voice supports either a single dominant editorial hand or close disciple-circle transmission.

Structure: call vision and commission (1-3, the merkavah / chariot-throne); oracles of judgment against Jerusalem (4-24, framed by the fall of the city in ch. 24); oracles against the nations (25-32, seven nations); oracles of restoration (33-39, including the dry-bones vision in 37 and the Gog of Magog oracle in 38-39); the temple vision (40-48, an idealized restored sanctuary with detailed measurements and a regulated polity).

Distinctive features: the prophet as "son of man" (ben ʾādām, ca. 93 occurrences); elaborate sign-acts (eating a scroll, lying on each side, shaving the head); concentrated divine glory (kāḇôd) theology — the glory departs the temple (chs. 8-11) and returns to the new temple (43); priestly purity vocabulary throughout. The detailed merkavah of ch. 1 became the foundational text for Jewish mystical tradition (Maʿaśeh Merkavah / Hekhalot literature).

Tensions: Ezekiel's temple law (chs. 40-48) diverges in measurable ways from the Pentateuchal cultic legislation (different sacrificial calendar, distinct land-allotment, restricted Levitical roles). The Talmud (b. Shabbat 13b; b. Menahot 45a) records that the rabbis nearly excluded Ezekiel from the canon and credit Hananiah ben Hezekiah with reconciling the contradictions over 300 jars of oil. Modern critical scholarship reads this divergence as evidence that Ezekiel's program represents a competing or supplementary priestly tradition.

Reception: foundational for apocalyptic literature ([[book-of-daniel]], 1 Enoch, [[revelation]]'s throne vision and Gog/Magog draw directly on Ezek 1, 38-39, 40-48). NT shepherd discourses ([[gospel-of-john]] 10) build on Ezek 34. Christian typology reads the dry bones as resurrection; Jewish tradition reads them as national restoration.
