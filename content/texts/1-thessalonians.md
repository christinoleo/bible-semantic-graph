Earliest undisputed Pauline letter and therefore — barring the contested early dating of [[galatians]] — the earliest document in the NT, written ca. 50-51 CE from Corinth shortly after [[paul]] left Thessalonica abruptly (cf. [[acts]] 17:1-10). The letter predates the Gospels by roughly two decades and gives the earliest documentary window onto Christian belief and practice.

Setting: Thessalonica was the capital of the Roman province of Macedonia, a free city on the Via Egnatia. [[paul]] founded the church on his second journey, accompanied by [[silas]] (Silvanus) and [[timothy]] (1:1 lists all three as co-senders). Sudden persecution forced Paul's departure; the letter follows after [[timothy]] returned with a positive report on the Thessalonians' faith and concerns about their grief over deceased members (3:1-6; 4:13).

Structure: thanksgiving and reminiscence (1-3), occupying an unusually large proportion of the letter; paraenesis (4-5). Thanksgiving section reviews the founding of the community, Paul's manner of ministry, his anxiety during separation, and Timothy's mission. Paraenesis: sexual ethics (4:1-8), love among believers (4:9-12), grief and the parousia (4:13-18), the day of the Lord (5:1-11), community life (5:12-22), closing benediction (5:23-28).

Eschatological core (4:13-5:11): the Thessalonians had evidently expected the parousia ("coming", παρουσία) to occur before any of them died; the deaths of some have caused acute pastoral crisis. Paul's response: those "who have fallen asleep" will not be disadvantaged — the Lord will descend, the dead in Christ rise first, then "we who are alive, who are left" will be caught up (ἁρπαγησόμεθα) to meet the Lord in the air (4:17). This passage is the proof-text for the "rapture" in dispensationalist eschatology (Darby, Scofield); critical and mainline scholarship reads it as standard apocalyptic imagery — the parousia visualized as imperial arrival (a city's citizens going out to meet the visiting emperor), not as a separate event from a "second-coming" later. The "day of the Lord" comes "like a thief in the night" (5:2).

Theological features: trinitarian formulae avant la lettre (1:1, 5-6); imitation as discipleship motif (1:6; 2:14); the parousia as horizon of every chapter (1:10; 2:19; 3:13; 4:13-18; 5:23); Paul's defense of his ministry as gentle and self-supporting ("we worked night and day", 2:9); the most extended treatment in the undisputed letters of pneumatic and eschatological community life.

The 2:14-16 passage on "the Jews who killed the Lord Jesus" has been the subject of major exegetical debate — some critics (Pearson 1971) argue it is a non-Pauline interpolation reflecting post-70 anti-Jewish polemic; defenders note its presence in all manuscripts and treat it as Paul's intra-Jewish rhetoric. The verse has weighed heavily in the Christian-Jewish dialogue.

Major figures: [[paul]] (author), [[silas]]/Silvanus (co-sender), [[timothy]] (co-sender and emissary).

Reception: the rapture-theology lineage from Darby through Scofield to Lindsey's Late Great Planet Earth and the Left Behind franchise has its primary text here, an interpretive trajectory unrecognizable to the first 18 centuries of Christian exegesis. Mainline and critical readings treat 4:13-18 as imminent-parousia consolation. The work-ethic verses (4:11-12; cf. [[2-thessalonians]] 3:10) generate the Protestant labor-discipline tradition.
