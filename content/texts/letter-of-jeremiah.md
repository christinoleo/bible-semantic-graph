Single-chapter (72 verses in LXX numbering) diatribe against idolatry, framed as a letter from [[jeremiah]] to Israelites about to be deported to Babylon, warning against the idols they will see there. Pseudepigraphic — the historical [[jeremiah]] is not the author. The work is preserved in Greek (LXX) as a separate book; Jerome's Vulgate appended it as chapter 6 of [[book-of-baruch]], a placement preserved in the Catholic deuterocanon (where citations read "Bar 6:X") but rejected by the Greek and Slavonic Orthodox tradition (which numbers it as a distinct book and lists it separately).

Composition. Original language disputed; a Hebrew or Aramaic Vorlage is widely posited on the basis of Semitisms and translation evidence, but no Semitic copy is extant — though a small Greek fragment from Qumran (7QpapEpJer gr = 7Q2) preserves a few verses, attesting circulation in the late Second-Temple period. Date is uncertain: termini are set by literary dependence on Jer 10 (so post-exilic) and by the Qumran fragment (pre-1st c. CE); most scholars place composition in the late Persian to mid-Hellenistic period (4th-2nd c. BCE), possibly in Diaspora.

Genre and style. The book is a sustained polemical parody, structured as ten unequal sections each closing on a refrain in the vein of "from this it is evident that they are not gods; so do not fear them" (vv. 16, 23, 29, 65, 69). The author piles satirical detail on idol manufacture, priestly graft, the indignity of cult objects ("bats and swallows alight on their bodies… cats also climb over them" v. 22), and the inability of the gods to act, see, hear, or move under their own power. The piece is closely modeled on the OT idol-polemic tradition of Jer 10:1-16, Isa 40:18-20 and 44:9-20, Ps 115, Ps 135, and shares genre with Wis 13-15 and [[bel-and-the-dragon]]. It is the most thoroughgoing extended idol-polemic in the deuterocanon.

Themes. Cultic monotheism in Diaspora — instruction for Jews living among the visible apparatus of Babylonian (and, by extension, all pagan) religion; the powerlessness of idols stripped of their mystique by cataloguing the trivial physical realities of statuary and priestly economy; the prohibition of fear of foreign gods. The polemic combines rationalist disenchantment (idols cannot save themselves from rust, woodworms, dust, theft) with theological-covenantal exhortation. The piece is markedly free of positive theological exposition; it is sustained negation.

Canonical history. In the LXX as a standalone book (positioned variously after Lamentations or after Baruch). The Vulgate's incorporation of the Letter as Baruch 6 reflects Jerome's source tradition and possibly a scribal grouping motivated by both works' Jeremianic association; the East kept it separate. Hippo, Carthage, Florence, and Trent affirmed canonicity (as part of Baruch in the Latin reckoning). Excluded from the Tanakh; rabbinic tradition preserves no copy. Reformers excluded; the King James Apocrypha printed it among the Apocryphal books with the title "The Epistle of Jeremie." Patristic citation is comparatively light but real ([[origen]], [[cyprian]], [[lactantius]]); 2 Macc 2:1-3 famously refers to a tradition that Jeremiah instructed the exiles in idolatry-avoidance, possibly alluding to this work or a related Jeremianic legendary stratum.
