Disputed Pauline letter, addressed to the church at Colossae (a small town in the Lycus valley of Asia Minor, ca. 100 miles east of Ephesus) which [[paul]] himself had not founded (cf. 2:1) — the community was evangelized by Epaphras (1:7; 4:12). Critical opinion divides: roughly half treat Colossians as authentic (written from imprisonment ca. 60-62, with Philemon); the other half judge it deutero-Pauline (ca. 70-80, by a disciple, anticipating the move further made in [[ephesians]]).

Arguments against authenticity: stylistic features (long sentences, piled synonyms, expanded relative clauses, ~30 hapax legomena); a developed eschatology in which believers have been "raised with Christ" (2:12; 3:1) — closer to [[ephesians]]' realized eschatology than to the undisputed letters' "now-and-not-yet"; advanced cosmic Christology (1:15-20 hymn); and the literary relationship with Ephesians. Defenders note the genuinely Pauline associates greeted in ch. 4 and the embedded relationship with the indisputably authentic [[philemon]] (Onesimus, Tychicus, Archippus appear in both).

The "Colossian heresy": Paul opposes a teaching characterized as "philosophy and empty deceit" (2:8) involving angel worship (2:18), ascetic regulations ("do not handle, do not taste, do not touch", 2:21), calendar observance, and "elemental spirits of the universe" (στοιχεῖα τοῦ κόσμου, 2:8, 20). Identification has been heavily debated:
- Gnosticizing syncretism (Lightfoot, older scholarship);
- a Jewish-mystical sect resembling Merkabah mysticism (Francis, Stuckenbruck);
- Phrygian folk religion mixed with Jewish elements (Arnold);
- the heresy as Pauline rhetorical construct, not a coherent external system (Hooker, Sumney).

Mainstream now resists "Gnostic" labels (Gnosticism proper is later) in favor of a syncretistic Jewish-Hellenistic mysticism with angelic-ascetic features.

Structure: thanksgiving (1:3-14); the Christ-hymn and the supremacy of Christ (1:15-23); Paul's apostolic suffering and stewardship (1:24-2:5); warning against the false teaching (2:6-23); new life in Christ — putting off the old self, putting on the new (3:1-17); household code (3:18-4:1, wives/husbands, children/parents, slaves/masters — close parallel to [[ephesians]] 5-6); travel arrangements and greetings (4:2-18).

The Christ-hymn (1:15-20): "image of the invisible God, firstborn of all creation", in whom "all things were created…all things hold together", "in him all the fullness was pleased to dwell" (πᾶν τὸ πλήρωμα). High Christology integrating Wisdom-tradition language ([[proverbs]] 8, Wisdom of Solomon 7) and reconciling cosmos and church through the cross. Foundational for patristic and Reformation incarnational theology.

Theological emphases: cosmic Christ subordinating "thrones, dominions, rulers, powers" (1:16); union with Christ in death and resurrection (2:11-13, baptismal); Christ-hymn cosmology; ethical formation as putting off and putting on; the household code's complication of imperial-domestic conventions.

Major figures: [[paul]] (author or pseudepigraphic claimant), [[timothy]] (co-sender), Epaphras (founder of the church), Tychicus (letter carrier), Onesimus (mentioned 4:9 — the slave of [[philemon]]), Aristarchus, Mark (4:10, cousin of Barnabas — possibly the [[mark]] of the gospel tradition), Luke ("the beloved physician", 4:14), Archippus, "Nympha and the church in her house" (4:15 — gender of name textually contested), Demas.

Reception: the Christ-hymn (1:15-20) is foundational for patristic Logos Christology and modern cosmic-Christ theologies (Teilhard, ecological theology). The household codes (3:18-4:1) shaped Christian marriage and slavery doctrine for centuries; their imperial subversion or accommodation is the central modern debate.
