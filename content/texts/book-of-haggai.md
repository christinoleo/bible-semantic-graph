Two chapters, four dated oracles delivered over a four-month span in the second year of Darius I — 520 BCE (1:1, 15; 2:1, 10, 20). The most precisely dated prophetic book in the OT alongside [[book-of-ezekiel]]. Composition almost certainly within years of delivery.

The historical setting is the early Persian period Yehud, eighteen years after Cyrus's 538 BCE decree authorizing return ([[book-of-ezra]] 1). The Temple foundations have been laid but rebuilding has stalled — partly through opposition from local non-returnee populations, partly through economic distress, partly through dispute about timing. Haggai and his contemporary [[zechariah]] are credited in [[book-of-ezra]] 5-6 with mobilizing Zerubbabel (Davidic governor) and Joshua ben Jehozadak (high priest) to resume construction; the Second Temple is dedicated in 515 BCE.

Structure: four oracles by date — call to rebuild the Temple, with diagnosis of present economic hardship as divine response to the people's neglect (1:1-15); Temple's future glory will exceed Solomon's, despite present poverty (2:1-9); priestly torah on holiness and defilement, applied to the people's offerings (2:10-19); messianic-style oracle to Zerubbabel — "I will make you like a signet ring" (2:20-23), reversing the curse on Jehoiachin in [[book-of-jeremiah]] 22:24.

Themes: Temple-restoration as precondition of blessing — Haggai's theology fuses cult, economics, and politics; "consider your ways" (1:5, 7) — the present hardship reread as divine pedagogy; the eschatological shaking of the nations and their wealth coming to Jerusalem (2:6-7, reapplied in [[hebrews]] 12:26-27 to eschatological judgment); Zerubbabel as Davidic hope — the signet-ring oracle is the closest the post-exilic prophets come to a named messianic candidate. Zerubbabel disappears from the historical record shortly after; the Davidic restoration he prefigures does not materialize politically.

Critical scholarship reads Haggai's editing as deliberately paired with [[book-of-zechariah]] 1-8 (and the two are sometimes grouped as "Haggai-Zechariah 1-8"), with shared date-formulas, theological emphasis on Temple, and a common redactional framework. The signet-ring oracle on Zerubbabel raises the question whether Persian authorities intervened against perceived royal pretensions — Zerubbabel's silence in later sources is suggestive but unproven.

Reception: Hag 2:6-7 is cited in [[hebrews]] 12:26-27 to argue that the "shaking" already foreseen by the prophet points eschatologically to the removal of the created order and the establishment of an unshakable kingdom. Patristic and rabbinic readings treat Haggai as the typological theologian of the Second Temple. Modern interest centers on the post-exilic socio-economic and political setting.
