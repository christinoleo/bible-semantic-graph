Longest and most systematic of [[paul]]'s letters, written ca. 56-58 CE from Corinth (cf. 16:1-2, recommendation of Phoebe of Cenchreae; 16:23, host Gaius). Undisputed Pauline authorship. Unique among the undisputed letters in being addressed to a community Paul had not founded and not yet visited — the letter functions as theological self-introduction in preparation for a projected visit en route to Spain (15:22-29), and as a request for support for that mission.

Recipients: the Roman house churches, mixed Jewish and Gentile in composition. Background context likely includes the aftermath of Claudius's expulsion of Jews from Rome (49 CE, cf. [[acts]] 18:2) and the return of Jewish Christians under Nero, producing tension over Torah observance, food laws, and calendar (engaged in chs. 14-15). The "weak" and the "strong" are most plausibly Jewish-Christian and Gentile-Christian factions respectively.

Structure: theological exposition (1-11) followed by paraenesis (12-15) and personal greetings (16). The theological core: universal sinfulness (1:18-3:20), justification by faith apart from works of the law (3:21-5:21), freedom from sin and law through union with Christ (6-7), life in the Spirit (8), the destiny of Israel (9-11), ethical formation of the community (12-15). Chapter 16's extensive personal greetings — and its near-absence from some manuscripts — has prompted theories that it was originally an Ephesian appendix; current consensus retains it as integral.

Key theological moves: "righteousness of God" (δικαιοσύνη θεοῦ) as both divine attribute and gift; faith of/in Jesus Christ (πίστις Χριστοῦ — objective vs subjective genitive debate); [[abraham]] as paradigm of pre-circumcision faith (Rom 4); Adam/Christ typology (Rom 5:12-21); sin and law personified as enslaving powers (Rom 7); cosmic groaning and adoptive sonship (Rom 8); the olive tree allegory and Israel's eventual restoration (Rom 9-11, "and so all Israel will be saved", 11:26 — interpreted in mutually incompatible ways: a future ethnic-Israel restoration, the elect within historic Israel, the church as "true Israel").

New Perspective on Paul (Sanders 1977; Dunn 1983; Wright 1997 onward): rereads "works of the law" not as legalistic merit-seeking but as Jewish boundary markers (circumcision, food, Sabbath), reframing Pauline justification as a doctrine of Gentile inclusion rather than a Reformation-style critique of works-righteousness. Old Perspective (Lutheran-Reformed) defenders (Westerholm, Carson, et al.) push back. The debate continues to structure Pauline studies.

Reception history: the most influential single document in Western theology. Augustine's conversion sparked by Rom 13:13-14 (Confessions 8.12). Luther's reading of Rom 1:17 ("the righteous shall live by faith") catalyzes the Reformation. Calvin's commentary defines Reformed soteriology. Karl Barth's Römerbrief (1919, 2nd ed. 1922) detonates 20th-c. theology, displacing liberal Protestantism. Romans 9-11 is central to Christian-Jewish dialogue after the Shoah.

Major figures: [[paul]] (author), [[priscilla]] and [[aquila]] (greeted in 16:3), Phoebe (carrier of the letter, 16:1-2), Junia (apostle, 16:7 — gender and apostolic status contested).
