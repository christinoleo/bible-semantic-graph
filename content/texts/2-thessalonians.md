Disputed Pauline letter. If authentic, written from Corinth shortly after [[1-thessalonians]] (ca. 51-52 CE). If pseudepigraphic — the majority critical view since Wrede (1903) — written by a later Pauline disciple, perhaps ca. 80-100 CE, to a community confused by precisely the kind of imminent-parousia teaching [[1-thessalonians]] could be read to support.

Authenticity debate: arguments against Pauline authorship include (1) closely paralleled structure with [[1-thessalonians]] (suggesting deliberate imitation); (2) eschatology that delays the parousia (2:3-12) — apparently inconsistent with 1 Thess 4:13-5:11's imminent expectation; (3) the explicit anti-forgery move ("we beg you not to be quickly shaken…either by spirit or by word or by letter, as though from us", 2:2; "I, Paul, write this greeting with my own hand. This is the mark in every letter of mine", 3:17) — read by critics as a pseudepigrapher's misdirection; (4) doctrinally fixed language ("the traditions you were taught by us, either by word or by letter", 2:15). Defenders (Wanamaker, Malherbe) treat the differences as addressing a different facet of the same eschatological problem from a slightly later moment.

Crisis: false teachers have spread the claim that "the day of the Lord has already come" (2:2, ἐνέστηκεν — already present), prompting upheaval, including believers abandoning ordinary work (3:6-12). The author counters with an eschatological timetable.

The man of lawlessness (2:3-12): "the day will not come unless the rebellion (ἀποστασία) comes first, and the man of lawlessness is revealed, the son of destruction (υἱὸς τῆς ἀπωλείας), the one who opposes and exalts himself against every so-called god or object of worship, so that he takes his seat in the temple of God, proclaiming himself to be God." Lawless figure currently "restrained" (τὸ κατέχον / ὁ κατέχων, "what restrains" / "the one who restrains", 2:6-7) — the identity of the restrainer is the most debated single phrase in the letter: the Roman empire (Tertullian, mainstream), the Holy Spirit (dispensationalists), Paul's own preaching, the archangel Michael, an angelic principle. The lawless one will be slain "by the breath of his mouth" at the Lord's parousia.

Background: heavy dependence on Jewish apocalyptic, especially [[book-of-daniel]]'s "abomination that makes desolate" (Dan 8:13; 9:27; 11:31; 12:11) and the Antiochene "self-deifying ruler" tradition (Dan 11:36, "the king…shall exalt himself and magnify himself above every god"). The Caligula crisis (39-41 CE, attempted installation of imperial statue in the Jerusalem temple) is a likely historical resonance.

Structure: thanksgiving and prayer for endurance under persecution (1); eschatological correction (2); paraenesis on prayer, work, and discipline (3, including the famous "if anyone is not willing to work, let him not eat" — 3:10).

Reception: the man-of-lawlessness figure becomes the Antichrist in subsequent Christian tradition, fusing with imagery from [[book-of-daniel]], [[revelation]] (the beast), and [[1-john]]. Major engine of historical apocalyptic identifications (Nero, papacy, Hitler, sundry political figures across centuries). 3:10 anchors the Protestant work-discipline tradition and 20th-c. welfare debates.

Major figures: [[paul]] (author or claimed author), [[silas]], [[timothy]] (co-senders).
