Jewish apocalypse pseudepigraphically attributed to [[baruch]] son of Neriah, [[jeremiah]]'s scribe, set fictively in the immediate aftermath of the Babylonian destruction of the First Temple in 587 BCE but transparently responding to the Roman destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE. Preserved fully only in a 6th-c. Syriac manuscript at the Ambrosian Library in Milan (Codex Ambrosianus B.21 Inf.), discovered and published by Ceriani in 1866. Arabic, Greek (fragmentary), and partial Latin versions survive secondarily. Not in any canon.

Composition: late 1st or very early 2nd c. CE in either Hebrew or Aramaic (Syriac translation evidence points to a Semitic original via Greek). The book responds to the same trauma as [[2-esdras]] — Why did God allow Zion to fall? Why do the righteous suffer while the wicked prosper? What now of the covenant? — but with a more confident theodicy. Where [[2-esdras]]/4 Ezra leaves the human Ezra in unresolved protest against divine inscrutability, 2 Baruch leads Baruch from dialogue through visions to a serene didactic letter.

Structure consists of seven sections of dialogue, lament, vision, and exhortation, climaxing in the Epistle of Baruch (chs. 78-87) addressed to the nine-and-a-half tribes in exile. The Epistle circulated independently as a separate work and is preserved in additional manuscripts; it is the most widely-attested portion. The vision of the cloud with twelve waters alternating dark and bright (chs. 53-74) is a periodized history-of-the-world apocalypse stretching from Adam to the messianic kingdom.

Theological emphases: Adam-blame for death and sin ("each man is the Adam of his own soul," 54:19 — a programmatic Jewish counterpoint to a strong Augustinian reading of Romans 5), the heavenly Jerusalem preserved untouched while the earthly is destroyed (4:2-7), the messianic age followed by a final consummation, bodily resurrection in recognizable form for judgment then transformation (49-51), and the continuing centrality of Torah even after the destruction of Temple cult — the Mishnaic project of post-Temple Judaism is nearly visible on the page.

The book's eschatology features a programmed messianic age with extravagant fertility (29:5: each vine with a thousand branches, each branch a thousand clusters, each cluster a thousand grapes — a passage [[papias]] reportedly heard from "elders of John" and attached to dominical sayings, transmitting it into early Christian millenarian expectation via [[irenaeus]] *Adversus Haereses* 5.33). The motif of the heavenly Jerusalem "prepared" before all things became a fixed apocalyptic-Christian topos (cf. [[revelation]] 21).

Relationship to [[2-esdras]]: the parallels are so close — same period, same crisis, same protagonist-figure, similar visionary structure, related theological vocabulary — that literary dependence in some direction is widely posited, though the direction is contested (Charles, Sayler argued 4 Ezra → 2 Baruch; Bogaert, Murphy, others argued 2 Baruch → 4 Ezra; current consensus leans toward independent witnesses to a shared late-1st-c. apocalyptic milieu).

Reception: little Western Christian reception; circulated in Syriac-speaking churches; influence on later medieval Jewish piety is mediated and indirect (the work was lost to rabbinic Judaism). Modern scholarly recovery since Ceriani has made it a primary text for studying post-Temple Jewish theology, the development of bodily-resurrection doctrine, and the comparative apocalyptic background of New Testament eschatology.
