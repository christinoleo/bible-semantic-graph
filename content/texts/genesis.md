First book of the [[torah]] / Pentateuch. Traditional ascription to [[moses]] is rejected by mainstream critical scholarship; the [[documentary-hypothesis]] partitions Genesis among J, E, and P strands, with final redaction in the Persian period.

Structure splits cleanly: primeval history (Gen 1-11) — creation, the garden, [[cain]] and [[abel]], the flood narrative with [[noah]], Babel — followed by patriarchal cycles (Gen 12-50) on [[abraham]] and [[sarah]], [[isaac]] and [[rebekah]], [[jacob]] / [[esau]] / [[leah]] / [[rachel]], and the Joseph novella (Gen 37-50) centered on [[joseph]] in Egypt.

Two creation accounts (Gen 1:1-2:4a, P; Gen 2:4b-25, J) are the canonical battleground for source criticism — divine name shift (Elohim vs YHWH), divergent order of creation, distinct vocabularies and theologies. Gen 1 parallels with [[enuma-elish]] in structural motifs (primordial waters, separation, ordering) while polemically inverting the combat-myth: [[yhwh]] creates by speech, not theomachy. Flood narrative shows comparable doublet structure and ANE parallels with Gilgamesh tablet XI.

Patriarchal narratives carry the [[abrahamic-covenant]] (land, seed, blessing) through Isaac and Jacob; [[binding-of-isaac-as-test]] at [[moriah]] is the theological crux of the Abraham cycle. The Joseph novella, formally distinct (sustained third-person prose, sapiential overtones, no direct theophany), is often treated as a discrete literary unit, possibly the latest patriarchal stratum.

Major figures: [[adam]], [[eve]], [[seth]], [[noah]], [[shem]], [[ham]], [[japheth]], [[abraham]], [[sarah]], [[hagar]], [[ishmael]], [[isaac]], [[rebekah]], [[jacob]], [[esau]], [[leah]], [[rachel]], [[joseph]], [[judah]], [[levi]], [[benjamin]], [[dinah]].

Reception: foundational for rabbinic midrash (Bereshit Rabbah), patristic typology (Adam/Christ, Isaac/Christ), Reformation debates over original sin and election, and modern controversies over creation and evolution. Comparative ANE study since the 19th century has reframed Genesis as participant in a wider Mesopotamian-Levantine literary world rather than an isolated revelation.
