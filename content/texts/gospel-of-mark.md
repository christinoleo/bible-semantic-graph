Shortest of the four Gospels (~11,300 words), almost universally held by critical scholarship to be the earliest written (Markan priority), composed ca. 65-75 CE, most often dated just before or just after the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE. Traditional ascription (Papias via Eusebius): [[mark]], interpreter of [[peter]], who recorded "accurately though not in order" the things Peter remembered. Critical scholarship treats this as a 2nd-c. legitimating tradition; the text itself is anonymous.

Greek is terse, paratactic, semitically inflected (frequent καί, vivid present-tense narration, retention of Aramaic phrases — talitha koum 5:41; ephphatha 7:34; abba 14:36 — translated for a non-Aramaic-speaking audience). Pace is breathless: εὐθύς ("immediately") appears ~42x, propelling the narrative from baptism to passion.

Structure pivots on Caesarea Philippi (8:27-30): Galilean ministry (1-8) characterized by exorcisms, healings, and incomprehension; passion prediction triad (8:31; 9:31; 10:33) leading toward Jerusalem (11-16). Roughly the final third of the book covers the last week — the so-called "passion narrative with extended introduction" (Kähler).

"Messianic secret" (Wrede 1901): [[jesus]] silences demons and healed parties from disclosing his identity (1:25, 34, 44; 3:12; 5:43; 8:30; 9:9). Wrede read this as Markan theological construction explaining why the historical Jesus had not been recognized as messiah in his lifetime; later critics treat it as redactional emphasis on the cross as the locus of true recognition (15:39, the centurion's confession).

Textual crux: the longer ending (16:9-20) is absent from the earliest and best manuscripts (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) and is universally regarded as a 2nd-c. addition compensating for the abrupt original ending at 16:8 ("they were afraid"). The "shorter ending" and "Freer logion" are further attempts at closure. Whether 16:8 was the intended ending or an accident of mutilated transmission remains debated.

Christology: low-narrative, high-dramatic. [[jesus]] is Son of God (1:1, though textually contested) and Son of Man, but the title "messiah" is functional and cruciform — true sonship is disclosed only at the crucifixion (15:39).

Major figures: [[jesus]], [[peter]] (oral source per Papias; prominent and frequently negatively portrayed — denial, rebuke), [[john-the-baptist]], [[judas-iscariot]], [[pontius-pilate]]. The Twelve are characteristically obtuse — a literary technique foregrounding the reader's superior insight.

Reception: long the neglected Gospel (Augustine called Mark "an abbreviator of Matthew" — exactly inverted by modern scholarship). Markan priority reshapes 20th-c. NT studies. The form-critical and redaction-critical movements (Bultmann, Marxsen) treat Mark as the laboratory case for understanding Gospel composition.
