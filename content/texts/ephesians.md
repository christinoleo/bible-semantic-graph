Disputed Pauline letter (deutero-Pauline in mainstream critical scholarship), written ca. 80-95 CE, possibly by a disciple of [[paul]] consolidating and extending his theology a generation after his death. Conservative scholarship defends Pauline authorship in the mid-60s. The address "to the saints who are at Ephesus" (1:1) is absent from the earliest manuscripts (P46, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus original hand), suggesting the letter was originally a circular without specific addressees — perhaps the "letter from Laodicea" mentioned in [[colossians]] 4:16.

Arguments against Pauline authorship: (1) vocabulary and style — long sentences (1:3-14 is a single Greek sentence), Wisdom-tradition idiom, ~80 hapax legomena; (2) close literary dependence on [[colossians]] (over a third of Ephesians has near-verbatim parallels), suggesting derivative composition; (3) developed ecclesiology — "church" (ἐκκλησία) refers to the universal church, not local communities; (4) eschatology shifted from imminent parousia to realized salvation ("seated us with him in the heavenly places", 2:6); (5) absence of Paul's characteristic concrete situation — no specific church problems, no greetings to named individuals in a community Paul ministered in for three years per [[acts]] 19. Defenders (Hoehner, Thielman) argue all features are explicable within Pauline range.

Structure: doctrinal exposition (1-3) followed by paraenesis (4-6). Doctrinal half: cosmic blessing in Christ (1), salvation by grace through faith (2:1-10), reconciliation of Jew and Gentile in one new humanity (2:11-22 — the central ecclesiological vision), the mystery of Christ now revealed (3). Paraenetic half: unity of the body and the gifts (4:1-16), old self / new self (4:17-32), walk in love and light (5:1-20), household codes (5:21-6:9, wives/husbands, children/parents, slaves/masters), armor of God (6:10-20).

Theology: cosmic Christology — Christ as head of the cosmos (1:10, ἀνακεφαλαιώσασθαι, "to sum up all things"); ecclesiology as central category — the church is Christ's body, his bride, the new temple, the new humanity reconciling Jew and Gentile; predestination and election (1:4-14); union with Christ (the formula "in Christ" appears densely). The household code (5:21-6:9) — debated as a Christianization of Greco-Roman conventions vs. a subversive reframing of submission as mutual — is the locus classicus for Christian theologies of marriage and (historically) slavery.

Reception: foundational for high ecclesiology — Catholic, Orthodox, and Anglican traditions read Ephesians' "one church, one Lord, one faith, one baptism" (4:4-6) as constituting visible unity; the Reformation tradition reads chs. 1-2 as the locus classicus of monergistic grace ("by grace you have been saved through faith, and this is not your own doing", 2:8-9). The household code shaped Christian patriarchy historically and is at the center of egalitarian-complementarian debates today. The armor passage (6:10-20) supplies durable martial imagery for Christian spirituality.
