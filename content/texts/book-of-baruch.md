Composite deuterocanonical document pseudonymously attributed to [[baruch]] ben Neriah, the scribe of [[jeremiah]] (cf. Jer 36; 45). The book situates itself in Babylon five years after the destruction of Jerusalem (Bar 1:1-2), with Baruch reading the scroll to the exiled community; in fact the text is widely held to be a Hellenistic-period composition (3rd-1st c. BCE), assembled from at least three originally independent units.

Composition and language. The book divides cleanly into (i) a historical prologue and penitential prayer in prose (1:1-3:8), Hebrew/Aramaic original probable, with extensive verbal overlap with the prayer of [[daniel]] 9; (ii) a wisdom poem (3:9-4:4) in Hebrew-style poetic parallelism, on Israel's abandonment of Wisdom (identified with Torah, cf. [[sirach]] 24); (iii) a prophetic-consolation poem (4:5-5:9) figuring Jerusalem as a bereaved mother promised return of her children, parallel in form to Psalms of Solomon 11 and verbally close to Isaiah 60-62. The Vulgate appends as ch. 6 the [[letter-of-jeremiah]], which the LXX preserves as a separate book. Surviving text is Greek (LXX); a Semitic original for at least 1:1-3:8 is generally conceded on the basis of Hebraisms and translation evidence.

Dating. The prayer of 1:15-3:8 is most likely the latest unit, post-dating Daniel 9 (since Bar 1:15-2:19 verbally tracks Dan 9:7-14 and likely depends on it) and thus mid-2nd-c. BCE or later. The wisdom poem may be older; the consolation poem fits a Hellenistic-period Jerusalem-restoration context. Final composite redaction is generally placed in the 2nd or 1st c. BCE.

Themes. Confession of corporate sin as cause of exile, on a clear Deuteronomic schema (Bar 1:15-22; cf. Deut 28-30; Lev 26); divine pedagogical justice; the gift of Wisdom embodied in Torah (3:9-4:4 — "she is the book of the commandments of God"); the city of Jerusalem personified as a grieving mother and promised joy (4:5-5:9). Bar 3:36-38 ("she appeared on earth and lived with humankind") was read by patristic and medieval Christianity as a Christological / incarnational prophecy and was widely cited in Marian devotion and incarnation theology — its proper philological referent is Wisdom, but the Christian afterlife of the verse outweighs its original context.

Reception and canon. In the LXX, Vulgate, Peshitta. Cited by [[origen]], [[athanasius]] (Festal Letter 39 places Baruch with Jeremiah and Lamentations and includes it in his recommended reading), [[cyril-of-jerusalem]]. Hippo, Carthage, Florence, and Trent affirmed canonicity. In Greek-speaking East, often counted with Jeremiah and Lamentations as a single complex; in Latin, Baruch became distinct. Excluded from the Tanakh and from Protestant canons at the Reformation. The Coptic, Ethiopic, Syriac, and Armenian traditions have variously included one or more of Baruch / Letter of Jeremiah / [[2-baruch]] / [[3-baruch]], creating a notoriously tangled "Baruch literature."

Important distinction: this book (sometimes "1 Baruch") is wholly distinct from the pseudepigraphal Baruch apocalypses — [[2-baruch]] (Syriac Apocalypse of Baruch, late 1st - early 2nd c. CE, Jewish post-70 response to the destruction, paralleling 4 Ezra) and [[3-baruch]] (Greek Apocalypse of Baruch, 1st-3rd c. CE, heavenly tour cosmography). None of those are in any major Christian canon (though 2 Baruch survives complete only in Syriac and was included in the Peshitta tradition).
