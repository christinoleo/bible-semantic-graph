Three distinct Ge'ez (Classical Ethiopic) books — *1 Meqabyan*, *2 Meqabyan*, *3 Meqabyan* — canonical in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church but unrelated in content to the Greek [[1-maccabees]] and [[2-maccabees]] beyond the shared title. The misleading naming has caused persistent confusion in Western reference works that conflate "Ethiopian Maccabees" with the Greek Maccabean books. The Greek-Hebrew Maccabean traditions are not part of the Ethiopian canon; *Meqabyan* occupies the canonical slot but with completely different narrative material.

Composition: indigenous Ethiopic composition, almost certainly in Ge'ez originally (no Greek, Syriac, Arabic, or Hebrew Vorlage is known or even strongly hypothesized), of uncertain but possibly medieval date (~13th-15th c.?). Scholarly consensus on date is weak because of limited critical study and the conservative manuscript tradition. The works show familiarity with the canonical Hebrew Bible (in the form known in Ethiopian Christianity, which includes [[1-enoch]] and [[jubilees]] as scripture) and with patristic theology.

*1 Meqabyan* tells of a king named Meqabis of Midian whose sons resist the Babylonian oppressor (with anachronistic-typological reference to Maccabean-style resistance against a Hellenistic king); a king of Midian named "Tseerutsaydan" (a Ge'ez transcription) takes the role analogous to [[antiochus-iv-epiphanes]] in the Greek tradition. Several brothers are martyred under torture; the historical content is unanchored to any extant Near Eastern history.

*2 Meqabyan* is a theological narrative including the story of King Meqabis of Moab, who, after a period of disobedience, repents and writes a confession. The work moves freely between narrative episodes and theological reflection on creation, sin, and repentance, with extensive expansions on the days of creation.

*3 Meqabyan* presents a programmatic theological argument on the resurrection of the dead, drawing on biblical proof-texts and apologetic argument. Its style approaches a homiletic-theological treatise more than a narrative, with extended discussion of [[adam]] and [[eve]]'s creation, of the imago Dei, and of bodily resurrection.

Theological function: in Ethiopian Tewahedo, *Meqabyan* fills the canonical-slot role of providing martyrological-theological reflection without inheriting the specifically Hellenistic-Egyptian and Hasmonean-Judean concerns of the Greek Maccabean tradition. The work supports a robustly resurrection-affirming Ethiopian theological tradition and a sustained engagement with creation theology.

Reception: liturgical and lectionary use within Ethiopian Tewahedo; no significant Western reception or scholarly study until the late 19th and 20th centuries (Dillmann, Cowley). English translations remain partial and provisional; no critical edition meeting Western text-critical standards yet exists. The works are crucial for any account of the Ethiopian biblical canon, which is the broadest of any canonical tradition with roughly 81 books in the "broader" or "older" enumeration.
