Anthology of 150 Hebrew religious lyrics: hymns, laments (individual and communal), thanksgivings, royal psalms, wisdom psalms, songs of ascent, enthronement psalms, liturgies. Canonical hymnbook of Second Temple Judaism and, transmitted through synagogue and church, the most influential single corpus of religious poetry in the West.

Authorship: superscriptions ascribe 73 psalms to [[david]], others to Asaph, the sons of Korah, Heman, Ethan, [[solomon]], [[moses]] (Ps 90), and many are anonymous. Critical scholarship treats the superscriptions as later editorial additions, often liturgically motivated, and dates the corpus across a wide span — from possibly Late Bronze Age fragments (Ps 29 has been read as adapted from a Canaanite hymn to Baal) through Persian-period compositions. The Davidic ascriptions establish David as the paradigmatic psalmist and prophet of cultic poetry but are not biographical claims.

Structure: five "books" (Pss 1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), each closed by a doxology — a deliberate Pentateuchal mirror. Pss 1-2 form a wisdom-and-royal introduction; Pss 146-150 close with a sustained Hallel. Form-criticism (Gunkel) classifies psalms by *Gattung*: hymn, individual lament, communal lament, individual thanksgiving, royal psalm, wisdom psalm, song of Zion, enthronement psalm. Cult-functional approaches (Mowinckel) tie many to specific liturgical settings, especially a hypothesized autumn enthronement festival.

Themes: praise of [[yhwh]] as creator and covenant God, lament and protest, royal-messianic hope (esp. Pss 2, 72, 110), Zion theology (Pss 46, 48, 87), retributive wisdom (Ps 1, 73), penitence (Ps 51 read with the [[bathsheba]] narrative of [[2-samuel]]), and the imprecatory psalms (Ps 137 et al.) with their durable ethical scandal.

Reception: structuring corpus for Jewish and Christian daily prayer; the basis of monastic Liturgy of the Hours; quarried for New Testament Christology (Pss 2, 22, 110 are the most-cited Old Testament texts in the New Testament); Calvin's preface to the Psalms calls them "an anatomy of all the parts of the soul." Reformation through modernity treats the Psalter as the canonical school of prayer.
