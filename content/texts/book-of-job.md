Wisdom book on innocent suffering: a righteous man, Job, is stripped of family, wealth, and health by a wager between [[yhwh]] and the Satan ("the accuser"), then disputes with three friends — Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar — and a younger interlocutor Elihu over the meaning of his affliction, before YHWH answers from the whirlwind.

Form is unusual: prose framing (Job 1-2 and 42:7-17) brackets a long poetic dialogue (Job 3-42:6). The frame's "patient Job" of the folktale clashes sharply with the protesting Job of the poetic core — critical scholarship treats this as evidence of an older folk substrate elaborated by a poet of extraordinary originality. Date of composition is contested, with most placing the poetic core in the post-exilic period (6th-4th c. BCE); the Elihu speeches (Job 32-37) are widely regarded as a later insertion. Hebrew of the poetic dialogue is exceptionally difficult, with heavy use of rare vocabulary.

Structure: prose prologue with the heavenly wager (Job 1-2); three cycles of dialogue between Job and his friends, with the third cycle textually disrupted (Job 3-27); the wisdom poem on the inaccessibility of wisdom (Job 28); Job's final defense (Job 29-31); Elihu's speeches (Job 32-37); YHWH's speeches from the whirlwind (Job 38-41), centered on Behemoth and Leviathan; Job's response of submission (Job 42:1-6); prose epilogue with restoration (Job 42:7-17).

Themes: the inadequacy of retributive theology, the refusal of facile theodicy, divine speech that does not explain suffering but reframes it cosmologically, the legitimacy of protest within faith, comparative ANE wisdom literature (parallels with the *Babylonian Theodicy* and *Ludlul bel nemeqi*). The Satan figure here is a court official, not yet the developed adversary of later apocalyptic and New Testament tradition.

Reception: rabbinic tradition reads Job ambivalently — was he Israelite or Gentile, contemporary of [[abraham]] or of [[moses]]? Patristic and medieval Christian reception (Gregory's *Moralia*) treats Job as type of suffering church. Modern reception is dense — Kierkegaard, Jung, MacLeish, Wiesel, Carl Jung's *Answer to Job*, Gustavo Gutiérrez — with Job consistently invoked as the canonical witness against complacent theodicy.
