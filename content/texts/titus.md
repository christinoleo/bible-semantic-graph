Third Pastoral Epistle, addressed to [[titus-companion]], a Gentile Christian companion of Paul (cf. [[galatians]] 2:1-3, where Titus is the test-case Gentile not circumcised at Jerusalem; [[2-corinthians]] 2:13; 7:6-16; 8:6, 16-24, Titus as emissary to Corinth). Here he is stationed in Crete to "set in order what was left undone and appoint elders in every town" (1:5).

Authenticity debate: classified with [[1-timothy]] and [[2-timothy]] as pseudepigraphic by mainstream critical scholarship on the same vocabulary/style/ecclesiology grounds (see [[1-timothy]]). Authentic-Pauline defenders again require a post-[[acts]] mission. Shortest and most schematic of the three Pastorals; the high density of formal lists (qualifications for elders, vices to avoid, virtues to cultivate, household-code duties) makes it the most "manual"-like of the corpus.

Structure: opening with extended salutation (1:1-4); qualifications for elders/overseers and warning against Cretan false teachers (1:5-16); instructions to age- and gender-defined groups within the household (older men, older women, younger women, younger men, slaves — 2:1-10); the central theological-paraenetic core on the "grace of God that has appeared" (2:11-3:8); final warnings and personal directions (3:9-15).

The "Cretan slander" (1:12): "One of themselves, a prophet of their own, said, 'Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons.'" The citation is from Epimenides (or a stoic-tradition liar-paradox attributed to him). The verse is exegetical embarrassment — Christian commentary has had to negotiate Paul's apparent endorsement of an ethnic stereotype against the Cretan church he is establishing.

False teachers: described as "those of the circumcision" teaching "Jewish myths" (1:10, 14) — likely a Jewish-Christian ascetic-speculative movement parallel to that opposed in [[1-timothy]]. Whether literal circumcision is the issue or a broader Judaizing tendency is debated.

Theological passages: 2:11-14 — "the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation to all people, training us to renounce ungodliness…awaiting the blessed hope and the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" — important Christological text (the grammar of "our great God and Savior" applied to Christ, the Granville Sharp construction, has been a major Trinitarian proof-text). 3:4-7 — "but when the kindness and loving-kindness (φιλανθρωπία) of God our Savior appeared…he saved us through the washing of regeneration and renewal by the Holy Spirit" — baptismal and Spirit theology, often read as embedded creedal/liturgical material.

Major figures: [[paul]] (author or claimed author), [[titus-companion]] (recipient), Artemas, Tychicus, Zenas the lawyer, Apollos (mentioned as a traveler, 3:13).

Reception: foundational for Reformed and confessional Protestant theologies of church order (presbyterian polity draws on Tit 1:5-9 alongside [[1-timothy]] 3); the "great God and Savior" Christology (2:13) is a standard text in Trinitarian apologetics; the Cretan slander (1:12) is much discussed in modern ethics and postcolonial readings of the NT.
