First of the three Pastoral Epistles (with [[2-timothy]] and [[titus]]). Addressed to [[timothy]], Paul's longtime associate, stationed in Ephesus (1:3). Critical scholarship since Schleiermacher (1807) and Baur (1835) has overwhelmingly judged the Pastorals pseudepigraphic, written ca. 90-130 CE by a later Pauline tradition consolidating church order and combating early-2nd-c. opponents. A minority defend authenticity, requiring an otherwise unattested period after the [[acts]] 28 house arrest in which Paul was released, traveled in the East, and was rearrested.

Arguments against authenticity: (1) vocabulary — ~36% of the words in the Pastorals do not appear in the undisputed Paulines; the three letters share a distinctive vocabulary cluster (εὐσέβεια "godliness", σωφρων- "self-control" family, "the faith" as fixed deposit) absent from the undisputed corpus; (2) literary style — short, sententious, list-driven, lacking Paul's dialectical argumentation; (3) developed ecclesiology — fixed offices (bishop, elder, deacon — 3:1-13), institutionalized widows (5:3-16), regularized procedures for accusations against elders (5:19-22); (4) theology of "the faith" (ἡ πίστις) as deposited body of doctrine to be guarded (6:20, "guard the deposit"), not as Paul's relational fiducia; (5) historical situation not fitting [[acts]]; (6) earlier rhetorical questions ("where now is the boasting?") and Pauline tropes are absent.

Opponents: described as teaching "myths and endless genealogies" (1:4), forbidding marriage and certain foods (4:3), having a "knowledge falsely so-called" (ψευδώνυμος γνῶσις, 6:20 — a phrase later seized on by Irenaeus as a label for Gnosticism), promoting "speculations" (1:4) rather than "the sound teaching" (1:10; 6:3). Identification varies: incipient Gnosticism, Jewish-Christian asceticism, syncretistic Hellenistic-Jewish wisdom-mysticism. Most current scholarship resists "proto-Gnostic" labels and reads the opponents as a Jewish-Christian ascetic-speculative tendency.

Structure: charge to Timothy and warning against false teachers (1); instructions on prayer and gender roles in worship (2); qualifications for bishops and deacons (3); prophecy about apostasy and pastoral instructions (4); treatment of widows, elders, slaves (5-6:2); final warnings against the love of money and false teaching (6:3-21).

Controversial passages: 1 Tim 2:8-15 ("I do not permit a woman to teach or to have authority over a man…women will be saved through childbearing") — the most-debated NT text on women's roles, central to complementarian-egalitarian debates. The household codes embedded in chs. 2-6 (women, slaves, the wealthy) systematize and intensify the conventions also visible in [[ephesians]] and [[colossians]]. 1 Tim 6:10 ("the love of money is the root of all kinds of evil") is among the most-quoted NT verses.

Theological emphases: God is "the blessed and only Sovereign, the King of kings and Lord of lords" (6:15) — high theology in imperial idiom; salvation as God's will for "all people" (2:4); embedded creed-like and hymn-like material (1:15; 2:5-6; 3:16; 6:15-16) suggesting incorporation of earlier liturgical tradition.

Major figures: [[paul]] (author or claimed author), [[timothy]] (recipient), Hymenaeus and Alexander (named opponents, 1:20).

Reception: the Pastorals consolidated the trajectory toward monarchical episcopacy (Ignatius of Antioch, early 2nd c., builds on a similar church order). 2:11-15 anchors the bulk of historical Christian arguments against women's ordination; egalitarian readings argue the passage addresses a specific Ephesian crisis (women influenced by the false teachers) rather than universal norm. The "deposit" language (παραθήκη, 6:20) is foundational for traditions of doctrinal continuity (Catholic, Orthodox, Confessional Protestant).
