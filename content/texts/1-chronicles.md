In the Tanakh, Chronicles closes the Ketuvim/Writings (and thus the entire Hebrew Bible); in the Christian Old Testament, 1-2 Chronicles follow 1-2 Kings as historical books. With [[2-chronicles]] originally a single scroll.

Author and date: the unnamed "Chronicler" is conventionally dated to the late Persian or early Hellenistic period (4th c. BCE). Older scholarship treated Chronicles, [[book-of-ezra]], and [[book-of-nehemiah]] as a single Chronistic History; more recent scholarship (Japhet, Williamson) treats Chronicles as a distinct work with its own theological signature.

A theologically motivated re-narration of Israel's story from Adam to the Persian period, parallels_with [[1-samuel]] and [[2-samuel]] for the Davidic material but radically reshaped: David is idealized (the Bathsheba affair and Absalom revolt entirely absent), the temple cultus elevated, the Levitical singers and gatekeepers organized, the northern kingdom largely ignored.

Structure: genealogies from Adam through the post-exilic community (1 Chr 1-9), establishing Davidic and Levitical lineage with theological scaffolding; death of [[saul]] (1 Chr 10) as brief negative foil; reign of [[david]] (1 Chr 11-29) emphasizing his preparations for temple construction — ark transfer, Davidic covenant (1 Chr 17, paralleling 2 Sam 7), organization of priests and Levites, transfer of plans and resources to [[solomon]].

Themes: David as cultic founder rather than warrior-king, the centrality of the Jerusalem temple, the Levitical singers, *immediate retribution* as theological principle (each king's fortune tracks his fidelity within his own lifetime, contrasting with Kings' deferred judgment), continuity of post-exilic community with pre-exilic Israel through genealogy and cult.

Reception: long treated as a derivative epitome of Samuel-Kings and undervalued, Chronicles has been recovered in recent decades as a sophisticated post-exilic theological reinterpretation, important for understanding Second Temple Judaism and the formation of biblical historiography.
