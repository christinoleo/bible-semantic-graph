Collection of eighteen Greek psalms pseudepigraphically attributed to [[solomon]], composed in Jerusalem in the mid- to late 1st c. BCE in the aftermath of [[pompey]]'s 63 BCE capture of the city and his desecration of the Temple. The Greek is almost certainly a translation from a lost Hebrew Vorlage. Excluded from every canon; preserved in a small number of Greek and Syriac manuscripts, typically appended to Septuagint manuscripts.

Historical setting fixes the date precisely. Several psalms (especially Pss. Sol. 2, 8, and 17) describe the entry into Jerusalem of "the lawless one" / "the dragon" from a distant land, his profaning of the altar, his initial impunity, and his subsequent humiliating death "on the mountains of Egypt." This matches [[pompey]]'s career — Jerusalem entered 63 BCE, killed at Pelusium 48 BCE — almost too neatly to be coincidence. Pss. Sol. 2:26-27, in particular, is read as a vaticinium ex eventu of Pompey's assassination.

The work's theological-political stance is best characterized as proto-Pharisaic: opposition to Hasmonean dynastic claims (the Hasmoneans are denounced as "sinners" who profaned the throne of David, 17:5-6), opposition to Sadducean priestly accommodation with Rome, affirmation of divine retributive justice in this age and in the resurrection of the righteous (3:11-12; 14:9-10), and ardent expectation of a coming Davidic king.

Pss. Sol. 17 is the most fully-developed pre-Christian Davidic-messianic text outside Qumran. The "Son of David" (17:21), raised up by God at his appointed time, will purge Jerusalem of Gentiles, gather a holy people, smash arrogance "with the rod of his mouth" (17:24, drawing on [[book-of-isaiah]] 11:4), and rule with wisdom and righteousness. He is explicitly a human king under God, not a divine or angelic figure; his weapons are word and judgment, not sword and chariot. The chapter is the closest pre-Christian parallel to the messianic categories used in the Gospel infancy narratives (cf. [[gospel-of-luke]] 1:32-33, 67-79).

Theological emphases beyond messianism: divine pedagogy through suffering (the righteous one is chastened lovingly while the sinner is destroyed), the distinction between "the righteous" and "the sinners" within Israel (a sectarian rhetoric typical of late-Second-Temple groups), individual eschatology with reward and judgment, and a sapiential ethic of trust in God's faithfulness despite Gentile triumph.

Reception: cited in the Stichometry of Nicephorus and in some early canonical lists as a disputed (antilegomena) book, often grouped with the Wisdom of [[solomon]] under the broader "Wisdom of Solomon" category. Not received in mainstream Christian liturgical use. Modern scholarly attention since J. Rendel Harris's 1909 Syriac edition has made it a key text for understanding Second-Temple messianism, the Pharisaic milieu of nascent Christianity, and the literary-theological response to Roman annexation.
