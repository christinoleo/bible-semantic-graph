Liturgical insertion of 68 verses between MT Daniel 3:23 and 3:24, occupying the narrative gap during which Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego stand in the furnace. The unit comprises three distinct components: (i) a prose narrative frame describing the three young men walking in the furnace (vv. 1-2, 23-27); (ii) the penitential prayer of Azariah (= Abednego; vv. 3-22); and (iii) the hymn of all three from within the fire (vv. 28-68), known in liturgical tradition as the Benedicite or Song of the Three, with refrains "bless the Lord, sing praise to him and highly exalt him forever."

The insertion creates the universally-printed Vulgate numbering Daniel 3:24-90, with native Daniel 3:24 (MT) becoming Daniel 3:91 in the Vulgate. Theodotion's Greek (the standard form of Greek Daniel) preserves the addition; Old Greek does too, with minor variations.

Textual situation and original language. The Greek shows Semitic syntactic features that have led most scholars to posit a Hebrew or Aramaic Vorlage at least for the prayer and possibly the hymn; no Semitic manuscript witness survives, and Qumran's Daniel materials do not preserve the addition. The hymn's stylized litany-with-refrain structure adapts Hebrew psalmodic genres (especially the catalogue-of-praise tradition of Ps 148) and converts to Greek with little difficulty. Date: late Persian to mid-Hellenistic, generally 3rd-2nd c. BCE.

Prayer of Azariah (vv. 3-22). A Deuteronomic penitential prayer in the genre of Dan 9, Bar 1:15-3:8, Ezra 9, and Neh 9: confession of corporate sin, acknowledgment that the exile is just chastisement, appeal to God's covenant fidelity to the patriarchs, plea for deliverance. Notable verses: 14-15 — "we have no ruler, no prophet, no leader, no burnt offering, no sacrifice, no oblation, no incense, no place to make an offering before you" — articulating the theology of substitute sacrifice during the Hellenistic period when access to the Temple was disrupted; 16-17 — "may we be accepted with contrite heart and humble spirit" — a locus classicus for the sufficiency of the contrite heart in lieu of cultic sacrifice.

Song of the Three (vv. 28-68). A liturgical hymn in two unequal parts: (a) a doxological opening blessing God in heaven, in the temple, on the throne, in the firmament (vv. 28-34); (b) the great cosmic Benedicite (vv. 35-68), in which every creature in the order of Gen 1 — heavens, angels, waters above and below, sun and moon, stars, rain and dew, winds, fire and heat, frost and snow, lightning and clouds, earth, mountains, seas and rivers, sea-creatures, birds, beasts, mortals, Israel, priests, servants of the Lord, spirits and souls of the righteous, holy and humble of heart, the three young men themselves — is summoned to bless the Lord with the refrain. Liturgically the most influential canticle of the OT-and-Apocrypha; the Benedicite became a standard canticle of the Christian morning office (Latin: ad Laudes), recited or sung daily in Benedictine, Roman, Anglican (BCP Morning Prayer), and Eastern Orthodox liturgies.

Theology. The prayer integrates penitential theology of corporate guilt with the theology of cultic interruption (cf. [[2-maccabees]]'s temple-desecration setting); the hymn integrates cosmic doxology with martyrological exemplarism — the three are protected in fire while singing the praise of creation, a vivid figura of the Church-in-trial. Patristic typology read the fourth figure in the furnace (Dan 3:25 / Vg 3:92) as the pre-incarnate Christ, with the Song as the Church's response.

Canonical history. Included in the LXX (both Old Greek and Theodotion), Vulgate, Peshitta. Cited and quoted extensively in patristic literature; the Benedicite was already a liturgical fixture in the 4th c. (cf. Apostolic Constitutions 7.47). Hippo, Carthage, Florence, Trent affirmed canonicity. Excluded from the Tanakh; rabbinic tradition does not preserve it. Reformers excluded; the King James Apocrypha prints it as "The Song of the Three Holy Children," and the Anglican BCP retained the Benedicite as a canticle even after the Articles of Religion had relegated the Apocrypha to non-doctrinal status — a striking case of liturgical retention surviving formal canonical demotion.
