Undisputed Pauline letter, the most polemical in the corpus. Dating depends on which Galatia: the "North Galatian" theory (ethnic Galatia, addressed late, ca. 53-55 CE) versus the "South Galatian" theory (Roman province including the Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra, Derbe evangelized in [[acts]] 13-14, allowing an earlier date, ca. 48-49 CE — possibly before the Jerusalem council). The earlier dating dovetails with reading Gal 2:1-10 as a different meeting from [[acts]] 15; the standard alternative equates them.

Crisis: rival missionaries — "agitators" (1:7; 5:12) — have arrived in the Galatian churches preaching that Gentile converts must be circumcised and observe Torah ("Judaizers" in older scholarship, the "circumcision party"). [[paul]] writes furiously to recall the Galatians from this "other gospel" (1:6-9), to which he assigns anathema.

Structure: salutation and curse formula (1:1-10) replacing the usual thanksgiving (Galatians is the only undisputed Pauline letter omitting a thanksgiving — Paul has nothing to thank God for in this church); autobiographical defense of Paul's apostolic independence (1:11-2:14, including the Damascus call, the post-conversion years in Arabia, the Jerusalem visit, the [[peter]] confrontation at Antioch); theological argument from scripture and experience (3-4, [[abraham]], the law as paidagogos, [[genesis|Hagar and Sarah]] allegory); ethical implications — life in the Spirit, freedom from the law, fruit of the Spirit (5-6).

The Antioch incident (2:11-14): Paul publicly rebukes [[peter]] for withdrawing from table fellowship with Gentile believers under pressure from "men from James". The episode is foundational for the historical question of relations between the Pauline mission and the Jerusalem leadership ([[peter]], [[james-brother-of-jesus]], [[barnabas]]) — and harder to harmonize with [[acts]]' irenic portrayal.

Key arguments: justification by faith, not works of the law (2:16); [[abraham]] justified before circumcision (3:6-9, citing Gen 15:6), making Gentiles by faith his true heirs; the law as temporary paidagogos until Christ (3:23-25); freedom and the Spirit against flesh (5); the cross as the only ground of boasting (6:14).

New Perspective debate: as in [[romans]], "works of the law" (ἔργα νόμου) has been re-read by Dunn, Wright, Sanders et al. as referring specifically to Jewish boundary-markers (circumcision, food, calendar) — making the controversy fundamentally about whether Gentiles must become Jews to be Christ-people, rather than the medieval/Reformation issue of meritorious works versus grace. Old-Perspective rebuttals stress that Paul's argument generalizes beyond ethnic markers.

Reception: foundational for Reformation soteriology — Luther's 1535 commentary on Galatians is his most beloved theological work ("the Katie von Bora of my books"). The letter underwrites Protestant doctrines of justification and Christian liberty; the Hagar/Sarah allegory (4:21-31) shapes patristic typology; "neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female" (3:28) becomes a touchstone for modern egalitarian readings of Pauline theology.
