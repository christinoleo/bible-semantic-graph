"Rewritten Bible" retelling of [[genesis]] 1 through [[exodus]] 24 framed as a series of revelations given to [[moses]] on Mount Sinai by an "angel of the presence." History from creation to the giving of the Law is partitioned into 49 "jubilees" of 49 years each (a Sabbath of Sabbaths), with the conquest of Canaan landing on the jubilee of jubilees. Canonical in Ethiopian Tewahedo (under the title *Mäṣḥäfä Kufale*, "Book of Division"); excluded from all other canons; preserved in full only in Ge'ez, with substantial Hebrew fragments at Qumran (at least fifteen manuscripts, more than any other "extra-canonical" text), partial Latin, and Syriac excerpts.

Composition: mid-2nd c. BCE in Hebrew, almost certainly in Hasmonean Judea, with strong sectarian or proto-sectarian overtones. The author writes in conscious tension with the Jerusalem priestly establishment, advocating a solar 364-day calendar (shared with [[1-enoch]] and the Qumran sect), strict purity halakhah on issues like Sabbath observance and intermarriage, and a starkly dualistic angelology (the angel Mastema serves as adversary, taking on roles assigned to God in canonical Genesis — e.g., the testing of Abraham at Mount Moriah is reassigned to Mastema's instigation).

The "rewriting" technique: each canonical pericope is retold, sometimes verbatim, with theological and halakhic supplements, gap-fills, and exegetical clarifications. Cain's wife is named (Awan, his sister); Adam and Eve's chronology is exactly specified; the [[binding-of-isaac-as-test]] is dated to 15 Nisan (proto-Passover); Noah composes a book of medicine; Levi receives priestly election from Abraham long before the Sinaitic priesthood. The genealogies of Genesis are tightened and named where canonical Genesis is silent.

Halakhic content: legal material is heavily expanded. Capital punishment for intermarriage with Gentiles is attached to the [[dinah]] episode; Sabbath laws are spelled out with case-by-case prohibitions; circumcision is the boundary of the covenant from Abraham forward without exception; the dietary laws receive priestly grounding. The author treats halakhah as ancestral practice predating Sinai — the patriarchs already kept the Torah — supporting an argument that the Mosaic Law is the codification of a pre-existing cosmic order rather than a temporal innovation.

Calendar polemic: the solar 364-day calendar (12 months of 30 days + 4 intercalary days at the equinox-solstice transitions) is presented as divinely revealed. Its appeal is structural: 364 = 7 × 52, so every date falls on the same weekday every year, and no festival can fall on a Sabbath (avoiding halakhic conflict). The lunar calendar of the Jerusalem establishment is denounced (Jub 6:36-38). This calendrical dispute is one of the proximate causes of the sectarian split visible at Qumran.

Reception: heavily used at Qumran (cited by name in CD 16:3-4 and in 4Q228); influential on early Christian chronography (the *Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum* of pseudo-Philo draws on similar traditions); fully canonical in Ethiopia, where it is read alongside [[genesis]] as exegetical complement; rejected by mainstream rabbinic Judaism and by patristic Christianity, though the Watcher-Mastema material in chs. 5 and 10 shaped Christian demonology. Modern critical recovery began with Dillmann's 19th-c. Ethiopic editions; the Qumran finds confirmed a Hebrew Vorlage and a date no later than ~150 BCE.
