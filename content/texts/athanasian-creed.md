The Latin doctrinal creed *Quicumque Vult* — "whosoever will be saved, before all things it is necessary that he hold the catholic faith" — a 5th- or early-6th-century southern Gaulish composition under Augustinian and Vincentian influence, traditionally (but erroneously) attributed to [[athanasius]]. Forty-two articulated clauses unfold in two halves: a Trinitarian section ("we worship one God in Trinity and the Trinity in Unity; neither confounding the Persons nor dividing the Substance"; each of Father, Son, and Spirit is uncreated, infinite, eternal, almighty, God, Lord — "yet there are not three Almighties but one Almighty"), and a Christological section ("we believe and confess that our Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, is God and man; God of the substance of the Father, begotten before the worlds, and man of the substance of his mother, born in the world; perfect God and perfect man; equal to the Father as touching his Godhead, inferior to the Father as touching his manhood; who although he be God and man, yet he is not two, but one Christ").

Two features make Quicumque distinctive among catholic creeds: it is the only one structured as a continuous theological exposition rather than a baptismal confession, and it explicitly threatens damnation on those who do not hold the faith "whole and undefiled" (the "damnatory clauses," opening and closing the creed). Used liturgically in the medieval Latin West at Prime on Sundays, retained at Prime in the Book of Common Prayer (1549; required confession in the 1662 BCP), the creed is received doctrinally — but not liturgically — by the Eastern Orthodox (who reject the [[filioque|*Filioque*]] clause it contains and the damnatory clauses' severity) and remains in the confessional standards of the Lutheran (Book of Concord) and Reformed (Belgic Confession) traditions.
