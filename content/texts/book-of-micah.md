Seven chapters. [[micah]] of Moresheth (a Judahite village in the Shephelah) active under Jotham, Ahaz, and [[hezekiah]] (Mic 1:1), making him a Judahite contemporary of [[isaiah]] (Jerusalem), [[hosea]] (north), and [[amos]] (Judahite-in-the-north). Mic 3:12's prediction of Jerusalem's ruin is cited a century later in [[book-of-jeremiah]] 26:17-19 to defend Jeremiah from a death sentence — providing an unusual external attestation of Micah's prophetic activity.

Critical scholarship typically treats chs. 1-3 as substantially Mican, with chs. 4-5 (Zion's exaltation, the Bethlehem oracle, the Assyrian threat) and chs. 6-7 (covenant lawsuit, lament, restoration) involving substantial later redaction extending into the post-exilic period. Mic 4:1-3 is virtually identical to [[book-of-isaiah]] 2:2-4 (swords to plowshares); the direction of dependence is debated, with most critical scholars favoring a shared earlier source.

Structure alternates judgment and hope: judgment on Samaria and Judah (1-3), restoration of Zion and the messianic ruler (4-5), covenant rîb / lawsuit (6:1-7:7), confidence in YHWH's forgiveness (7:8-20).

Themes: indictment of the ruling, priestly, and prophetic elites for economic predation (2:1-2, "they covet fields and seize them"; 3:1-3, leaders who "tear the skin from my people"); the Bethlehem oracle (5:2, "you, O Bethlehem Ephrathah… from you shall come forth for me one who is to be ruler in Israel"); the famous threefold summary of religion (6:8, "what does YHWH require of you but to do justice, to love ḥesed, and to walk humbly with your God") — the OT's most cited single verse on prophetic ethics; YHWH's incomparability in pardon (7:18, mî ʾēl kāmôḵā, "who is a god like you?", punning on Micah's own name).

Reception: Mic 5:2 is cited in [[gospel-of-matthew]] 2:6 to locate the [[jesus|Messiah's]] birthplace at [[bethlehem]] — the basis of the Magi narrative's geography. Mic 6:8 became a foundational ethical text in Jewish (cited in Talmud b. Makkot 24a as the great summation of the Torah after David and Isaiah) and modern progressive Christian usage. The juxtaposition of judgment and hope made Micah influential for post-exilic and Second Temple eschatology.
