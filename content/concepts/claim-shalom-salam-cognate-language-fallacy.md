The Islamic argument trades on the shared root **S-L-M** between Hebrew *šālōm* and Arabic *salām*, treating linguistic kinship as evidence of religious continuity. The move is fallacious on three independent grounds.

**(i) Cognates descend from a common ancestor, not from each other.** Hebrew and Arabic both inherit S-L-M from Proto-Semitic — alongside Aramaic, Ge'ez, Akkadian, Phoenician, and the rest of the Semitic family. The shared root proves only that *both languages are Semitic*. By the same logic, English "father" and German *Vater* would prove that English speakers practice the religion of Wotan.

**(ii) Chronology cuts the other way.** *Šālōm* is attested as a greeting in the Hebrew Bible — Ps 122:6-8, Ps 125:5, Ps 128:6, Judg 6:23, 1 Sam 25:6 — and as a deeply embedded element of Second Temple Jewish piety roughly **1500 years before** Muhammad. [[jesus]] greeting with εἰρήνη ὑμῖν / *šālōm* (Luke 24:36, John 20:19) is recognizable Jewish practice. Arabia in the 7th century absorbed the greeting through Jewish and Christian communities present in the Hijaz; that is the documented direction of historical transmission. The Islamic claim reverses chronology.

**(iii) The semantic fields diverge sharply.** *Šālōm* in Hebrew foregrounds **wholeness, completeness, well-being, intactness** — the cluster Jewish thought builds around shalom-as-restoration (Isa 9:6, Jer 29:7). *Salām* in Arabic shares the root but in the Quranic and post-Quranic religious deployment is paired with *islām* — both built on S-L-M, the second meaning **submission**. When a Muslim says *as-salāmu ʿalaykum*, the greeting is theologically lodged inside the broader vocabulary of submission-to-Allah — *as-Salām* is one of the [[allah|99 names of Allah]] (Q 59:23). When [[jesus]] says *šālōm ʿalēkhem*, no comparable theological loading toward Islamic submission is in view — and could not be, since Islam did not exist.

The Muslim apologist needs a stronger thesis than "the words share a root." They need to show that the *form, semantic content, and theological context* of Jesus's greeting were Islamic in some recognizable sense — which on the linguistic and historical evidence runs the other direction. The greeting is *Jewish*, inherited by Christianity from its parent tradition, and *inherited by Islam* from the same parent tradition. Continuity at the level of cognate sound is not continuity at the level of religious practice.
