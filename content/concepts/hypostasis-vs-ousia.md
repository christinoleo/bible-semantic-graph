The technical distinction worked out by the Cappadocian Fathers — [[basil-the-great|Basil]], [[gregory-of-nyssa|Gregory of Nyssa]], [[gregory-of-nazianzus|Gregory of Nazianzus]] — that resolves the apparent paradox of the [[trinity]]: God is **one *ousia*** (essence, the *what*) and **three *hypostaseis*** (persons, the *who*). *Ousia* names the common divine nature shared identically by Father, Son, and Spirit (the ground of [[nicaea-i|Nicene]] *homoousios*); *hypostasis* names each particular subsistence of that nature, distinguished only by relations of origin (paternity, filiation, spiration) — without dividing the essence or multiplying gods. Latin theology subsequently rendered *hypostasis* as *persona* and *ousia* as *substantia*, generating Tertullian's *una substantia, tres personae*; the formula is the conceptual scaffold both for the Trinitarian dogma of [[constantinople-i|Constantinople I]] (381) and for the [[chalcedonian-christology|Chalcedonian]] insistence that the incarnate Son is one *hypostasis* in two *physeis*. Failing the distinction collapses Trinitarianism into either modalism (one *hypostasis* with three masks) or tritheism (three *ousiai*).
