The dogmatic term — Greek *homoousios*, Latin *consubstantialis*, "of the same substance" — inserted into the [[nicaea-i|Nicene]] Creed (325) to assert that the Son is of the *identical* divine essence (*ousia*) as the Father, against [[arianism|Arius]]'s claim that the Son is a creature *ex ouk ontōn* ("out of nothing"). The term's force is precisely what made it controversial: it has no scriptural pedigree, comes from a philosophical vocabulary, and had earlier been used (and condemned) in modalist senses; the post-Nicene controversies are largely about whether one can affirm Trinitarianism more cheaply with *homoiousios* ("of *like* substance") or the indeterminate *homoios* ("like"). The Cappadocian settlement at [[constantinople-i]] (381) finally fixes *homoousios* by pairing it with the [[hypostasis-vs-ousia|hypostasis/ousia]] distinction: one *ousia* (so *homoousios*) in three *hypostaseis* (so not modalism). The term is presupposed by every subsequent Catholic, Orthodox, and Reformation-era Trinitarian confession.
