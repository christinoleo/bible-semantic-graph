The 5th-century theological dispute between [[augustine-of-hippo]] and [[pelagius]] over the relationship between divine grace and human free will in salvation. The controversy crystallized Western Christian doctrine on `original-sin`, prevenient grace, and the limits of unaided human moral capacity.

[[pelagius]] (and his disciple Caelestius) held that Adam's sin damaged only Adam — humans are born morally neutral and capable, by free will, of avoiding sin and achieving salvation. Infant baptism was therefore not strictly necessary for forgiveness, since infants have no sin to forgive. Grace, in this scheme, is the gift of free will itself + the moral example of Christ + the illuminative help of the law.

[[augustine-of-hippo]] held the opposite: Adam's fall transmitted both guilt and a vitiated will (concupiscence) to all descendants. Without prevenient grace, fallen humans cannot will the good — `liberum arbitrium` post-lapsum is freedom-toward-sin only. Salvation is initiated, sustained, and completed by grace acting on a will that grace itself enables.

The dispute was settled juridically against [[pelagius]] at multiple regional synods (Carthage 411, 416, 418; Ephesus 431 reaffirming). But Augustine's stronger positions — especially on predestination and the unbaptized — were never received without controversy, and the **Semi-Pelagian** debates (John Cassian, Faustus of Riez) and the Council of Orange (529) settled a more moderate Augustinianism.

The controversy is generally read as the foundational chapter of Western soteriology. Eastern Orthodox theology, having developed under different pressures and never having faced Pelagianism in the same form, maintained a synergistic account that Augustinian Westerners often misread as Pelagian.
