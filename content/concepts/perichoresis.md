The doctrine — Greek *perichōrēsis*, Latin *circumincessio* / *circuminsessio*, "mutual indwelling" or "coinherence" — that the three divine persons of the [[trinity]] mutually contain and interpenetrate one another without confusion, such that to encounter one is to encounter all three. The classical formulation is John of Damascus's (*De Fide Orthodoxa* I.8: "they have their being in each other"), grounded scripturally in [[gospel-of-john|John]] 14:10-11 and 17:21 — "I am in the Father, and the Father in me" — and developed conceptually to answer the question of how three [[hypostasis-vs-ousia|hypostaseis]] can share one *ousia* without dissolving into modalism or fragmenting into tritheism. The same term is later applied [[chalcedonian-christology|Christologically]] to the relation of the two natures in the incarnate Son, which has made it a load-bearing concept for both Trinitarian and Christological doctrine; modern social-Trinitarian theologies (Moltmann, Boff, Volf) have pressed *perichoresis* outward as a model for community, personhood, and creation — a move classical theologians caution risks projecting created mutuality back into the simple divine essence.
