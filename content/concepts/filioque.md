The Latin clause *qui ex Patre Filioque procedit* — "who proceeds from the Father *and the Son*" — inserted into the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed in the Latin West (first at Toledo III in 589, generalized over the early medieval centuries, dogmatized for Rome at the Fourth [[lateran-iv|Lateran Council]] in 1215) and rejected by the Byzantine East as both unilateral creedal alteration and theological error. The dispute is the proximate doctrinal cause of the East-West schism of 1054 and the central failure-point of every subsequent reunion attempt (Lyon II 1274, Florence 1439). The Latin defense, rooted in Augustine's *De Trinitate*, reads the eternal procession of the Spirit as *from the Father through the Son* and argues that the *Filioque* makes that "through" explicit; the Greek refusal, rooted in Photius's *Mystagogia*, insists that the Father alone is the unbegotten source (*monos archē*) within the Godhead and that introducing a second principle compromises both the [[trinity|Trinitarian]] grammar of the monarchy of the Father and the [[hypostasis-vs-ousia|hypostatic]] distinctions. Modern ecumenical statements (Klingenthal 1979, North American Orthodox-Catholic Consultation 2003) have substantially narrowed the disagreement without resolving the canonical question.
