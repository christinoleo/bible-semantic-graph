The Islamic confession of faith — Arabic *kalimat al-shahāda*, "the word of testimony" — composed of two clauses: "*lā ilāha illā Allāh, Muḥammadun rasūl Allāh* — there is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the messenger of Allah." Sincere recitation of the Shahada is the formal act by which one enters Islam, the first of the *arkān al-Islām* (Five Pillars), and the structural confession of [[tawhid|Tawḥīd]] in negative-then-positive form: first the *nafy* (negation) of all rival deities, then the *ithbāt* (affirmation) of [[allah|Allah]] alone. The first clause has a structural parallel in the Jewish [[shema-deut-6-4|Shema]] ("YHWH our God, YHWH is one") and a polemical relation to the [[trinity|Trinitarian]] Christian creed: where Christianity makes its monotheism confessable only by also confessing the Son and the Spirit, the Shahada admits no internal differentiation in God whatsoever. The second clause — affirming Muhammad's prophetic role — distinguishes Islamic from generic Abrahamic monotheism and is the basis of the Qur'an's claim to be the final, uncorrupted revelation (cf. [[tahrif]] applied to prior scriptures).
