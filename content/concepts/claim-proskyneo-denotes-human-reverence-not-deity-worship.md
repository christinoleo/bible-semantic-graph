The Christian argument turns on treating every Gospel use of προσκυνέω directed at [[jesus]] as theologically loaded worship-of-God. But the same verb covers a wide range in Koine Greek — from the cultural prostration of a slave before a king (**Matt 18:26**, the unforgiving-servant parable, where the servant *προσεκύνει* the king and no one calls it idolatry) to the *mocking* prostration of Roman soldiers (Mark 15:19, *προσεκύνουν αὐτῷ* while striking him). The magi *προσεκύνησαν* an infant (Matt 2:11) — royal homage to a *king of the Jews*, not deity-worship of a not-yet-speaking baby.

Read across these uses, Gospel προσκυνέω directed at Jesus is intelligible as royal-messianic homage — exactly what the Quran would expect a Jewish prophet-messiah to receive. Jesus does not deflect because nothing illegitimate is being offered. The deflection cases (Acts 10:25-26, Rev 19:10) refuse a different and stronger thing: proskynesis offered to a mere human or angel *under the explicit category of divine worship*. None of the Gospel scenes contain that explicit category-marking.

**John 20:28** is the hardest passage and admits two Islamic readings: (i) Thomas's exclamation is an emotional outburst at recognizing the risen Jesus, not a creedal confession of Trinitarian deity — Jesus's reply ("because you have seen me, you have believed") addresses the *believing*, not the metaphysical content of the exclamation; (ii) under [[tahrif|Tahrīf]], the formula ὁ κύριός μου καὶ ὁ θεός μου represents post-Pauline Christological inflation imported into a Johannine corpus that elsewhere (John 17:3, 14:28) preserves the original subordinationist Christology. The exegesis is contested; the polysemy of προσκυνέω is not.

The argument concedes nothing about whether Jesus was a faithful Muslim prophet — only that the Gospel scenes do not, on their own, establish divine-worship-acceptance.
