The doctrine — developed by the Cappadocians and central to Eastern Orthodox [[trinity|Trinitarian]] theology — that within the Godhead the Father alone is the unbegotten source (*archē anarchos*, *aitia*), from whom the Son is eternally begotten and the Spirit eternally proceeds. The monarchy preserves both monotheism (one ungenerated origin) and personal distinction (the Father's relational priority is not temporal or essential but constitutive — the Son and Spirit receive the one divine *ousia* from him), and it is the structural reason Orthodox theology rejects the Latin [[filioque|Filioque]]: introducing a second principle (the Son) into the procession of the Spirit dissolves the monarchy and so the asymmetry the Cappadocians used to keep the Trinity from collapsing into either modalism or tritheism. Modern Eastern theologians (Zizioulas, Lossky) read the monarchy as the ontological ground of personhood itself — being is communion, derived eternally from the freely loving Father. Western theology has often suspected that pressed too hard, the monarchy reintroduces a subtle [[arianism|subordinationism]]; Eastern theology answers that the asymmetry is purely *taxic* (an order of origin), not *essential*.
