The dogma — confessed since [[constantinople-iii]] (681) — that the incarnate [[jesus]] has *two* wills (*thelēmata*) and two operations (*energeiai*), one divine and one human, corresponding to and inseparable from his two [[chalcedonian-christology|natures]] in one *hypostasis*. Maximus the Confessor's argument, against the imperial [[monothelitism|Monothelite]] compromise, is that will is a property of nature, not of person, so a complete human nature must include a human will — otherwise the [[chalcedonian-christology|Chalcedonian]] *enhypostatic* humanity is humanity in name only. The Gethsemane prayer — "*ouch hōs egō thelō, all' hōs sy* — not my will but yours" (Matt 26:39) — is read as the scriptural locus where the two wills are exegetically visible: a real human will, freely and perfectly conformed to the divine will it shares with the Father, not absorbed by it. The dogma is the Christological corollary of the [[hypostasis-vs-ousia|hypostasis/ousia]] distinction extended to the level of agency.
