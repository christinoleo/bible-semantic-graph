The rule of Christological predication — Greek *antidosis tōn idiōmatōn*, Latin *communicatio idiomatum*, "exchange / communication of properties" — that whatever can truly be said of either of [[chalcedonian-christology|Christ's two natures]] can be said of the one *hypostasis* of the incarnate Son, since the natures subsist not as two subjects but in one. So "God was crucified" (predicated *in concreto* of the divine person who suffered in his humanity) and "the carpenter created the world" (predicated of the human face of the Son who, as the eternal Logos, is creator) are both grammatically licensed, while "the divinity died" or "the humanity is eternal" (predicated *in abstracto* of the natures themselves) are not. The rule is what makes [[theotokos]] orthodox — Mary bears the *person* of the Son, who is God by nature, so she is rightly called Mother of God — and what makes [[nestorianism]]'s refusal of the title doctrinally significant rather than merely verbally squeamish. Latin scholasticism formalized the rule into three modes (*idiomatum*, *operationum*, *charismatum* — Lutheran orthodoxy later added *genus maiestaticum*, a move Reformed theology rejects), but the substance is patristic.
