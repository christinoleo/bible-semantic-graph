The doctrine — from Greek *kenōsis*, "emptying" — derived from the [[philippians|Philippians]] hymn (2:6-11) that the Son, "though he was in the form of God, did not consider equality with God a thing to be grasped, but *emptied himself* (*heauton ekenōsen*), taking the form of a servant." Classical patristic exegesis (especially [[cyril-of-alexandria|Cyril]]) reads the emptying as the *assumption of humanity* in the [[chalcedonian-christology|Chalcedonian]] sense — the Son does not lose or set aside divine attributes but takes the form of a slave in addition to and without compromise of his divinity, so that the same Son is *homoousios* with the Father in divinity and *homoousios* with us in humanity. 19th-century German kenotic theology (Thomasius, Gess, later Mackintosh and the British Forsyth) radicalized the term to claim that the incarnate Son actually divests himself of certain divine attributes for the duration of the incarnation — a move classical Christology resists as compromising both the [[trinity]] (the divine essence is simple, indivisible) and Chalcedon (the divinity remains *atreptōs* — unchanged). The Christian reading of Jesus's [[prostration-of-jesus|prostration]] in Gethsemane invokes kenosis in the classical, not radical, sense.
