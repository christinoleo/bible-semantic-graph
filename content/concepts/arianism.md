The fourth-century Christology — named for [[arius]] of Alexandria — that the Son is a creature, the highest and first-begotten of God's works but ontologically subordinate and brought into being out of nothing: *ēn pote hote ouk ēn*, "there was a time when he was not." The position is the canonical foil against which Nicene [[trinity|Trinitarian]] orthodoxy and the term [[homoousios]] ("of the same substance") are formulated at [[nicaea-i]] (325), and the controversy's long tail through the fourth century (the Eusebian network, the homoian compromise of Constantius II, the Cappadocian settlement) is the historical engine that produces both the doctrine of the Trinity and the technical vocabulary of [[hypostasis-vs-ousia|hypostasis and ousia]]. Re-condemned at [[constantinople-i]] (381), Arianism survives liturgically among Gothic and other Germanic kingdoms into the 7th century and reappears structurally (without historical descent) in modern unitarian Christologies — [[jehovahs-witness]] doctrine being the most explicit modern restatement, and [[islamic-christology]] sharing the underlying intuition that any full divinity of the Son threatens monotheism.
