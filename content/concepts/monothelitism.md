The seventh-century Christology — promoted imperially by Heraclius's *Ecthesis* (638) and Constans II's *Typos* (648) as a compromise to reconcile [[chalcedonian-christology|Chalcedonians]] with [[miaphysitism|Miaphysites]] — that the incarnate [[jesus]] has only one will (*thelēma*) and one operation (*energeia*), namely the divine. Maximus the Confessor's response was that a complete human nature must include a human will — to deny it is to repeat [[eutychianism|Eutyches]]'s mistake one level deeper, dissolving the humanity into the divinity not in its substance but in its agency. The Sixth Ecumenical Council ([[constantinople-iii]], 681) condemned monothelitism and dogmatized *dyothelitism* — two wills (one divine, one human) in the one person of Christ, the human will freely conformed to the divine throughout the incarnate life and especially in Gethsemane (Matt 26:39: "*not my will, but yours, be done*"). Posthumously the council condemned Pope Honorius I for his support of the formula, a fact much exploited in 19th-century Catholic debates over papal infallibility.
