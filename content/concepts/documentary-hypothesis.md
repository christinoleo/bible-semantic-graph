The classical theory, developed in 19th-century German scholarship and given canonical form by Julius Wellhausen, that the Pentateuch is composed of four originally independent source documents: **J** (Jahwist, ~10th c. BCE), **E** (Elohist, ~9th c. BCE), **D** (Deuteronomist, ~7th c. BCE), and **P** (Priestly, ~6th c. BCE), interwoven by later redactors.

Evidence cited includes: doublets (two creation accounts, two flood accounts), shifts in divine name ([[yhwh]] vs. Elohim), theological emphases differing by passage, and stylistic markers.

The hypothesis has been substantially modified, supplemented, and in places rejected in late-20th and 21st-century scholarship — by Rolf Rendtorff's "fragment" model, by neo-documentarians like Joel Baden, by literary-final-form readers like Robert Alter, and by minimalists who push composition dates much later. The "JEDP" label has become more of a heuristic than a confessional commitment.
