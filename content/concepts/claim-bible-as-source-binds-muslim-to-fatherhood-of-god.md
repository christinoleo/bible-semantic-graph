If the Muslim apologist appeals to the Gospels to prove that [[jesus]] prostrated (Matt 26:39), greeted with *shalom* (John 20:19), or kept the Sabbath (Luke 4:16), they are accepting the Gospels as a reliable record of Jesus's practice. But the *same verse* that records the prostration (Matt 26:39) records Jesus addressing God as *"My Father"* (Πάτερ μου) — and the parallel verses (Mark 14:36 *Abba, ho patēr*; Luke 22:42 *Πάτερ*) reinforce a sustained, intimate paternal address that runs through the entire Gospel corpus.

The Muslim cannot consistently say "Jesus prostrated *exactly as Muslims do*" and then refuse to say "Jesus addressed God *exactly as Muslims do not*." Either the Gospels describe Jesus's practice reliably (in which case he prayed to a heavenly Father, which Islamic *[[tawhid|Tawḥīd]]* categorically forbids) or they do not (in which case the prostration claim collapses with them).

The selective use of [[tahrif]] — "the Gospels are corrupt where they conflict with the Quran, reliable where they confirm it" — is the unprincipled move the dilemma exposes. The apologist needs an *independent* methodology for which Gospel passages count and which do not; absent that, [[tahrif]] is doing all the work in both directions and the practice argument has no force of its own.

This is structurally a refutation by internal contradiction: the Islamic argument trades on a textual authority it simultaneously denies.
