[[c-s-lewis]]'s argument in *Mere Christianity* (1952), repurposed across modern evangelical apologetics: [[jesus]] either was who he said he was (Lord), or he knew he was not and lied, or he sincerely believed false things about himself (lunatic). The "great moral teacher but not divine" option is foreclosed because someone making the claims Jesus made — to forgive sins, to be the judge of all, to be one with the Father — is either correct or insane or dishonest, not merely wise.

The standard counter, conceded in academic literature, is that the trilemma omits a fourth option ("legend" — see [[claim-fourth-l-legend-against-trilemma]]) and a fifth ("mistaken sincere reporter") — that the Gospels misrepresent what Jesus actually said. The trilemma's force therefore presupposes a prior commitment to the historical reliability of the Gospels' "I-am" statements — exactly what [[surge-new-testament-reliability-framework|SURGE]] and the [[claim-1-cor-15-creed-earliest-witness|early creed]] arguments are deployed to secure.
