The scholarly frame [[wesley-huff]]'s PhD dissertation operates within: treat surviving New Testament manuscripts not merely as text-vehicles whose value lies in reconstructing the original wording, but as *material objects* — papyrus or parchment, with paleographic features, codicological choices, marginal annotations, prior uses (palimpsests), and findspots — that encode social indicators about the communities that produced and used them. Method draws on codicology and paleography rather than only stemmatic textual criticism; conclusions concern early Christian community shape (scribal training, literacy levels, network connections, regional dispersion) as much as textual reconstruction. Distinct from the apologetic register of [[surge-new-testament-reliability-framework|SURGE]], which compresses similar evidence into a polemical mnemonic.
