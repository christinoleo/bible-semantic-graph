The corpus of reports (*aḥādīth*) of the sayings, actions, and tacit approvals of [[muhammad]], transmitted with an *isnād* (chain of narrators) and a *matn* (content), and collected through the 8th–10th centuries. Hadith are the second textual source of Islamic doctrine and law after the [[quran]] and the primary witness to the [[sunnah]] — the normative example of the Prophet.

The Sunni tradition recognizes six canonical collections (*Kutub al-Sittah*): [[sahih-al-bukhari]] and [[sahih-muslim]] (the two *Ṣaḥīḥān*, regarded as most rigorously authenticated), [[sunan-abu-dawud]], [[jami-al-tirmidhi]], [[sunan-al-nasai]], and [[sunan-ibn-majah]]. The Shia tradition follows a different corpus, centered on al-Kulayni's *Al-Kāfī* and the *Four Books* (*al-Kutub al-Arbaʿah*).

**Authority is not flat across the canonical six.** Mainstream Sunni reception places them in two distinct tiers, with sharp internal ranking:

1. **First tier — the *Ṣaḥīḥayn*** (regarded by most Sunni scholarship as authoritative after the [[quran]] itself):
   1. [[sahih-al-bukhari]] — ranked highest by mainstream consensus on the strength of its authentication criteria.
   2. [[sahih-muslim]] — second; some Maghrebi/Andalusi scholars rank it above Bukhārī on technical *isnād* grounds, but this is a minority position.
2. **Second tier — the four *Sunan*** (each contains reports graded below *ṣaḥīḥ*; consulted heavily for *fiqh* but not granted the *Ṣaḥīḥān*'s presumption of authenticity):
   1. [[sunan-abu-dawud]] — strongest of the four; explicitly flags weak reports.
   2. [[sunan-al-nasai]] — applied the strictest authentication of the four compilers; some rankings place it above Abū Dāwūd.
   3. [[jami-al-tirmidhi]] — distinguished by its juristic commentary and the systematic use of the *ḥasan* grade.
   4. [[sunan-ibn-majah]] — admitted to the canonical six gradually and not universally; contains the largest proportion of weak (*ḍaʿīf*) and a small number of fabricated (*mawḍūʿ*) reports per later critics.

Beyond the Six, Mālik's *Muwaṭṭaʾ* (8th c.; substituted for Ibn Mājah in some Maghrebi rankings) and Ibn Ḥanbal's *Musnad* (9th c.; more comprehensive but with looser authentication) are widely consulted. The discipline of hadith criticism (*ʿilm al-rijāl*, *muṣṭalaḥ al-ḥadīth*) grades each individual report's chain on the scale *ṣaḥīḥ* (sound) → *ḥasan* (good) → *ḍaʿīf* (weak) → *mawḍūʿ* (fabricated) — so authority gradations exist *within* every collection, not just across them. Western historical-critical scholarship since [[ignaz-goldziher]] and [[joseph-schacht]] has argued that many *isnāds* are retrojections from later legal debate, a thesis sharply contested by Islamic scholarship.
